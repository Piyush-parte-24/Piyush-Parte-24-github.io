🛂PART 1 — CITIZENSHIP (नागरिकता)

🔵 1.1 Definition & Meaning

Citizenship = The legal & political bond between an individual and a State, where the citizen owes allegiance to the State and in return enjoys protection, civil rights, and political rights.
👤
Who is a Citizen?
A full member of the political community possessing political rights — voting, contesting elections, holding public office.
🌍
Who is an Alien?
A foreigner residing in India without political rights. Classified as friendly alien or enemy alien (during war).
🇮🇳
Single Citizenship
India follows Single Citizenship (borrowed from UK) — unlike USA which has dual citizenship (federal + state).

🔵 1.2 Constitutional Provisions (Part II — Articles 5 to 11)

ArticleProvision
Art. 5Citizenship at commencement of Constitution (26 Jan 1950) — based on domicile in India
Art. 6Rights of persons who migrated from Pakistan to India
Art. 7Rights of persons who migrated to Pakistan (post-Partition resettlers)
Art. 8Rights of persons of Indian origin residing abroad
Art. 9No dual citizenship — voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship ends Indian citizenship
Art. 10Continuance of citizenship subject to law made by Parliament
Art. 11Parliament's power to regulate citizenship → enacted Citizenship Act, 1955

🔵 1.3 Acquisition of Citizenship (Citizenship Act 1955)

📌 Amendments: 1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, 2019 (CAA)

👶
1. By Birth
• 26 Jan 1950 – 1 July 1987: Born in India = citizen (regardless of parents).
• 1 Jul 1987 – 3 Dec 2004: At least one parent must be Indian.
• After 3 Dec 2004: Both parents Indian, OR one Indian + other not an illegal migrant.
👨‍👧
2. By Descent
Person born outside India where at least one parent is an Indian citizen. After 3 Dec 2004 — birth must be registered at Indian consulate within 1 year.
📝
3. By Registration
For PIOs residing in India for 7 years; persons married to Indian citizen (7 yrs); minor children of Indian citizens; OCI holders after 5 years.
🌐
4. By Naturalisation
Foreigner residing in India for 12 years (last 12 months continuous + 11 of preceding 14) + good character + renunciation of previous nationality + knowledge of a scheduled language.
🗺️
5. By Incorporation of Territory
When new territory becomes part of India, people of that territory become citizens. Examples: Goa (1961), Sikkim (1975), Pondicherry (1962).
🕊️
CAA 2019 (Special)
Citizenship Amendment Act grants citizenship to Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Christians from Afghanistan, Pakistan, Bangladesh who entered India before 31 Dec 2014. Residence requirement reduced to 5 years.

🔵 1.4 Loss of Citizenship

ModeMeaningIcon
RenunciationCitizen of full age voluntarily gives up Indian citizenship by declaration. Minor children also lose citizenship but can resume at majority.✋
TerminationAutomatic loss when citizen voluntarily acquires foreign citizenship (Art. 9 + Sec. 9 of Act).🚫
DeprivationCompulsory cancellation by Central Government on grounds: fraud, disloyalty to Constitution, trading with enemy during war, sentenced to ≥2 years imprisonment within 5 years of registration, continuous residence abroad for 7+ years.⚖️

🔵 1.5 Special Categories

🌎
OCI – Overseas Citizen of India
Introduced 2005. NOT full citizenship. ❌ No voting rights, ❌ No government job, ❌ No constitutional posts (President/VP/Judge/MP). ✅ Multi-purpose lifelong visa, ✅ Can buy property (non-agricultural).
✈️
NRI – Non-Resident Indian
An Indian citizen who resides outside India for work, business, or education for >182 days a year. Retains full citizenship rights including voting (in person at constituency).
🏃
Refugee
Not a citizen. India is NOT a signatory to UN Refugee Convention 1951. Refugees managed under Foreigners Act 1946 & Passport Act. Still entitled to Art. 14 & 21 protections.
🪪
PIO – Person of Indian Origin
PIO card scheme merged with OCI in 2015. Now a single OCI card document.
EXAM ALERT: Articles 15 & 16 (non-discrimination + equality in public employment), 19 (six freedoms), 29 & 30 (cultural & educational rights of minorities) are available only to citizens. Articles 14, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 28 are available to all persons (including foreigners).

⚖️PART 2 — FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS (मौलिक अधिकार)

🔴 2.1 Introduction (Part III, Articles 12–35)

Fundamental Rights are the basic human freedoms guaranteed by the Constitution to every citizen (and some to all persons), enforceable directly by the Supreme Court (Art. 32) and High Courts (Art. 226). They are the bedrock of political democracy.
📜
Source
US Bill of Rights (primary inspiration). Concept of writs from UK.
🔢
Originally 7 Rights
44th Amendment (1978) deleted Right to Property (Art. 31). Now it is a legal/constitutional right under Art. 300A.
🛡️
Art. 12 – Definition of "State"
Includes Govt of India, Parliament, State Govts, State Legislatures, all local authorities (municipalities, panchayats) and "other authorities" (PSUs, statutory bodies).
⚡
Art. 13 – Judicial Review
Any law inconsistent with FRs shall be void. Establishes Doctrine of Severability & Doctrine of Eclipse.

📍 Art. 20 — Protection in Respect of Conviction (All Persons)

  • No Ex-Post Facto Law — no retrospective criminal law; penalty cannot exceed that prescribed at time of offence.
  • No Double Jeopardy — no person punished twice for the same offence (US concept).
  • No Self-Incrimination — no person can be compelled to be a witness against himself.

"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." — Now interpreted as "due process of law" after Maneka Gandhi v. UoI (1978).

📍 Art. 21A — Right to Education (86th Amendment, 2002)

Art. 21A: Free and compulsory education for all children aged 6–14 years. Implemented via the Right to Education (RTE) Act 2009. Mandates 25% reservation in private unaided schools for EWS/disadvantaged children.

⛓️
Preventive Detention
Detention without trial up to 3 months without Advisory Board approval. Beyond 3 months — Advisory Board (HC judges) must approve. Laws: NSA, UAPA, PSA, COFEPOSA.

▸ Cultural & Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

🎭
Art. 29 – Protection of Minorities' Interests
Any section of citizens having distinct language, script, or culture has the right to conserve it. No discrimination in admission to state-aided institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste, language.
🏫
Art. 30 – Right of Minorities to Educational Institutions
Religious & linguistic minorities have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. State shall not discriminate in granting aid. T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002).

▸ Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) 💎

📖 Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "Heart and Soul of the Constitution" — without it, all other Fundamental Rights would be mere paper rights.

🔧 The Five Magnificent Writs

⛓️ Habeas Corpus
"You may have the body"
Court orders release of a person from illegal detention. Issued against State OR private individuals.
📢 Mandamus
"We command"
Commands a public authority to perform its public duty. NOT against President/Governor/private persons.
🛑 Prohibition
"To forbid"
Issued by higher court to lower court/tribunal to stop proceedings exceeding jurisdiction. Only against judicial bodies.
📜 Certiorari
"To be certified"
Quashes the order already passed by lower court/tribunal for lack of jurisdiction or error of law. Curative writ.
❓ Quo Warranto
"By what authority"
Inquires into the legality of a person's claim to a public office. Cannot be issued for private/ministerial offices.
Article 32 vs Article 226: Art. 32 (Supreme Court — only for FR enforcement) is itself a Fundamental Right. Art. 226 (High Courts — for FR + any other legal right) has wider scope. Art. 32 can be suspended during National Emergency (except Art. 20 & 21 — 44th Amendment).

🔴 2.4 Key Doctrines under FR

✂️
Doctrine of Severability
Only the part of a law violating FR is struck down — rest remains valid (Art. 13).
🌑
Doctrine of Eclipse
Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with FR become dormant (not dead). Revived if FR is later amended.
⚖️
Doctrine of Waiver
A person CANNOT waive his Fundamental Rights (Basheshar Nath v. CIT, 1959) — FRs are a matter of public policy.
🏛️
Basic Structure Doctrine
Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — Parliament cannot amend the "Basic Structure" of the Constitution, including FRs that form part of it.

📜PART 4 — DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES OF STATE POLICY (DPSP)

🟠 4.1 Introduction (Part IV, Articles 36–51)

DPSP are guidelines or instructions to the Central and State governments to be kept in mind while framing laws and policies, with the objective of establishing a welfare state based on social, economic & political justice.
🍀
Source
Irish Constitution (1937), which itself borrowed from Spanish Constitution.
❌
Justiciability
Non-justiciable (Art. 37) — but "fundamental in governance of the country" and State has a duty to apply them.
🎯
Aim
To realise economic and social democracy, complementing the political democracy provided by FRs.
📖
Granville Austin
Called FR + DPSP together as the "Conscience of the Constitution".

🌐PART 5 — HUMAN RIGHTS (मानवाधिकार)

🌐 5.1 Definition & Meaning

Human Rights = Rights inherent to all human beings, regardless of nationality, residence, sex, ethnicity, color, religion, language, or any other status. They are universal, inalienable, indivisible, interdependent, and inter-related.
UDHR (Universal Declaration of Human Rights) adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 (celebrated as Human Rights Day). Drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt. India was a signatory. The UDHR has 30 articles.

🌐 5.2 Three Generations of Human Rights

GenerationFocusExamplesSymbol
1st Gen — Negative / Blue RightsCivil & Political (Liberté)Right to life, speech, vote, fair trial, freedom from torture🗳️ 🕊️
2nd Gen — Positive / Red RightsEconomic, Social & Cultural (Égalité)Right to work, education, health, housing, social security💼 📚 🏥
3rd Gen — Solidarity / Green RightsCollective / Group Rights (Fraternité)Right to development, peace, healthy environment, self-determination🌍 ✌️ 🌳

🌐 5.3 Human Rights in the Indian Constitution

  • Fundamental Rights (Part III): Cover most 1st & 2nd generation rights.
  • DPSP (Part IV): Cover 2nd & 3rd generation rights.
  • Art. 21 has been interpreted to include numerous human rights — dignity, health, shelter, privacy, clean environment.
  • Preamble — secures Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity to all citizens (echoes UDHR values).

🕊️PART 6 — LIBERTY (स्वतंत्रता)

🕊️ 6.1 Definition & Meaning

Liberty = The state of being free from oppressive restrictions imposed by authority on one's way of life, behaviour, or political views. The Preamble guarantees "Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship" — drawn from the French Revolution (1789).
🚫
Negative Liberty
Freedom from external interference (classical liberal view of J.S. Mill, Isaiah Berlin). Focus: absence of restraint.
🌱
Positive Liberty
Freedom to develop one's full potential (Rousseau, T.H. Green). Focus: self-realization, presence of opportunities.

🕊️ 6.2 Types of Liberty (Detailed)

TypeDefinitionIndian ExampleIcon
Natural LibertyAbsolute freedom in absence of any law — leads to anarchy. Rejected in civilised society.State of nature (Hobbes' Leviathan)🌿
Civil LibertyFreedom enjoyed by citizens as members of a state — protected by law.Art. 19 — six freedoms; Art. 21 — life & liberty🏙️
Political LibertyRight to participate in the political process of the State.Right to vote (Art. 326), contest elections, form government, criticize government🗳️
Economic LibertyFreedom from poverty, unemployment & exploitation; freedom to choose profession.Art. 19(1)(g), Art. 41 (right to work), Art. 300A💼
National LibertyFreedom of a nation from foreign domination.India's Independence — 15 August 1947🇮🇳
Personal LibertyFreedom of body & mind — no arbitrary arrest or detention.Art. 21, 22 — protection against arbitrary arrest🧑‍⚖️
Religious LibertyFreedom of conscience, religious practice & propagation.Art. 25-28🛐
Moral LibertyFreedom to act according to one's conscience.Right to dissent, freedom of thought💭

🕊️ 6.3 Safeguards of Liberty in India

  • Written Constitution — guarantees FRs as enforceable rights.
  • Constitutional Remedies (Art. 32 & 226) — writs to protect liberty.
  • Independent Judiciary — guardian & interpreter of the Constitution.
  • Rule of Law — A.V. Dicey's three principles: supremacy of law, equality before law, predominance of legal spirit.
  • Free Press — implied under Art. 19(1)(a), the "fourth pillar" of democracy.
  • Separation of Powers — checks and balances among Legislature, Executive, Judiciary.
  • Decentralisation — Panchayati Raj brings power closer to people.
  • Vigilant Civil Society & Public Opinion.

🕊️ 6.4 Reasonable Restrictions on Liberty [Art. 19(2)–(6)]

Liberty is NOT absolute. Restrictions can be imposed by State on grounds of: sovereignty & integrity of India, security of State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to offence. Restrictions must be (i) reasonable, (ii) not arbitrary, (iii) have proximate connection with the objective — established in Chintaman Rao v. State of MP (1951).

🟣PART 7 — EQUALITY (समानता)

🟣 7.1 Definition & Meaning

Equality = Absence of special privileges to any section of society + provision of adequate opportunities for all individuals without discrimination. The Preamble guarantees "Equality of status and of opportunity".
📏
Formal Equality
Treating everyone identically under the same law — equality of treatment. "Like cases treated alike".
⚖️
Substantive Equality
Recognising historic disadvantages and providing affirmative action (reservations, special provisions) — equality of outcome.

🟣 7.4 Reasonable Classification — Allowed under Art. 14

TWO conditions for valid classification:
➀ Intelligible Differentia — must be based on a rational, discernible distinction.
➁ Rational Nexus — the basis of classification must have a rational connection with the objective sought to be achieved.

🟣 7.5 Exceptions to Equality (Art. 14)

  • Art. 361: President & Governors enjoy immunity from civil & criminal proceedings during tenure.
  • Art. 361A: No civil/criminal proceedings against newspapers for true reports of Parliamentary/State Legislature proceedings.
  • Art. 105 & 194: Parliamentary privileges — MPs/MLAs not liable for anything said in House.
  • Diplomatic Immunity: Foreign ambassadors & UN officials exempt from local jurisdiction.

🟣 7.6 Equality vs Liberty — The Eternal Twins

⚖️PART 8 — JUSTICE (न्याय)

⚖️ 8.1 Definition & Meaning

Justice = The quality of being fair, just, and morally right; the distribution of rewards & punishments in accordance with law, ethics, and equity. The Preamble secures "Justice — Social, Economic and Political" (inspired by the Russian Revolution 1917).
📚 John Rawls — A Theory of Justice (1971): "Justice as Fairness". Two principles:
➀ Equal Liberty Principle — each person has equal claim to basic liberties.
➁ Difference Principle — social & economic inequalities are justified only if they benefit the least advantaged.

✨PART 10 — Ultimate Exam Hack

🎯 20 Must-Remember Facts

  1. 42nd Amendment (1976) — "Mini Constitution": Added Socialist, Secular, Integrity to Preamble; Added Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A); Added DPSP 39A, 43A, 48A.
  2. 44th Amendment (1978): Removed Right to Property from FR (Art. 31) → became legal right under Art. 300A. Art. 20 & 21 cannot be suspended even during Emergency.
  3. 86th Amendment (2002): Added Art. 21A (Right to Education), modified Art. 45, added 11th Fundamental Duty.
  4. Heart & Soul of Constitution: Art. 32 (Right to Constitutional Remedies) — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.
  5. Soul of Constitution: Preamble (per Justice Bhagwati in Kesavananda Bharati).
  6. Voting Age: Lowered from 21 to 18 years by 61st Amendment (1989).
  7. Right to Privacy: Part of Art. 21 — K.S. Puttaswamy v. UoI (2017), 9-judge bench.
  8. Right to Education: Free & compulsory for 6–14 years (Art. 21A + RTE Act 2009).
  9. Bonded Labour: Prohibited under Art. 23; Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976.
  10. NHRC: Statutory body (not constitutional); established 1993 under Protection of Human Rights Act.
  11. Human Rights Day: 10 December (UDHR adopted 1948).
  12. Uniform Civil Code: Art. 44 (DPSP); Goa has it from Portuguese era; Uttarakhand became first state in independent India to enact UCC (2024).
  13. Writs: Supreme Court — Art. 32 (only for FR); High Courts — Art. 226 (for FR + any other legal right — wider scope).
  14. Doctrine of Severability: Only the unconstitutional part of a law is struck down (Art. 13).
  15. Doctrine of Eclipse: Pre-constitutional law violating FR becomes dormant; revived if FR amended.
  16. PIL (Public Interest Litigation): Relaxation of locus standi; pioneered in 1980s by Justices P.N. Bhagwati & V.R. Krishna Iyer.
  17. Right to Die: Active euthanasia ❌; Passive euthanasia ✅ with living will allowed (Common Cause v. UoI, 2018).
  18. Freedom of Press: Implied in Art. 19(1)(a) — no separate article.
  19. Double Jeopardy [Art. 20(2)]: Cannot be prosecuted & punished twice for the same offence.
  20. Ex-Post Facto Law: Prohibited only for criminal offences — civil & tax laws can be retrospective.
