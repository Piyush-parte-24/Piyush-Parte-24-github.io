🧭 Prologue · The constitutional architecture

Rights protect the person. Duties sustain the republic.

The Constitution does not treat liberty, equality and justice as isolated slogans. It combines enforceable Fundamental Rights, non-justiciable but governance-defining Directive Principles, citizen duties, and the broader human-rights framework.

🛂

Citizenship · Part II

Articles 5–11 deal with citizenship at commencement and Parliament’s power to regulate citizenship by law. The Citizenship Act, 1955 supplies the principal statutory framework.

🛡️

Fundamental Rights · Part III

Articles 12–35 protect constitutional freedoms and guarantees. Some are available to citizens; several are available to every person.

🇮🇳

Fundamental Duties · Part IVA

Article 51A lists eleven duties of citizens. They are not directly enforceable as a standalone constitutional code, but legislation may give effect to them.

📜

DPSP · Part IV

Articles 36–51 guide the State towards social and economic democracy. Article 37 makes them non-justiciable yet fundamental in governance.

Four Preamble ideals: Justice — social, economic and political; Liberty — thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; Equality — status and opportunity; Fraternity — assuring individual dignity and national unity and integrity.

A crucial distinction: who holds which protection?

🛂PART 1 — CITIZENSHIP (नागरिकता)

🔵 1.1 Definition & Meaning

Citizenship = The legal & political bond between an individual and a State, where the citizen owes allegiance to the State and in return enjoys protection, civil rights, and political rights.
👤
Who is a Citizen?
A full member of the political community possessing political rights — voting, contesting elections, holding public office.
🌍
Who is an Alien?
A foreigner residing in India without political rights. Classified as friendly alien or enemy alien (during war).
🇮🇳
Single Citizenship
India follows Single Citizenship (borrowed from UK) — unlike USA which has dual citizenship (federal + state).

🔵 1.2 Constitutional Provisions (Part II — Articles 5 to 11)

🔵 1.3 Acquisition of Citizenship (Citizenship Act 1955)

📌 Amendments: 1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, 2019 (CAA)

👶
1. By Birth
• 26 Jan 1950 – 1 July 1987: Born in India = citizen (regardless of parents).
• 1 Jul 1987 – 3 Dec 2004: At least one parent must be Indian.
• After 3 Dec 2004: Both parents Indian, OR one Indian + other not an illegal migrant.
👨‍👧
2. By Descent
Person born outside India where at least one parent is an Indian citizen. After 3 Dec 2004 — birth must be registered at Indian consulate within 1 year.
📝
3. By Registration
Registration is available only to statutory categories and subject to prescribed conditions. Examples include persons of Indian origin ordinarily resident in India for 7 years, spouses of Indian citizens ordinarily resident for 7 years, minor children of Indian citizens, certain persons whose parents are Indian citizens, and eligible OCI cardholders after the statutory OCI-holding and ordinary-residence conditions are met. It is not an automatic grant.
🌐
4. By Naturalisation
The general naturalisation residence test is 12 years in total: 12 months immediately before the application plus an aggregate 11 years during the preceding 14 years, along with the other statutory qualifications (including good character, Eighth Schedule language knowledge and intention to reside or serve in India). The Third Schedule contains a special 5-year aggregate residence substitution for the specified communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan covered by the 2019 amendment. India does not recognise dual citizenship.
🗺️
5. By Incorporation of Territory
When new territory becomes part of India, people of that territory become citizens. Examples: Goa (1961), Sikkim (1975), Pondicherry (1962).
🕊️
CAA 2019 (Special)
Section 6B of the Citizenship Act, inserted by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019, creates a special eligibility route for Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh or Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014 and meet the statutory conditions. It does not confer citizenship automatically: eligible applicants must apply for registration or naturalisation and satisfy the prescribed process. The Third Schedule substitutes an aggregate 5-year residence period for the specified 11-year period, while retaining the separate 12-month immediately preceding residence condition. Statutory exclusions apply, including specified Sixth Schedule tribal areas and Inner Line Permit areas.

🔵 1.5 Special Categories

🌎
OCI – Overseas Citizen of India (overseas citizenship status, not Indian citizenship)
Introduced 2005. Not Indian citizenship and not dual citizenship. OCI cardholders cannot vote or hold constitutional offices reserved for citizens; they are generally ineligible for appointment to public services/posts, subject to statutory notifications and specified exceptions. OCI status carries visa and notified parity benefits, not the full political rights of citizens. ✅ Multi-purpose lifelong visa, ✅ Can buy property (non-agricultural).
✈️
NRI – Non-Resident Indian
An Indian citizen who ordinarily resides outside India for employment, business, education or other purposes. “NRI” is a common usage, not a separate constitutional citizenship class; the 182-day test is commonly associated with tax-residence rules and should not be treated as a universal citizenship definition. An overseas elector may vote in the constituency of ordinary residence in India, but must be physically present at the polling station and produce the original passport.
🏃
Refugee
Not a citizen. India is NOT a signatory to UN Refugee Convention 1951. Immigration and foreigners are now governed principally by the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 (in force from 1 September 2025), which repealed the Foreigners Act, 1946 and specified earlier enactments. India is not party to the 1951 Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol. Still entitled to Art. 14 & 21 protections.
🪪
PIO – Person of Indian Origin
PIO card scheme merged with OCI in 2015. Now a single OCI card document.
EXAM ALERT: Articles 15 & 16 (non-discrimination + equality in public employment), 19 (six freedoms), 29 & 30 (cultural & educational rights of minorities) are available only to citizens. Articles 14, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 28 are available to all persons (including foreigners).
PRECISION NOTE · CITIZENSHIP IS NOT THE SAME AS RESIDENCE

“NRI” is a descriptive expression for an Indian citizen living abroad; it is not a separate constitutional citizenship category. A person’s income-tax residential status is determined under tax legislation and should not be used as a substitute for citizenship status. OCI is a statutory overseas status, not dual citizenship.

🧭

Article 14 — “person”

Equality before law and equal protection of laws apply to every person within India’s territory, subject to constitutionally recognised immunities and lawful classifications.

🗳️

Article 19 — “citizen”

The six freedoms in Article 19(1) are guaranteed to citizens, not to every foreign national. Restrictions must fit the grounds and tests in the relevant clauses.

⚖️

Article 32 and Article 226

Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and the Supreme Court’s writ jurisdiction under it is for enforcement of Part III rights. High Courts under Article 226 may issue writs for Part III rights and “for any other purpose,” so the subject-matter reach is wider.

📜

Article 37 — DPSP

DPSPs are non-justiciable in court as such, but are declared fundamental in governance; the State has a constitutional duty to apply them in making laws.

⚖️PART 2 — FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS (मौलिक अधिकार)

🔴 2.1 Introduction (Part III, Articles 12–35)

Fundamental Rights are the basic human freedoms guaranteed by the Constitution to every citizen (and some to all persons), enforceable directly by the Supreme Court (Art. 32) and High Courts (Art. 226). They are the bedrock of political democracy.
📜
Source
US Bill of Rights (primary inspiration). Concept of writs from UK.
🔢
Originally 7 Rights
44th Amendment (1978) deleted Right to Property (Art. 31). Now it is a legal/constitutional right under Art. 300A.
🛡️
Art. 12 – Definition of "State"
Includes Govt of India, Parliament, State Govts, State Legislatures, all local authorities (municipalities, panchayats) and "other authorities" (PSUs, statutory bodies).
⚡
Art. 13 – Judicial Review
Any law inconsistent with FRs shall be void. Establishes Doctrine of Severability & Doctrine of Eclipse.

📍 Art. 20 — Protection in Respect of Conviction (All Persons)

  • No Ex-Post Facto Law — no retrospective criminal law; penalty cannot exceed that prescribed at time of offence.
  • No Double Jeopardy — no person punished twice for the same offence (US concept).
  • No Self-Incrimination — an accused person cannot be compelled to give testimonial evidence against himself; the protection does not automatically cover every form of physical or non-testimonial evidence.

📍 Art. 21 — Right to Life & Personal Liberty 🌟

"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." — In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court held that the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable and not arbitrary. “Due process” is an interpretive description; the text of Article 21 still says “procedure established by law.”

📍 Art. 21A — Right to Education (86th Amendment, 2002)

Art. 21A: Free and compulsory education for all children aged 6–14 years. Implemented via the Right to Education (RTE) Act 2009. Section 12(1)(c) of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 requires specified categories of schools to admit at least 25% children from weaker sections and disadvantaged groups at the entry level, subject to the Act’s scope and applicable rules; it is not a blanket rule for every private school.

▸ Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) 💎

📖 Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "Heart and Soul of the Constitution" — without it, all other Fundamental Rights would be mere paper rights.

🔧 The Five Magnificent Writs

⛓️ Habeas Corpus
"You may have the body"
Court orders release of a person from illegal detention. Issued against State OR private individuals.
📢 Mandamus
"We command"
Commands a public authority to perform its public duty. NOT against President/Governor/private persons.
🛑 Prohibition
"To forbid"
Issued by higher court to lower court/tribunal to stop proceedings exceeding jurisdiction. Only against judicial bodies.
📜 Certiorari
"To be certified"
Quashes the order already passed by lower court/tribunal for lack of jurisdiction or error of law. Curative writ.
❓ Quo Warranto
"By what authority"
Inquires into the legality of a person's claim to a public office. Cannot be issued for private/ministerial offices.
Article 32 vs Article 226: Art. 32 (Supreme Court — only for FR enforcement) is itself a Fundamental Right. Art. 226 (High Courts — for FR + any other legal right) has wider scope. During a Proclamation of Emergency, Article 359 may suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights, but Articles 20 and 21 are excluded from such an order after the 44th Amendment. Article 19 is affected under Article 358 only in the constitutionally specified emergency circumstances.

🔴 2.4 Key Doctrines under FR

✂️
Doctrine of Severability
Only the part of a law violating FR is struck down — rest remains valid (Art. 13).
🌑
Doctrine of Eclipse
Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with FR become dormant (not dead). Revived if FR is later amended.
⚖️
Doctrine of Waiver
A person CANNOT waive his Fundamental Rights (Basheshar Nath v. CIT, 1959) — FRs are a matter of public policy.
🏛️
Basic Structure Doctrine
Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — Parliament cannot amend the "Basic Structure" of the Constitution, including FRs that form part of it.

📜PART 4 — DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES OF STATE POLICY (DPSP)

🟠 4.1 Introduction (Part IV, Articles 36–51)

DPSP are guidelines or instructions to the Central and State governments to be kept in mind while framing laws and policies, with the objective of establishing a welfare state based on social, economic & political justice.
🍀
Source
Irish Constitution (1937), which itself borrowed from Spanish Constitution.
❌
Justiciability
Non-justiciable (Art. 37) — but "fundamental in governance of the country" and State has a duty to apply them.
🎯
Aim
To realise economic and social democracy, complementing the political democracy provided by FRs.
📖
Granville Austin
Called FR + DPSP together as the "Conscience of the Constitution".

🟠 4.2 Article-by-Article Constitutional Guide — Articles 36–51

How to read this section: The short “constitutional meaning” summaries follow the current consolidated text of Part IV. “Insight” and “exam lens” are explanatory study notes, not additional constitutional wording. Articles 39, 38 and 51 are broken down clause by clause so no sub-clause is hidden.
Scope note: Article 36 defines “State” for Part IV by borrowing the meaning in Part III, Article 12 (unless context otherwise requires). The familiar three-stream classification—socialistic, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual—is a teaching aid, not a classification written into the Constitution. Several Articles overlap more than one stream.
36ARTICLEDefinition — Who is “the State”?Part IV’s interpretive starting point

Constitutional meaning: Unless the context otherwise requires, “the State” in Part IV has the same meaning as in Part III. Article 12’s definition includes the Government and Parliament of India, State governments and legislatures, and local or other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

INSIGHT

DPSPs are addressed to public authorities within the constitutional meaning of “State”; they are not merely suggestions to the Union Cabinet. The contextual qualification in Article 36 must be retained.

Exam lens: Article 36 is definitional; it does not itself prescribe a welfare policy. Link it with Article 12, but do not assume the two provisions are textually identical in every context.

37ARTICLEApplication — Non-justiciable, yet fundamentalThe constitutional status of every DPSP

Constitutional meaning: No court may enforce Part IV provisions as such. Nevertheless, the principles are fundamental in the governance of the country, and the State has a duty to apply them when making laws.

What non-justiciable means

A person cannot ordinarily obtain a court order merely because the government has not implemented a particular DPSP.

What it does not mean

DPSPs are not optional political decoration. They guide legislation, public policy and constitutional interpretation, subject to the rest of the Constitution.

Exam lens: Article 37 contains both limbs—non-enforceability and fundamental importance/state duty. Mention both for a complete answer.

38ARTICLESocial order for people’s welfareClauses (1) and (2)

Clause (1): The State must strive to promote people’s welfare by securing and protecting a social order in which social, economic and political justice inform all institutions of national life.

Clause (2): The State must particularly strive to minimise inequalities in income and endeavour to eliminate inequalities in status, facilities and opportunities—not only between individuals but also between groups living in different areas or engaged in different vocations.

INSIGHT

Clause (1) states the broad welfare-state objective; clause (2) makes distributive equality more concrete and expressly includes inter-group, regional and occupational disparities.

Amendment: Article 38 was renumbered as clause (1), and clause (2) was added by the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 (effective 20 June 1979).

Exam lens: Do not reduce Article 38(2) to income inequality alone; it also names status, facilities and opportunities.

39ARTICLECertain principles of policySix clauses: (a) to (f)

Opening direction: The State must direct its policy towards securing the following principles.

39(a) · Livelihood

Citizens, men and women equally, should have an adequate means of livelihood.

Insight: Focuses on access to livelihood, not a guarantee of a particular job or salary to every individual.

39(b) · Common good

Ownership and control of the community’s material resources should be distributed to best serve the common good.

Insight: Concerns the social purpose of resource distribution; it does not prescribe one single economic model.

39(c) · Prevent concentration

The economic system should not operate so as to concentrate wealth and the means of production to the common detriment.

Insight: Distinct from clause (b): clause (b) addresses distribution for common good; clause (c) guards against harmful concentration.

39(d) · Equal pay

There should be equal pay for equal work for both men and women.

Insight: The constitutional text expressly mentions men and women. Courts have treated the principle as relevant to equality jurisprudence, but comparison depends on the nature of work and applicable law.

39(e) · Worker and child protection

The health and strength of workers, men and women, and the tender age of children must not be abused; economic necessity must not force citizens into work unsuited to their age or strength.

Insight: It addresses exploitative labour conditions and economic coercion, not only workplace safety.

39(f) · Childhood and youth

Children must receive opportunities and facilities to develop healthily, in freedom and dignity; childhood and youth must be protected against exploitation and moral and material abandonment.

Insight: The present wording was substituted by the 42nd Amendment (effective 3 January 1977), strengthening the child-development and dignity focus.

Exam lens: Memorise the sequence: livelihood → common-good resources → prevent wealth concentration → equal pay → worker/child protection → dignified child development.

39AARTICLEEqual justice and free legal aidAccess to justice

Constitutional meaning: The legal system must promote justice on the basis of equal opportunity. The State must provide free legal aid through legislation, schemes or other means so that economic or other disabilities do not deny a citizen the opportunity to secure justice.

INSIGHT

Formal equality before courts is insufficient if a person cannot afford advice, representation or access to the legal process. Article 39A addresses this practical barrier.

Institutional link: The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 provides the statutory framework for legal-services institutions and eligibility-based legal aid. Article 39A was inserted by the 42nd Amendment (1976).

Related rights: Read with Articles 14 and 21; in Hussainara Khatoon and later decisions, fair procedure and legal assistance have been treated as important dimensions of access to justice.

40ARTICLEOrganisation of village panchayatsGrass-roots self-government

Constitutional meaning: The State must take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with the powers and authority necessary to function as units of self-government.

INSIGHT

Article 40 supplies a constitutional policy foundation for democratic decentralisation. The detailed constitutional framework for Panchayats is now in Part IX, inserted by the 73rd Amendment Act, 1992 (effective 24 April 1993).

Important distinction: Municipalities are addressed separately in Part IXA, inserted by the 74th Amendment Act, 1992 (effective 1 June 1993); they are not the subject of Article 40 itself.

Exam lens: Article 40 = village Panchayats; Part IX = constitutional Panchayat framework; Part IXA = urban municipalities.

41ARTICLEWork, education and public assistanceSubject to capacity and development

Constitutional meaning: Within the limits of its economic capacity and development, the State must make effective provision for securing the right to work, education and public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness, disablement and other cases of undeserved want.

INSIGHT

The express qualification—“within the limits of its economic capacity and development”—is central. Article 41 is a Directive Principle, not an independently enforceable Fundamental Right to a job or universal cash benefit.

Policy connections: Employment programmes, social-security schemes, disability support, pensions and public education can advance this principle, but their legal entitlements depend on the relevant statutes and scheme rules.

Exam lens: Do not confuse Article 41’s broad policy direction with Article 21A’s enforceable right to free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen.

42ARTICLEHumane work conditions and maternity reliefLabour welfare and dignity

Constitutional meaning: The State must make provision for securing just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.

INSIGHT

The Article links labour conditions with human dignity and recognises maternity relief as a constitutional policy concern. Its scope is broader than maternity leave alone.

Statutory connection: The Maternity Benefit Act, 1961, as amended in 2017, provides maternity benefits including up to 26 weeks for eligible women in covered establishments, subject to statutory conditions and exceptions. Do not describe 26 weeks as an unconditional entitlement for every worker in every workplace.

Exam lens: Article 42 = humane working conditions + maternity relief; Article 43 = living wage and quality of life for workers.

43ARTICLELiving wage and decent life for workersIncludes cottage industries

Constitutional meaning: The State must endeavour, through suitable legislation, economic organisation or other means, to secure for all workers—agricultural, industrial or otherwise—work, a living wage, conditions ensuring a decent standard of life, leisure, and social and cultural opportunities. It must particularly endeavour to promote cottage industries on an individual or co-operative basis in rural areas.

INSIGHT

A living wage is conceptually broader than a bare subsistence/minimum wage: it is connected to a decent standard of life, leisure and social-cultural participation. The Constitution states an objective; actual wage entitlements arise through applicable law and policy.

Classification: Often grouped as both socialistic (worker welfare) and Gandhian (rural cottage industries). This overlap shows why classifications are illustrative.

43AARTICLEWorkers’ participation in managementIndustrial democracy

Constitutional meaning: The State must take steps, through suitable legislation or otherwise, to secure workers’ participation in the management of undertakings, establishments or other organisations engaged in any industry.

INSIGHT

Article 43A goes beyond wages and working conditions: it concerns workers having a role in management and decision-making within industry.

Amendment: Inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 (effective 3 January 1977).

Exam lens: Article 43A = participation in management; Article 43B = cooperative societies. They are distinct provisions.

43BARTICLEPromotion of co-operative societiesVoluntary, autonomous and democratic cooperatives

Constitutional meaning: The State must endeavour to promote the voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies.

INSIGHT

Four design values matter: voluntary formation, autonomy, democratic member control and professional management. The Article does not simply direct the State to increase the number of cooperatives.

Amendment: Inserted by the 97th Amendment Act, 2011; the constitutional text records its commencement on 15 February 2012.

Exam lens: Article 43B is the express DPSP on cooperatives. Its subject is also reflected in Part IXB, whose application to State cooperative societies was limited by the Supreme Court’s 2021 decision in Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah; avoid assuming every part of the 97th Amendment applies identically to every cooperative.

44ARTICLEUniform Civil CodeCommon civil code objective

Constitutional meaning: The State must endeavour to secure for citizens a Uniform Civil Code throughout the territory of India.

INSIGHT

The provision concerns a common civil-law framework for citizens in matters traditionally governed by personal laws. It is a Directive Principle, not itself a presently self-executing code and not a direction that any particular draft or model must be adopted.

Constitutional balance: Any legislative design must be assessed against Fundamental Rights, equality, religious freedom, cultural protections, federal arrangements and the relevant legal context.

Exam lens: Article 44 is in Part IV and therefore non-justiciable as such. Distinguish the constitutional objective from the content, scope and commencement of any State or Union legislation.

45ARTICLEEarly childhood care and educationChildren below six years

Constitutional meaning: The State must endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years.

INSIGHT

Article 45 now focuses on the pre-school/early-childhood stage. The original Article 45 directed free and compulsory education for all children until age fourteen within ten years of commencement; the 86th Amendment reworked it.

Related constitutional provisions: Article 21A requires free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen; Article 51A(k) places a duty on a parent or guardian to provide educational opportunities to a child/ward aged six to fourteen.

Amendment: Substituted by the 86th Amendment Act, 2002; brought into force on 1 April 2010.

46ARTICLEEducational and economic interests of weaker sectionsSpecial care for SCs and STs

Constitutional meaning: The State must promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation.

INSIGHT

The text expressly singles out SCs and STs while retaining the wider category of “weaker sections”. Do not rewrite the constitutional text as if it expressly names OBCs in this Article.

Policy connections: Educational support, scholarships, hostels, targeted welfare, safeguards against exploitation and protective legislation may serve this objective. Specific eligibility and remedies come from the relevant laws and schemes.

Exam lens: Article 46 is a DPSP; Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 16(4) are separate enabling provisions in the Fundamental Rights chapter.

47ARTICLENutrition, standard of living and public healthPublic health as a primary duty

Constitutional meaning: The State must regard raising nutrition levels and standards of living, and improving public health, as among its primary duties. It must particularly endeavour to bring about prohibition of consumption—except for medicinal purposes—of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health.

INSIGHT

Article 47 contains two connected but distinct directions: (1) nutrition, living standards and public health; (2) public-health-oriented prohibition of harmful intoxicants, with a medicinal-purpose exception.

Federal dimension: Public health and intoxicating liquors engage State-level legislative and administrative responsibilities under the Seventh Schedule, subject to the precise distribution of powers and applicable law. The DPSP itself does not create a single uniform national prohibition statute.

Classification: Commonly grouped with Gandhian principles because of the prohibition objective, and also with social-welfare principles because of nutrition and health.

48ARTICLEAgriculture and animal husbandryScientific organisation and cattle preservation

Constitutional meaning: The State must endeavour to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines and, in particular, take steps to preserve and improve breeds and prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle.

INSIGHT

The text combines modern/scientific agricultural organisation and animal-husbandry development with breed improvement and a specific cattle-protection direction. Its implementation involves legislation and policy within the constitutional framework.

Exam lens: The constitutional wording expressly names cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle. Do not paraphrase it as a blanket constitutional ban on slaughter of every animal species.

48AARTICLEEnvironment, forests and wildlifeEnvironmental protection

Constitutional meaning: The State must endeavour to protect and improve the environment and safeguard the forests and wildlife of the country.

INSIGHT

Article 48A places environmental protection expressly among the State’s constitutional policy duties. Read it with Article 51A(g), the citizen’s fundamental duty to protect and improve the natural environment, and Article 21 jurisprudence on life and environmental quality.

Amendment: Inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 (effective 3 January 1977).

Exam lens: Article 48A = State duty; Article 51A(g) = citizen duty; Article 21 = judicially developed rights framework. The provisions are related but not interchangeable.

49ARTICLEMonuments and objects of national importanceProtection of cultural heritage

Constitutional meaning: The State is obliged to protect monuments, places and objects of artistic or historic interest that are declared by or under a law made by Parliament to be of national importance, against spoliation, disfigurement, destruction, removal, disposal or export.

INSIGHT

The parliamentary-law declaration of national importance is a key qualification in the text. Article 49 is not an unrestricted description of every old building or object.

Statutory connection: The Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958 is a major central law in this field.

50ARTICLESeparation of judiciary from executiveJudicial independence in public services

Constitutional meaning: The State must take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

INSIGHT

The immediate institutional concern is the separation of judicial functions from executive control, particularly in the subordinate judiciary. It supports impartial adjudication and judicial independence.

Related provisions: Read with Articles 233–237 on subordinate courts and the constitutional structure of the judiciary. Do not reduce Article 50 to a claim that one procedural statute alone “implements” it.

Exam lens: Article 50 is a DPSP on institutional separation; judicial independence is also recognised as part of the Constitution’s basic structure.

51ARTICLEInternational peace and securityFour clauses: (a) to (d)

Opening direction: The State must endeavour to promote the following objectives in international relations.

51(a) · Peace and security

Promote international peace and security.

Insight: Establishes a constitutional orientation towards peaceful international order.

51(b) · Honourable relations

Maintain just and honourable relations between nations.

Insight: Emphasises principled, responsible relations among States.

51(c) · International law

Foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in dealings of organised peoples with one another.

Insight: Expressly connects domestic constitutional policy with respect for international legal commitments.

51(d) · Arbitration

Encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration.

Insight: Promotes peaceful dispute resolution; it does not mean every international dispute must legally be arbitrated.

Exam lens: Remember the four-part sequence: peace → honourable relations → international law/treaties → arbitration.

Amendment map for Part IV: 42nd Amendment (1976) inserted Articles 39A, 43A and 48A and substituted Article 39(f); 44th Amendment (1978) added Article 38(2); 86th Amendment (2002) substituted Article 45; 97th Amendment (2011) inserted Article 43B. Article 38(2) and Article 43B’s commencement dates are stated in the official consolidated text’s footnotes.

🌐PART 5 — HUMAN RIGHTS (मानवाधिकार)

🌐 5.1 Definition & Meaning

Human Rights = Rights inherent to all human beings, regardless of nationality, residence, sex, ethnicity, color, religion, language, or any other status. They are universal, inalienable, indivisible, interdependent, and inter-related.
UDHR (Universal Declaration of Human Rights) adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 (celebrated as Human Rights Day). Drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt. India voted in favour of adoption; the UDHR is a declaration, not a treaty open for signature. The UDHR has 30 articles.

🌐 5.2 Three Generations of Human Rights

CLASSIFICATION NOTE: The “three generations” framework is commonly associated with Karel Vasak. It is a teaching and historical classification, not a binding legal hierarchy. The generations overlap; civil-political, economic-social-cultural and solidarity rights are interdependent.
GenerationFocusExamplesSymbol
1st Gen — Negative / Blue RightsCivil & Political (Liberté)Right to life, speech, vote, fair trial, freedom from torture🗳️ 🕊️
2nd Gen — Positive / Red RightsEconomic, Social & Cultural (Égalité)Right to work, education, health, housing, social security💼 📚 🏥
3rd Gen — Solidarity / Green RightsCollective / Group Rights (Fraternité)Right to development, peace, healthy environment, self-determination🌍 ✌️ 🌳

🌐 5.3 Human Rights in the Indian Constitution

  • Fundamental Rights (Part III): Cover most 1st & 2nd generation rights.
  • DPSP (Part IV): Cover 2nd & 3rd generation rights.
  • Art. 21 has been interpreted to include numerous human rights — dignity, health, shelter, privacy, clean environment.
  • Preamble — secures Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity to all citizens (echoes UDHR values).

LAW UPDATE · IMMIGRATION & FOREIGNERS ACT, 2025

With effect from 1 September 2025, the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 replaced the Foreigners Act, 1946, the Registration of Foreigners Act, 1939, the Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920 and the Immigration (Carriers’ Liability) Act, 2000. Older study notes that still cite the Foreigners Act, 1946 as the current general foreigners statute need updating. This does not make India a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention.

CASE-LAW UPDATE · HARISH RANA v. UNION OF INDIA (11 MARCH 2026)

The Supreme Court’s 2026 decision (2026 INSC 222) clarified how withdrawal of clinically assisted nutrition and hydration may be considered within the passive-euthanasia framework. The decision stresses the patient’s best interests, medical assessment, dignity and procedural safeguards. Do not reduce the law to the shorthand “passive euthanasia is allowed”: the legal route is tightly regulated, and active euthanasia is not thereby authorised.

🕊️PART 6 — LIBERTY (स्वतंत्रता)

🕊️ 6.1 Definition & Meaning

Liberty = The state of being free from oppressive restrictions imposed by authority on one's way of life, behaviour, or political views. The Preamble guarantees "Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship" — drawn from the French Revolution (1789).
🚫
Negative Liberty
Freedom from external interference (classical liberal view of J.S. Mill, Isaiah Berlin). Focus: absence of restraint.
🌱
Positive Liberty
Freedom to develop one's full potential (Rousseau, T.H. Green). Focus: self-realization, presence of opportunities.

🕊️ 6.2 Types of Liberty (Detailed)

TypeDefinitionIndian ExampleIcon

🕊️ 6.3 Safeguards of Liberty in India

  • Written Constitution — guarantees FRs as enforceable rights.
  • Constitutional Remedies (Art. 32 & 226) — writs to protect liberty.
  • Independent Judiciary — guardian & interpreter of the Constitution.
  • Rule of Law — A.V. Dicey's three principles: supremacy of law, equality before law, predominance of legal spirit.
  • Free Press — implied under Art. 19(1)(a), the "fourth pillar" of democracy.
  • Separation of Powers — checks and balances among Legislature, Executive, Judiciary.
  • Decentralisation — Panchayati Raj brings power closer to people.
  • Vigilant Civil Society & Public Opinion.

🕊️ 6.4 Reasonable Restrictions on Liberty [Art. 19(2)–(6)]

Liberty is NOT absolute. Restrictions can be imposed by State on grounds of: sovereignty & integrity of India, security of State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to offence. Restrictions must be (i) reasonable, (ii) not arbitrary, (iii) have proximate connection with the objective — established in Chintaman Rao v. State of MP (1951).

🟣PART 7 — EQUALITY (समानता)

🟣 7.1 Definition & Meaning

Equality = Absence of special privileges to any section of society + provision of adequate opportunities for all individuals without discrimination. The Preamble guarantees "Equality of status and of opportunity".
📏
Formal Equality
Treating everyone identically under the same law — equality of treatment. "Like cases treated alike".
⚖️
Substantive Equality
Recognising historic disadvantages and providing affirmative action (reservations, special provisions) — equality of outcome.

🟣 7.4 Reasonable Classification — Allowed under Art. 14

DO NOT STOP AT THE TWO-TEST FORMULA: Article 14 also guards against manifest arbitrariness. A classification may satisfy intelligible differentia and rational nexus, yet a measure can still be invalid if it is manifestly arbitrary or violates another constitutional guarantee.
TWO conditions for valid classification:
➀ Intelligible Differentia — must be based on a rational, discernible distinction.
➁ Rational Nexus — the basis of classification must have a rational connection with the objective sought to be achieved.

🟣 7.5 Constitutional Immunities & Special Protections

  • Art. 361: President & Governors enjoy immunity from civil & criminal proceedings during tenure.
  • Art. 361A: No civil/criminal proceedings against newspapers for true reports of Parliamentary/State Legislature proceedings.
  • Art. 105 & 194: Parliamentary privileges — MPs/MLAs not liable for anything said in House.
  • Diplomatic Immunity: Foreign ambassadors & UN officials exempt from local jurisdiction.

⚖️PART 8 — JUSTICE (न्याय)

⚖️ 8.1 Definition & Meaning

Justice = The quality of being fair, just, and morally right; the distribution of rewards & punishments in accordance with law, ethics, and equity. The Preamble secures "Justice — Social, Economic and Political" (inspired by the Russian Revolution 1917).
📚 John Rawls — A Theory of Justice (1971): "Justice as Fairness". Two principles:
➀ Equal Liberty Principle — each person has equal claim to basic liberties.
➁ Difference Principle — social & economic inequalities are justified only if they benefit the least advantaged.

EXAM PRECISION · POLITICAL JUSTICE

Article 326 establishes elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage, subject to constitutional and statutory qualifications. The right to vote is not generally classified as a Fundamental Right; the Supreme Court has treated it as a statutory right operating within a constitutional framework. The right to contest elections is likewise regulated by eligibility and disqualification laws.

✨PART 10 — Ultimate Exam Hack

🎯 20 Must-Remember Facts

  1. 42nd Amendment (1976) — "Mini Constitution": Added Socialist, Secular, Integrity to Preamble; Added Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A); Added DPSP 39A, 43A, 48A.
  2. 44th Amendment (1978): Removed Right to Property from FR (Art. 31) → became legal right under Art. 300A. Art. 20 & 21 cannot be suspended even during Emergency.
  3. 86th Amendment (2002): Added Art. 21A (Right to Education), modified Art. 45, added 11th Fundamental Duty.
  4. Heart & Soul of Constitution: Art. 32 (Right to Constitutional Remedies) — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.
  5. Soul of Constitution: Preamble (per Justice Bhagwati in Kesavananda Bharati).
  6. Voting Age: Lowered from 21 to 18 years by the 61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988 (brought into force in 1989).
  7. Right to Privacy: Part of Art. 21 — K.S. Puttaswamy v. UoI (2017), 9-judge bench.
  8. Right to Education: Free & compulsory for 6–14 years (Art. 21A + RTE Act 2009).
  9. Bonded Labour: Prohibited under Art. 23; Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976.
  10. NHRC: Statutory body (not constitutional); established 1993 under Protection of Human Rights Act.
  11. Human Rights Day: 10 December (UDHR adopted 1948).
  12. Uniform Civil Code: Art. 44 (DPSP); Goa has it from Portuguese era; Uttarakhand enacted a UCC statute in 2024, brought into force in 2025; its scope includes specified exemptions, including protections for Scheduled Tribes. Avoid describing it as literally uniform without exceptions.
  13. Writs: Supreme Court — Art. 32 (only for FR); High Courts — Art. 226 (for FR + any other legal right — wider scope).
  14. Doctrine of Severability: Only the unconstitutional part of a law is struck down (Art. 13).
  15. Doctrine of Eclipse: Pre-constitutional law violating FR becomes dormant; revived if FR amended.
  16. PIL (Public Interest Litigation): Relaxation of locus standi; pioneered in 1980s by Justices P.N. Bhagwati & V.R. Krishna Iyer.
  17. Right to die with dignity: Common Cause v. Union of India (2018) recognised advance medical directives and passive euthanasia subject to safeguards; it did not legalise active euthanasia. In Harish Rana v. Union of India (11 March 2026, 2026 INSC 222), the Supreme Court clarified the framework for withdrawal of clinically assisted nutrition and hydration and emphasised best-interest assessment and procedural safeguards.
  18. Freedom of Press: Implied in Art. 19(1)(a) — no separate article.
  19. Double Jeopardy [Art. 20(2)]: Cannot be prosecuted & punished twice for the same offence.
  20. Ex-Post Facto Law: Prohibited only for criminal offences — civil & tax laws can be retrospective.
