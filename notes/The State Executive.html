🎬 Opening Scene · The Big Picture

🌳 Master Mind Map — One Executive, Four Pillars

India follows the parliamentary model in the States. Executive power is formally vested in the Governor (the constitutional head), but it is ordinarily exercised through a Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister (the real executive), which is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly. Everything in Articles 153–167 is a variation on this single idea. 🧩

💡 The Constitutional Triad: Art. 154 vests executive power in the Governor → Art. 163 says he acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (except where the Constitution requires discretion) → Art. 164(2) makes that Council collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly. Remember the three together and most descriptive answers write themselves.

👑 Constitutional head

Formal executive power, assent to Bills, oaths, appointments, summoning and addressing the Legislature.

🔗 Federal link

Appointed by the President, reports on constitutional breakdown (Art. 356), reserves Bills for the President (Art. 200), and may act as Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory (Art. 239(2)).

📚 Statutory & special roles

Chancellor of State universities (by university statute, not by Arts. 153–167) and special duties in Scheduled and Tribal Areas (Fifth and Sixth Schedules).

📌 Scope note: Part VI governs States. Union Territories with legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir) are run by Lieutenant Governors or Administrators under separate provisions and statutes — do not mix them with Arts. 153–167.

🎬 Act I · The Constitutional Head

👑 The Governor's Office — Articles 153–160

Begin with the office itself: why every State has a Governor, how power is vested, who appoints, how long he serves, what he must be, what he may not be, and how he swears in. Keep three kinds of authority apart: constitutional text, convention and commission recommendation.

153👑 Governors of States🔴 Core · A Governor for each State · Common Governor allowed

153 There shall be a Governor for each State. The proviso — inserted by the Seventh Amendment, 1956 — allows the same person to be appointed Governor of two or more States.

A common Governor does not merge the States. He performs each office separately, normally on the advice of that State's own Council of Ministers. Emoluments are apportioned under Art. 158(3A).

🚨 Exam trap: "Every State has a Governor" ≠ "every State has a different Governor". Also, a Lieutenant Governor in a Union Territory is not an Art. 153 Governor.
↔ Art. 52 (President)↔ Art. 158(3A)7th Amendment · 1956
154⚙️ Executive Power of the State🔴 Core · Vesting · Direct or subordinate exercise

154(1) The executive power of the State is vested in the Governor and is exercised by him either directly or through officers subordinate to him, in accordance with this Constitution.

154(2)(a) It does not transfer to the Governor functions that an existing law confers on another authority.

154(2)(b) Parliament or the State Legislature may by law confer functions on authorities subordinate to the Governor.

🔍 What is "executive power"?

It is the residual governmental power — administering departments, framing and implementing policy, maintaining public order and conducting government affairs — that is neither legislative nor judicial. Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955) shows that it is wider than merely executing statutes, yet it cannot override a law, violate rights without legal authority, or spend public money without legislative sanction.

🔗 Read together: 154 (where power vests) → 163 (who advises) → 164(2) (who is answerable) → 166 (how action is expressed).
🚨 Do not write "the Governor personally exercises all executive power". "In accordance with this Constitution" imports the parliamentary system.
↔ Art. 53 (President)↔ Art. 162↔ Arts. 163–164, 166
155📜 Appointment of Governor🔴 Core · President · Warrant under hand and seal

155 The Governor is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal. There is no popular election and no State electoral college. In practice the President acts on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers (Art. 74).

The Constituent Assembly preferred nomination to election, in the Canadian rather than the American style, so that a popularly elected Governor would not rival the Chief Minister's mandate.

⚖️ Hargovind Pant v. Raghukul Tilak (1979): The Governor holds an independent constitutional office, not employment under the Government of India. Appointment by the President does not make him an agent or subordinate of the Union Government.
📌 Convention ≠ constitutional condition: Choosing a person from outside the State and consulting the Chief Minister are conventions and Sarkaria/Punchhi recommendations. They are not written into Art. 155 or 157.
↔ Art. 54 (President is elected)↔ Art. 74↔ Art. 157
156⏳ Term of Office of Governor🔴 Core · Pleasure · Resignation · Five years · Holdover
  • 156(1) The Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President.
  • 156(2) He may resign by writing under his hand addressed to the President.
  • 156(3) Subject to the above, the term is five years from the date he enters upon office.
  • Proviso: even after the term expires, he continues until his successor enters upon office.

157🪪 Qualifications for Appointment🔴 Core · Citizen of India · Age 35 · Nothing more

No person is eligible for appointment as Governor unless he is (a) a citizen of India and (b) has completed 35 years of age.

There is no requirement of a degree, legislative experience, residence in the State, or being an outsider.

🚨 President comparison: Art. 58 also requires the President to be qualified for election to the Lok Sabha. Art. 157 has no such requirement for the Governor.
🤝 Conventions & recommendations (non-justiciable): an eminent person in some walk of life; not belonging to the State; not recently active in politics; meaningful consultation with the Chief Minister (Sarkaria 1988, Punchhi 2010).
🧠 C–35Citizen + 35 — the two express qualifications.
158🏠 Conditions of the Governor's Office🟠 Supporting · No seat · No profit · Protected emoluments
  • 158(1) Not a member of Parliament or a State Legislature. If such a member is appointed, his seat is deemed vacated on the date he enters office.
  • 158(2) Shall not hold any other office of profit.
  • 158(3) Entitled, without payment of rent, to official residences (Raj Bhavan), and to emoluments, allowances and privileges determined by Parliament by law (Second Schedule until then).
  • 158(3A) A common Governor's emoluments are allocated among the States by the President.
  • 158(4) Emoluments and allowances cannot be diminished during his term.
💰 Three authorities, three jobs: Parliament fixes the Governor's emoluments; the President allocates them for a common Governor; Art. 202(3)(a) makes them charged expenditure on the State Consolidated Fund (discussed, not voted).
🚨 The Governor is a part of the State Legislature (Art. 168) but not a member of either House. His emoluments are fixed by Parliament, not by the State Legislature.

159🤚 Oath or Affirmation by the Governor🟠 Supporting · High Court Chief Justice · Text is in the Article

Every Governor and every person discharging the Governor's functions must, before entering office, make and subscribe an oath or affirmation before the Chief Justice of the High Court exercising jurisdiction over the State, or, in his absence, the senior-most available Judge of that Court.

  • Faithfully execute the office (or discharge the functions);
  • Preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law;
  • Devote himself to the service and well-being of the people of the State.
🚨 Location trap: the Governor's oath is set out in Article 159 itself, not in the Third Schedule. A Minister's oath (Art. 164(3)) is in the Third Schedule.
🧠 F–P–SFaithfully execute · Preserve the Constitution & law · Serve the people.
↔ Art. 60 (President's oath)↔ Art. 164(3)
160🔄 Discharge of Functions in Contingencies🟢 Factual · President makes arrangements

The President may make such provision as he thinks fit for the discharge of the Governor's functions in any contingency not provided for in this Chapter (death, illness, absence and similar situations).

In practice the arrangement is often an additional charge to a neighbouring Governor, and on occasion the Chief Justice of the High Court has acted as Governor.

🧩 No automatic succession: the Chief Minister, Speaker or Chief Justice does not become Governor by operation of the Constitution. Art. 160 requires a presidential order, and Art. 159 requires the oath again.

🎬 Act II · Mercy & Executive Reach

🕊️ Clemency & Competence — Articles 161–162

Art. 161 asks "when may the Governor soften punishment?" Art. 162 asks "over which subjects does State executive power run?" Both are bounded by the State's own sphere.

161🕊️ Power to Grant Pardons, etc.🔴 Core · State executive field · Advice-bound · Reviewable

The Governor may grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the State extends.

🕊️ Distinguish the forms of relief

PardonRelieves the offender of the sentence and its penal consequences. It is executive relief, not a judicial acquittal.🧽 Wipe clean
CommutationSubstitutes one kind of punishment with a lighter kind (e.g., rigorous to simple imprisonment).🔁 Change the kind

📝 Counting note: the Article names four nouns (pardon, reprieve, respite, remission) and three verbs (suspend, remit, commute). Textbooks collapse these into five forms; the text also mentions suspension. Know both.

162🗺️ Extent of Executive Power of the State🔴 Core · Follows legislative competence · Union proviso

Subject to the Constitution, the executive power of a State extends to matters on which the State Legislature has power to make laws (State List, and Concurrent List where the State may legislate).

Proviso: in a matter on which both the State Legislature and Parliament may legislate, the State's executive power is subject to, and limited by, executive power expressly conferred on the Union or its authorities by the Constitution or by a law of Parliament.

🏡 State List

Executive reach follows legislative reach — for example public order, police and public health.

🤝 Concurrent List

The State is not automatically displaced because Parliament can legislate. Look for an express Union executive assignment in the Constitution or a parliamentary law.

🔍 Do not confuse: Art. 162 concerns executive power; Art. 254 concerns repugnancy of laws in the Concurrent field. Federal links: Art. 256–257 (Union directions), Art. 258 (Union entrusts functions to a State) and Art. 258A (Governor entrusts functions to the Union with its consent).
🧠 Reach + RestrictionLegislative field gives the reach; the Constitution and express Union assignments give the limits.

🎬 Act III · The Democratic Engine

🤝 Chief Minister & Council of Ministers — Articles 163–164

These are the engine-room provisions of State parliamentary government. Art. 163 settles who advises and where discretion survives; Art. 164 settles who is appointed, how many, to whom they answer, and when they must be legislators.

163🧭 Council of Ministers to Aid and Advise🔴 Core · General rule · Narrow discretion · Confidential advice

163(1) There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of his functions, except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion.

163(2) If a question arises whether a matter is one in which the Governor must act in his discretion, his decision is final, and anything done is not to be questioned on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in discretion. This is not a charter for unlimited discretion: courts can still examine whether a constitutional discretion exists.

163(3) Courts cannot inquire into whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the Governor.

🚨 Confidential advice ≠ unreviewable action. Art. 163(3) shields the advice from inquiry; it does not immunise every governmental decision from judicial review.

164🏛️ Other Provisions as to Ministers🔴 Core · Appointment · 15% · Responsibility · Oath · Six months

1. 👤 Appointment — Art. 164(1)

The Governor appoints the Chief Minister. Other Ministers are appointed by the Governor on the advice of the Chief Minister. Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the Governor — but in a parliamentary system that pleasure is exercised on the Chief Minister's advice and cannot be used to dismiss a Ministry that enjoys the Assembly's confidence.

2. 🌿 Tribal welfare Minister — proviso to 164(1)

There must be a Minister in charge of tribal welfare in Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha; he may also hold SC welfare, backward class welfare or other work. The 94th Amendment (2006) removed Bihar and added Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand.

🧠 C–J–M–OChhattisgarh · Jharkhand · Madhya Pradesh · Odisha. (Bihar is not on the list.)

3. 📏 Size of the Council — Art. 164(1A) (91st Amendment, 2003)

The total number of Ministers, including the Chief Minister, must not exceed 15% of the total membership of the Legislative Assembly alone (not the Council's strength), and must not be fewer than 12.

4. 🔀 Defectors — Art. 164(1B)

A member disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule is also barred from being appointed a Minister from the date of disqualification until his term would expire or he is re-elected, whichever is earlier. Art. 361B similarly restricts remunerative political posts — keep it separate from Art. 361 (immunities).

5. 🤝 Collective responsibility — Art. 164(2)

The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly — never to the Legislative Council. If the Assembly withdraws confidence, the whole Ministry must go. Cabinet solidarity means Ministers publicly defend agreed policy; a Minister who cannot do so should resign.

6. 🤚 Oath — Art. 164(3)

Before entering office a Minister takes the oath of office and the oath of secrecy before the Governor, in the forms in the Third Schedule.

7. ⏳ Six-month rule — Art. 164(4)

A Minister who for six consecutive months is not a member of the State Legislature ceases to be a Minister at the end of that period. Membership of either House suffices, so a Chief Minister may be an MLC.

🚨 Two limits on the six-month rule: S. R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab (2001) — a non-member cannot be reappointed repeatedly to restart the clock. B. R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu (2001) — Art. 164(4) does not allow a person who is already disqualified from legislative membership to become Chief Minister.

8. 💰 Salaries — Art. 164(5)

Determined by the State Legislature by law; until then, the Second Schedule applies.

🧭 Collective vs individual responsibility

Collective (164(2)) is to the Assembly. Individual flows from pleasure (164(1)): a Minister may be dropped on the Chief Minister's advice.

🪑 Council vs Cabinet

The Council of Ministers is the wider body; the Cabinet is its senior decision-making core. Ranks such as Cabinet Minister and Minister of State are practice — Art. 164 does not prescribe a compulsory three-tier hierarchy.

🧩 Resignation insight: the death or resignation of the Chief Minister ends the existing Ministry as a Ministry, though a caretaker arrangement keeps government running. One other Minister's resignation does not dissolve the Council.

🎬 Act IV · The State's First Law Officer

⚖️ Advocate-General — Article 165

The Advocate-General is the State's highest constitutional law officer. Do not confuse the office with the Law Minister, the Attorney-General of India, a High Court judge or the Public Prosecutor.

165⚖️ Advocate-General for the State🔴 Core · Appointment · Qualification · Duties · Pleasure

165(1) The Governor appoints a person qualified to be appointed a Judge of a High Court as Advocate-General. In practice this appointment is made on ministerial advice.

📋 Qualification link (Art. 217(2)): citizen of India who has held a judicial office for at least ten years in India, or been a High Court advocate for at least ten years. The Advocate-General need not be a sitting or retired judge.

165(2) Duties: advise the State Government on legal matters; perform other legal duties assigned by the Governor; discharge functions conferred by the Constitution or any other law.

165(3) Tenure: holds office during the Governor's pleasure; receives remuneration as the Governor determines.

166📂 Conduct of Business of the State Government🔴 Core · Governor's name · Authentication · Rules of business

166(1) All executive action of the Government of a State is expressed to be taken in the name of the Governor.

166(2) Orders and instruments made and executed in the Governor's name are authenticated as specified in rules made by the Governor, and their validity cannot be questioned on the ground that they were not made or executed by the Governor personally.

166(3) The Governor makes rules for the more convenient transaction of State Government business and for the allocation of business among Ministers, except business where the Constitution requires him to act in discretion.

🖊️ No personal signature needed: a Minister's or officer's decision under the rules of business is, in law, the Governor's. Authentication lets a large administration function.
🚨 Authentication does not cure illegality. It only removes the objection "the Governor did not personally make this". An order can still be challenged for lack of competence, breach of statute, violation of fundamental rights or non-compliance with mandatory rules.

167🌉 Duties of the Chief Minister towards the Governor🔴 Core · Communicate · Furnish · Submit

It is the Chief Minister's duty:

  • 167(a) to communicate to the Governor all decisions of the Council of Ministers on State administration and proposals for legislation;
  • 167(b) to furnish such information on administration and legislative proposals as the Governor calls for;
  • 167(c) if the Governor so requires, to submit for the Council's consideration any matter on which a Minister has decided but which the Council has not considered.

📨 Information bridge

The constitutional head must be informed. Art. 167 locates that duty squarely in the Chief Minister.

⛔ Not a general veto

Seeking information or collective consideration does not let the Governor replace the Council's lawful policy with a personal decision.

🎯 Art. 167(c) insight: it protects collective responsibility by stopping a single Minister from taking a major decision without Cabinet consideration.

168🏛️ Governor as Part of the State Legislature🟠 Supporting · Arts. 168, 171(5), 177, 192

168 The Legislature of a State consists of the Governor and one House (the Assembly) or two Houses in the bicameral States. The Governor is a constitutional component of the Legislature but not a member of any House — which is why his assent (Art. 200) is needed for a Bill to become law.

  • Art. 171(3)(e), 171(5): the Governor nominates about one-sixth of the Legislative Council from persons with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, the co-operative movement and social service.
  • Art. 177: Ministers and the Advocate-General may speak in and take part in proceedings of either House and its committees, with no right to vote by virtue of this Article.
  • Art. 192: a question whether a member has incurred a disqualification under Art. 191(1) goes to the Governor, who must obtain the Election Commission's opinion and act according to it.
🚨 Do not merge disqualification routes: Art. 192 covers 191(1) grounds (office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, citizenship, Parliament's law). Defection under the Tenth Schedule (Art. 191(2)) is decided by the Speaker or Chairman as a tribunal (Kihoto Hollohan, 1992), subject to judicial review. The Governor does not act on the Council of Ministers' advice in Art. 192 matters.
📌 History: Anglo-Indian nomination (Art. 333) ended in 2020 under the 104th Amendment; do not list it as a present gubernatorial power.
174–176🔔 Sessions, Dissolution & Addresses🔴 Core · Summon · Prorogue · Dissolve · Address

174(1) The Governor summons the House(s) as he thinks fit, but six months must not intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next (at least two sessions a year).

174(2) He may prorogue either House and dissolve the Legislative Assembly.

175 The Governor may address either House or both together and send messages on pending Bills or other matters. This is a general optional power.

176 The Governor must address the Assembly (or both Houses together) at the commencement of the first session after each general election and the first session of each year, explaining the causes of the summons. Time must be allotted for discussing the address (Motion of Thanks).

⚖️ These powers are exercised on ministerial advice (Shamsher Singh; Nabam Rebia). The Governor's address is drafted by the Council of Ministers and states the government's policy.
🚨 Not every session: Art. 176 does not require an address at every session, and one address suffices if both triggers coincide. Budget, Monsoon and Winter sessions are convention, not constitutional text.
200👑 Governor's Assent to Bills🔴 Core · Assent · Withhold · Reserve · Return

When a Bill passed by the Legislature is presented, the Governor declares that he assents, withholds assent, or reserves it for the President.

First proviso: as soon as possible after presentation he may return a Bill other than a Money Bill with a message asking for reconsideration. If it is passed again, with or without amendment, he shall not withhold assent.

Second proviso: he must reserve a Bill that would so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position that Court is designed to fill.

CourseKey point
AssentThe Bill becomes law.
Withhold assentThe Bill does not become law. State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor (2023) says a Governor who withholds assent must follow the return procedure, rather than sit on the Bill indefinitely.
ReturnOnly for non-Money Bills; after repassage assent cannot be withheld.
ReserveSent to the President; Art. 201 governs. Mandatory in the High Court situation.
🚨 Current-law discipline: Art. 200 fixes no number of days; only "as soon as possible" appears in the first proviso. The 2025 Supreme Court proceedings in the Tamil Nadu litigation and the later Presidential Reference advisory opinion deal with timelines and "deemed assent". Do not present earlier judicially prescribed deadlines as a settled final rule; read the official opinions for the exact current position.

201 Bills Reserved for the President🔴 Core · Presidential choice · Six-month reconsideration

The President declares assent or withholding of assent. For a non-Money Bill he may direct the Governor to return it with a message, and the House(s) must reconsider it within six months of receiving the message. If passed again, it is presented again to the President.

🚨 Whose six months? It is the Legislature's reconsideration period, not a deadline for the President to decide. Unlike Art. 200's first proviso, Art. 201 does not expressly oblige assent after repassage.
🔗 Federal link: under Art. 254(2), a State law on a Concurrent List matter that is repugnant to an earlier Union law may prevail in that State if reserved for and assented to by the President — though Parliament may override it later.

202(1) The Governor causes to be laid before the House or Houses of the State Legislature, for every financial year, a statement of estimated receipts and expenditure — the Annual Financial Statement (State Budget).

202(2) The statement must show expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund separately from other expenditure, and distinguish revenue expenditure from other expenditure.

202(3) The following are charged expenditure (discussable, but not submitted to the vote):

  • (a) the Governor's emoluments and allowances and other expenditure relating to his office;
  • (b) salaries and allowances of the Speaker, Deputy Speaker and (where there is a Council) the Chairman and Deputy Chairman;
  • (c) debt charges of the State;
  • (d) salaries and allowances of High Court Judges;
  • (e) sums required to satisfy judgments, decrees or awards of any court or tribunal;
  • (f) any other expenditure declared by the Constitution or by the State Legislature by law to be so charged.

203 Charged expenditure may be discussed but is not voted. Other expenditure goes to the Legislative Assembly as demands for grants; the Assembly may assent, refuse or reduce a demand, but cannot increase it. No demand may be made except on the Governor's recommendation (203(3)). The Legislative Council may discuss the Budget but does not vote on demands.

204 After the grants are voted, an Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund. No amendment may vary the amount or alter the destination of a grant or vary charged expenditure, and no money may be withdrawn except under appropriation made by law.

205 Supplementary, additional or excess grants are presented when the authorised amount is insufficient, a new service arises, or expenditure has exceeded the grant. 206 The Assembly may also pass votes on account, votes of credit and exceptional grants.

207 Financial Bills need the Governor's recommendation. A Bill dealing with the matters in Art. 199(1) — including a Money Bill — cannot be introduced or moved without it, and a Bill that would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund cannot be passed by either House unless the Governor has recommended its consideration.

267(2) A State law may create a Contingency Fund at the Governor's disposal, from which advances can be made for unforeseen expenditure pending legislative authorisation.

💡 Financial initiative: only the executive proposes expenditure and taxation (through the Governor's recommendation); only the elected Assembly votes it. The Governor does not prepare a rival budget — the Council of Ministers does.
🚨 Certification trap: whether a Bill is a Money Bill is certified by the Speaker (Art. 199(3)), not the Governor. The Council cannot originate a Money Bill and must return it with recommendations within fourteen days (Art. 198); the Assembly may accept or reject those recommendations.

213⚡ Ordinance-Making Power🔴 Core · Legislative recess · Immediate action · Six weeks from reassembly

213(1) Except when the Legislative Assembly (or, in a bicameral State, both Houses) is in session, the Governor may promulgate Ordinances if satisfied that circumstances make immediate action necessary. In practice the power is exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers.

President's instructions are required if a Bill containing the same provisions (i) would have needed the President's previous sanction for introduction, (ii) the Governor would have thought it necessary to reserve for the President, or (iii) an Act containing them would be invalid unless reserved for and assented to by the President.

213(2) An Ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of the Legislature, but it must be laid before the Assembly (and Council, if any) and ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly — or earlier if a disapproving resolution is passed. Where the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six weeks run from the later date. The Governor may withdraw it at any time.

213(3) An Ordinance that would be invalid if it were an Act of the Legislature is void.

⚖️ D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) condemned routine re-promulgation as a fraud on the Constitution. Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) confirmed that ordinance-making is an emergency power, not a parallel legislature, that placing the Ordinance before the Legislature is mandatory, and that the Governor's satisfaction can be judicially examined.
🚨 Time-limit trap: the textual rule is six weeks from reassembly. The familiar "six months + six weeks" is a derived outer limit: up to six months may pass between sittings (Art. 174(1)), after which the six weeks run. Do not describe it as the wording of Art. 213.

239(2)🧩 Governor as Administrator of an Adjoining Union Territory🟠 Supporting · Separate capacity · Independent of the State ministry

The President may appoint the Governor of a State as the Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory. In that capacity he acts independently of his State Council of Ministers.

🚨 Two constitutional hats: this independence exists only while discharging the Administrator's separate office. It does not make the Governor generally independent of the Council when governing the State.

316📋 Other Constitutional Appointments by the Governor🟢 Factual · State PSC · SEC · Finance Commission · Judicial service

316 The Governor appoints the Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission. The term is six years or until age sixty-two, whichever is earlier.

243K The Governor appoints the State Election Commissioner, who can be removed only in the manner and on the grounds applicable to a High Court Judge.

243I The Governor constitutes the State Finance Commission every five years to review Panchayat finances. Art. 243Y applies the same arrangement to Municipalities.

233–234 District Judges are appointed, and the State judicial service is recruited, only after the consultation the Constitution requires (High Court, and for recruitment also the State PSC).

🚨 Appointment ≠ removal: a State PSC member is removed by the President under Art. 317 on a Supreme Court inquiry. The Governor has only a limited power of suspension in the circumstances laid down there.
356🚨 Failure of Constitutional Machinery🔴 Core · Governor's report · Presidential proclamation · Judicial review

If the President is satisfied, on a report from the Governor or otherwise, that the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, he may issue a Proclamation assuming State functions and declaring that the Legislature's powers are exercisable by or under Parliament.

The Governor reports; he does not impose President's Rule. A report is not indispensable, because the text says "or otherwise".

Duration: Parliamentary approval is needed within two months, with a special rule if the Lok Sabha is dissolved. An approved Proclamation runs for six months at a time, up to a general maximum of three years, with extra conditions for continuation beyond one year.

⚖️ S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994):
  • Presidential satisfaction is judicially reviewable (mala fides, irrelevant or extraneous material).
  • A disputed majority should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the Assembly.
  • The Assembly should be kept in suspended animation until Parliament approves the Proclamation, not dissolved at once.
🚨 Bad government ≠ constitutional breakdown. Political disagreement, corruption allegations or a change of government at the Union do not by themselves justify Art. 356. Rameshwar Prasad (2006) struck down a speculative dissolution.

361🛡️ Protection of the President and Governors🔴 Core · Personal immunity · Governmental action remains reviewable

361(1) The Governor is not answerable to any court for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of his office, or for acts done or purporting to be done in their exercise. Proceedings against the Government remain possible.

361(2) No criminal proceedings may be instituted or continued against the Governor during his term.

361(3) No process for arrest or imprisonment may issue from any court during his term.

361(4) Civil proceedings in respect of a personal act may be instituted during the term only after two months' written notice stating the nature of the proceedings, the cause of action, the parties and the relief claimed.

🚨 Personal immunity ≠ immunity for unconstitutional action. Courts can review State action (Arts. 32 and 226) by naming the State or appropriate officials. Art. 361B (disqualification for remunerative political posts) is a different provision.

🧠 Active Recall Studio

🧠 Memory Lab — Retrieve Before You Reveal

Answer aloud before opening each card. Separate the rule from its exceptions, then attach one article number and one case wherever possible.

1. Does every State need a different Governor?

No. Art. 153 (proviso, 7th Amendment, 1956) allows the same person to be Governor of two or more States.

2. Who appoints the Governor, and who can impeach him?

The President appoints under Art. 155. There is no constitutional impeachment; tenure is governed by Art. 156 (five years, subject to pleasure, with holdover).

3. Where is the Governor's oath located?

In Art. 159 itself. A Minister's oaths of office and secrecy are in the Third Schedule.

4. Can the Governor pardon a death sentence?

No. He can suspend, remit or commute it for offences within the State's executive field. Only the President can pardon (Art. 72).

5. Is Art. 163 a source of unlimited personal discretion?

No. Discretion needs a constitutional basis. Shamsher Singh and Nabam Rebia are the central guardrails. Art. 163(3) bars inquiry into the advice, not into the action.

6. A 60-member Assembly: what is the maximum ministry size?

15% of 60 = 9, but the floor of 12 applies, so the maximum is 12 including the Chief Minister.

7. To which House is the ministry collectively responsible?

The Legislative Assembly (Art. 164(2)), never the Council. A Chief Minister may nevertheless be an MLC.

8. Which four States require a tribal-welfare Minister?

Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha (C–J–M–O). Bihar was removed in 2006.

9. Can authentication save an unconstitutional order?

No. Art. 166(2) only removes the objection that the Governor did not personally make it. It does not cure illegality.

10. What are the Chief Minister's three duties under Art. 167?

Communicate Council decisions, furnish information the Governor calls for, and submit an individual Minister's decision for collective consideration when required (C–F–S).

11. When does an Ordinance under Art. 213 lapse?

Six weeks after reassembly (from the later date if the Houses meet on different dates), or earlier on disapproval or withdrawal.

12. Who removes a State PSC member?

The President under Art. 317, after a Supreme Court inquiry — not the Governor, even though the Governor appoints.

🏆 The Closing Frame

🏆 One Connected Constitutional Story

The Governor supplies constitutional form, the Council supplies democratic direction, the Assembly supplies accountability, and the courts enforce legal boundaries. Read every power through that structure rather than treating the Governor as a parallel elected government.

The office is constitutional; the government is parliamentary; responsibility is democratic; and every power remains bounded by law.
