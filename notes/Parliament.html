Part A · The Philosophy

🌱 WHY DO WE NEED A PARLIAMENT AT ALL?

Before the articles and the arithmetic — the moral case for a talking chamber.

🤔 The Core Idea

A Parliament exists because people cannot all govern directly — so they govern through representatives whom they choose, control, and can remove. It is indirect democracy engineered into an institution.

The Greek city-state could gather in the Agora; a nation of 1.4 billion cannot. Parliament is therefore the technology of scale for democracy — it compresses the will of the multitude into a debating chamber of a few hundred, without severing the thread of accountability that runs back to the voter.

🗳️Public ParticipationEnables ordinary citizens to take part in decision-making indirectly, through people they elect and can throw out.
✅Consent of the GovernedGovernment derives authority from people's consent, not force. Locke's social contract, institutionalised.
🧾AccountabilityExecutive is answerable daily — Question Hour, motions, committees, and ultimately the No-Confidence Motion.
🔍DeliberationNational issues get debated openly before becoming law. "Government by discussion" — Bagehot.
⚖️LegitimacyLaws made by the people's representatives command moral obedience, not merely legal compliance.
🕊️Peaceful ChangeParliament allows governments to fall and policies to reverse without bloodshed — the ballot replaces the bullet.
🪞Representation of DiversityA federal, multi-lingual, multi-faith society finds a single arena where every identity has a seat and a voice.
🎓Political EducationDebates telecast on Sansad TV educate the nation; Mill called this the "teaching" function.
🛡️Grievance RedressalMill's "nation's Committee of Grievances" — the safety valve where public discontent is voiced, not suppressed.

Part B · Definition

📖 MEANING & DEFINITION OF PARLIAMENT

From a French verb to Article 79 — how a word became an institution.

1️⃣ Etymology 🔤

The word “Parliament” comes from the French parler = “to talk / to speak” (Old French parlement, Medieval Latin parliamentum).

👉 Hence, Parliament literally = “the place where the nation talks” — a deliberative body, not merely a law-factory. 🗣️ The very name reminds us that a Parliament that does not talk — that passes bills without debate — has betrayed its own etymology.

🇮🇳 The Indian equivalents carry the same soul: Sansad (from Sanskrit sam + sad = “to sit together”), Lok Sabha = Assembly of the People, Rajya Sabha = Council of the States.

2️⃣ Constitutional Definition 📜

ARTICLE 79 — Constitution of Parliament

“There shall be a Parliament for the Union which shall consist of the President and two Houses to be known respectively as the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha).” — The Constitution of India, Part V, Chapter II

🧮 THE PARLIAMENT EQUATION

   👑 PRESIDENT   +   🏛️ RAJYA SABHA   +   🏛️ LOK SABHA   =   🇮🇳 PARLIAMENT
   (Head of State)   (Council of States)  (House of People)
                      Upper / Second        Lower / First
                          House                House
                      Federal Chamber     Popular Chamber
                      Indirectly elected  Directly elected
                      NEVER dissolved     5-year life
⚠️ Precision Point: Parliament ≠ the two Houses alone. A Bill passed by both Houses is not law until the President assents. Therefore the President is not decorative — he is constitutionally constitutive of Parliament.

👑 WHY IS THE PRESIDENT A PART OF PARLIAMENT?

(Even though he/she is NOT a member of either House!) — Very high-yield Prelims point ⚠️

#Reason
1️⃣ AssentNo Bill can become law without the President's assent Art. 111. He may assent, withhold, or return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration (suspensive veto — must assent if re-passed).
2️⃣ SummonSummons and prorogues both Houses; dissolves the Lok Sabha Art. 85. Without summoning, Parliament legally cannot meet.
3️⃣ OrdinancePromulgates Ordinances when Parliament is not in session Art. 123 — a direct legislative power, co-extensive with Parliament's.

6️⃣ NominateNominates 12 members to the Rajya Sabha Art. 80(3); (formerly 2 Anglo-Indians to the LS under old Art. 331).
7️⃣ RecommendRecommendation needed for Money Bills, Financial Bills & demands for grants Arts. 110, 113, 117.
8️⃣ SanctionCertain Bills need prior sanction — e.g. Art. 3 (reorganisation of States), Art. 304(b) proviso (State trade restrictions).
9️⃣ Lay ReportsCauses to be laid before Parliament the reports of the CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission, NCSC/NCST, NCBC and the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities.

1️⃣2️⃣ BudgetCauses the Annual Financial Statement to be laid before both Houses Art. 112.

⚠️ Fine Distinction: The President is part of Parliament ✅ but NOT a member of either House ❌. Hence parliamentary privileges do NOT extend to the President — they extend to Ministers and the Attorney-General, who under Art. 88 may speak in either House, in a joint sitting and in committees, but have no right to vote (unless a Minister is a member of that House).

⚠️ Classic Trap: Nominated members of Parliament CANNOT vote in the election of the President (electoral college = elected MPs + elected MLAs only) — but they CAN vote in the election of the Vice-President (electoral college = all members of both Houses, elected and nominated). 🗺️ Representation of Union Territories in the Rajya Sabha

Only 3 UTs are represented: Delhi — 3 Puducherry — 1 Jammu & Kashmir — 4. Chosen by an electoral college constituted for the purpose (in practice, the elected members of the UT Assembly), via PR by STV. The other UTs (Chandigarh, Ladakh, Lakshadweep, A&N, D&NH-Daman&Diu) have no Rajya Sabha representation because they lack legislatures / adequate population. Seat allocation for States & UTs is in the Fourth Schedule.
Part E · The Heart ❤️

🧬 THE 20 DEFINING CHARACTERISTICS

Twenty structural DNA strands that make the Indian Parliament what it is.

1️⃣🏛️ BICAMERAL (Two-Chambered)

Two Houses — Rajya Sabha (Upper) + Lok Sabha (Lower).

Why bicameralism? ✅

  • Checks hasty, defective, careless & ill-considered legislation — provides “revision and second thought.”
  • Gives representation to States → federal balance ⚖️
  • Brings in experts/eminent persons who cannot or will not win direct elections (the 12 nominated).
  • Acts as a revising chamber, a “sober second thought” / “de-bunking chamber.”
  • Provides continuity — the RS never dissolves, so the legislature never wholly disappears.
  • Acts as a safety valve against majoritarianism in the popular House.
🗣️ Sir John Seeley: “The second chamber is a brake, not an engine.”  |  🗣️ Abbé Sieyès (critique): “If the second chamber agrees with the first, it is superfluous; if it disagrees, it is mischievous.” — Use both for balanced Mains answers.

2️⃣👑 PRESIDENT IS AN INTEGRAL PART

Unlike the US (where the President is excluded from Congress), India follows “Crown-in-Parliament” → “President-in-Parliament.” (12 reasons detailed in Part C.) The doctrine means Parliament acts as a tri-partite composite: two deliberating chambers plus one assenting head.

3️⃣🗳️ REPRESENTATIVE & DEMOCRATIC

Based on Universal Adult Franchise — every citizen 18+ (reduced from 21 by the 61st Amendment Act, 1988 ✨, effective 28 March 1989) who is not otherwise disqualified may vote.

🏛️Lok Sabha → DIRECTElected directly by the people from single-member territorial constituencies. Article 326 guarantees adult suffrage.
🏛️Rajya Sabha → INDIRECTElected by the elected MLAs of State Assemblies (MLCs excluded) via PR-STV with an open ballot (since 2003) to curb cross-voting.
🆕 Reform note: The RPA (Amendment) Act, 2003 removed the “domicile/residence” requirement for RS candidates and introduced the open ballot. The Supreme Court upheld both in Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2006), holding that the Rajya Sabha is not required to be a chamber of "residents", and that the secrecy of the ballot is not an absolute constitutional value in an indirect election.

4️⃣🔗 RESPONSIBLE / ACCOUNTABLE GOVERNMENT

Article 75(3): The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Art. 75(4) individual responsibility to the President; Art. 75(1A) caps the CoM at 15% of LS strength (91st Amendment, 2003); Art. 75(5) — a Minister must become an MP within 6 months.

→ The Executive is born of, sustained by and removable by the Legislature. Fusion, not separation, of powers.

💡 Insight: Collective responsibility means the CoM "sink or swim together" — a no-confidence motion against one Minister is a motion against the whole Council. Even Ministers from the Rajya Sabha fall when the Lok Sabha withdraws confidence. Notably, only the Lok Sabha can enforce this; the Rajya Sabha can criticise but cannot remove.

5️⃣⚖️ FEDERAL FLAVOUR (with a Unitary Tilt)

  • RS represents States → the federal principle 🤝
  • BUT representation is on the basis of POPULATION, not equality of States.
    🇮🇳 UP = 31 seats; Maharashtra = 19; Tamil Nadu = 18; while Sikkim, Nagaland, Mizoram, Tripura, Manipur, Arunachal, Meghalaya, Goa = 1 each.
    🇺🇸 USA: every State gets exactly 2 Senators (50 States → 100 Senators), regardless of size.
  • 📜 The Fourth Schedule allocates RS seats to States & UTs.
  • ➡️ The Indian RS is thus a “quasi-federal” chamber — less federal than the US Senate, but far more federal than the House of Lords.
⚠️ Additional unitary tilts: (i) State legislatures have no role in RS members' removal; (ii) an RS member need not be a resident of the State he represents (post-2003); (iii) Art. 249 allows the RS itself to surrender State autonomy; (iv) Art. 3 lets Parliament redraw States without their consent.

7️⃣🗺️ DUAL ELECTORAL SYSTEMS

HouseSystem
🏛️ Lok SabhaFirst-Past-The-Post (FPTP) + Territorial Representation + Single-member constituencies — whoever comes first wins (no 50% needed!). Secret ballot.
🏛️ Rajya SabhaProportional Representation by means of the Single Transferable Vote (STV) — open ballot for MLAs; preferences marked 1, 2, 3… (1st preference mandatory, else the vote is invalid).
📌 PR by STV is used for: President 👑 (secret ballot), Vice-President 🎖️ (secret ballot), Rajya Sabha (open ballot), and State Legislative Councils.
❌ Not used for: Lok Sabha & State Legislative Assemblies.
🧮 Quota formula (RS): Quota = ⌊(Total valid votes ÷ (Seats + 1))⌋ + 1. Each MLA's vote carries a value of 100 and preferences are transferred by the surplus-transfer method.

🚫 Why the Constitution Rejected PR for the Lok Sabha

📚Too complicatedUnsuited to a largely illiterate electorate in 1950.
💰Highly expensiveHuge multi-member constituencies, costly campaigns and counting.
🗳️No by-electionsVacancies filled from lists, weakening the voter's direct choice.
💔No intimate contactEliminates the close voter–representative bond of a small constituency.
🧩Minority thinkingPromotes group interests, sectionalism and communalism.
🎭Party over voterIncreases the significance of the party machine, reduces that of the individual voter.
⚠️Multiplies partiesFragmentation → coalition instability → unsuited to a parliamentary government needing a stable majority.
🏛️Weak governmentPerpetual hung houses would defeat Ambedkar's aim of a "responsible and workable" executive.
📉 The FPTP Critique (for Mains): FPTP systematically produces a seats–votes mismatch — a party can win a majority of seats on ~35–40% of the vote, while sizeable minorities remain unrepresented. Reform suggestions include the Mixed Member Proportional (MMP) model (Law Commission's 170th Report, 1999, suggested adding 25% seats via a list system) and the run-off / 50%+1 requirement.

8️⃣📊 POPULATION-BASED, EQUITABLE REPRESENTATION Art. 81(2)

  • Seats : Population ratio shall be the same for all States → uniformity between States.
  • Each constituency's population ratio shall be the same throughout a State → uniformity within a State.
  • 🛡️ Exception: the "same ratio between States" rule does not apply to States with a population below 60 lakh.

🆕 BIG CURRENT AFFAIR (April 2026): Three Bills were introduced in the Lok Sabha on 16 April 2026 — the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026, the Delimitation Bill, 2026, and the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026. The Constitution Amendment Bill sought to raise the Lok Sabha's maximum strength from 550 → 850 (815 from States + 35 from UTs), to let Parliament decide by ordinary law which census delimitation would use (effectively the 2011 census), and to de-link women's reservation from the "first census after 2023" trigger.

➡️ The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 was DEFEATED in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026 — it secured 298 ayes to 230 noes, short of the special majority (2/3 of members present and voting and a majority of the total membership — roughly 352 in a House of 543). This is a rare and highly examinable instance of a Constitution Amendment Bill failing on the floor. (Verify the latest status before your exam, as the issue remains live.)
⚖️ The Delimitation Dilemma: Southern and smaller States that curbed fertility fear losing relative weight; populous northern States argue that "one person, one vote, one value" demands re-proportioning. On 2011 figures at constant strength, Tamil Nadu would fall 39→32 and Kerala 20→15, while UP would rise 80→89 and Bihar 40→46. Proposed middle paths: freeze each State's share while expanding the House; strengthen the Rajya Sabha with equal State representation; or devolve more powers to States (the "federal bargain" approach).

9️⃣🤝 SOCIAL JUSTICE BUILT IN — Reservation of Seats

  • ❌ Communal / separate electorates ABANDONED (the spirit of the Poona Pact, 1932). SC/ST MPs are elected by ALL voters of the constituency (joint electorate) and may also contest general seats. (Only SC/ST candidates may contest a reserved seat, but every voter votes.)
  • 🪑 Arts. 330 & 332: SC/ST reservation in the LS and State Assemblies on a population-ratio basis — originally till 1960 (Art. 334), extended every 10 years → now till 25 January 2030 by the 104th Amendment Act, 2019. Current LS: 84 SC + 47 ST seats.
  • 🚫 Anglo-Indian nomination (2 in LS, 1 in some Assemblies) — provision NOT extended by the 104th Amendment; it lapsed on 25 January 2020 → maximum LS strength effectively 552 → 550 and actual 545 → 543.
  • 👩 106th Amendment Act, 2023 — the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam — inserts Arts. 330A, 332A, 239AA(2)(b): reserves 1/3rd of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly (including within the SC/ST quota); to operate for 15 years, extendable by Parliament; seats to rotate after each delimitation. Commencement was tied to the first census after 2023 + delimitation. ❌ Not applicable to the Rajya Sabha or Legislative Councils.
  • ⚠️ No reservation of any kind in the Rajya Sabha — not for SC/ST, not for women.
🆕 2026 Status: The Census 2027 is underway (house-listing phase from April 2026; population enumeration February 2027; reference date 1 March 2027) and is also India's first census to collect caste data since 1931. Because delimitation on the 2027 census cannot realistically be completed before the 2029 general election, the government's 2026 Bills tried to de-link women's reservation from that trigger — but the constitutional component was defeated on 17 April 2026, leaving the timing of women's reservation genuinely uncertain. 🎯 A prime Mains/essay flashpoint.

1️⃣1️⃣🚫 NOT SOVEREIGN (Unlike the British Parliament)

🇬🇧 British Doctrine of Parliamentary Sovereignty — A.V. Dicey's 3 Implications

  1. Parliament can make, amend, substitute or repeal ANY law.
  2. Constitutional laws can be made by the same procedure as ordinary laws — no distinction.
  3. Parliamentary laws cannot be declared invalid by the judiciary — no judicial review.
“The British Parliament can do everything except make a woman a man and a man a woman.”— Jean-Louis De Lolme

🇮🇳 The Chains on the Indian Parliament ⛓️

#LimitationExplanation
📜 1Written ConstitutionThe Constitution is the fundamental law; it defines the authority and jurisdiction of all three organs. Parliament must operate within it. (Britain: unwritten ❌)
🗺️ 2Federal SystemDivision of powers between the Union and the States (7th Schedule). Parliament cannot normally legislate on the State List — except in 5 abnormal situations, and then only temporarily. (Britain: unitary ❌)
⚖️ 3Judicial ReviewThe SC & HCs can strike down laws as void and ultra vires (Arts. 13, 32, 226, 131, 137). (Britain: no judicial review ❌)
🛡️ 4Fundamental RightsArticle 13 bars the State from making laws that take away or abridge Part III rights. (Britain: no written charter — liberty rests on the Rule of Law ❌)
➕ 5Basic Structure DoctrineKesavananda Bharati (1973), reaffirmed in Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao (1981), S.R. Bommai (1994), I.R. Coelho (2007) — even the constituent power under Art. 368 is limited.
🇺🇸 Note: The US Congress is also NOT sovereign, for the same 4 reasons (written Constitution, federalism, judicial review, Bill of Rights). Hence Indian Parliament ≈ US Congress in legal status, and ≠ British Parliament. However, the Indian Parliament is more powerful than the US Congress in one respect — it can amend most of its own Constitution unilaterally, whereas the US requires ratification by 3/4 of the States.
📉 Postscript on Britain: Even British sovereignty is today qualified in practice — by devolution (Scotland/Wales/NI), the Human Rights Act 1998 (courts may issue "declarations of incompatibility"), and the establishment of the UK Supreme Court in 2009. Still, formally, no British court can annul an Act of Parliament.

1️⃣4️⃣🛡️ PRIVILEGED & AUTONOMOUS

  • Art. 105 — Freedom of speech in Parliament; no court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in the House or its committees; immunity for publication of proceedings under the House's authority.
  • Art. 122 — Courts cannot inquire into parliamentary proceedings on grounds of irregularity of procedure. (But illegality and unconstitutionality remain reviewable — Raja Ram Pal v. Speaker (2007).)
  • Art. 121 — No discussion in Parliament on the conduct of SC/HC judges except on a motion for their removal.
  • Art. 98 — Each House has its own Secretariat and staff, independent of the executive.
  • Art. 118 — Each House makes its own Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business.
  • 👥 Individual privileges: freedom from arrest in civil cases during a session and 40 days before/after (❌ not in criminal or preventive-detention cases); exemption from jury service; freedom of speech.
  • 🏛️ Collective privileges: right to publish/prohibit publication of proceedings; to exclude strangers; to hold secret sittings; to make its own rules; to punish for contempt (admonition, reprimand, imprisonment); to receive immediate information of a member's arrest/detention; to institute inquiries.
⚠️ Privileges are still NOT codified. The 44th Amendment (1978) dropped the direct textual reference to the British House of Commons (as at the commencement of the Constitution) without changing the substance. Sources today: the Constitution, laws made by Parliament, the Rules of the Houses, parliamentary conventions, and judicial interpretations. Codification is debated — it would bring clarity but would also make privileges justiciable.
⚖️ Key cases: P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998) granted immunity to MPs who took bribes and then voted — overruled in 2024 by a 7-judge bench in Sita Soren v. Union of India, which held that bribery is not protected by Arts. 105/194 because the crime is complete on acceptance, irrespective of the vote. Also Keshav Singh's case (1965) on the balance between privileges and fundamental rights, and Raja Ram Pal (2007) upholding expulsion but affirming judicial review for illegality.

1️⃣5️⃣🗓️ SESSION-BASED, NOT CONTINUOUS

  • Art. 85(1): the gap between two sessions cannot exceed 6 months → Parliament must meet at least twice a year.
  • 3 usual sessions: Budget (Feb–May, longest, usually in two parts with a recess), Monsoon (Jul–Sep), Winter (Nov–Dec, shortest). Special sessions may be summoned at any time (e.g. September 2023, when the new building was occupied and the 106th Amendment was passed).
  • ❗ The Constitution prescribes NO minimum number of sitting days — a major criticism 📉. (The NCRWC recommended a minimum of 120 days for Parliament and 100 for larger State Assemblies.)
  • ❗ The Constitution does not mandate three sessions; it mandates only the 6-month gap.
  • ⏰ Typical sitting: morning 11 am–1 pm + post-lunch 2 pm–6 pm. Sittings may be extended, and the House does not sit on Saturdays, Sundays or gazetted holidays.
  • 🔑 Vocabulary: Summoning (President calls the House) · Sitting (a single day's meeting) · Session (period between summoning and prorogation) · Recess (period between prorogation and reassembly) · Adjournment sine die (terminating a sitting for an indefinite period) · Prorogation · Dissolution · Quorum.
  • 🚨 Art. 352(8): a special sitting of the Lok Sabha must be held within 14 days if 1/10th of the members give notice of a resolution disapproving a National Emergency.

1️⃣6️⃣🌐 MULTILINGUAL YET WORKABLE Art. 120

Business is transacted in Hindi or English; the Presiding Officer may permit a member who cannot adequately express himself in either to address the House in his/her mother tongue. Simultaneous interpretation is now available for all 22 Eighth Schedule languages into Hindi and English. English was to cease after 15 years (i.e. in 1965), but the Official Languages Act, 1963 allowed its indefinite continuance — a decisive act of linguistic accommodation after the anti-Hindi agitations.

📌 Compare: Art. 210 makes parallel provision for State legislatures (regional language, Hindi or English), and Art. 348 requires the authoritative texts of all Bills, Acts, Ordinances and judgments to be in English.

1️⃣7️⃣🧑‍⚖️ PRESIDING OFFICERS WITH BUILT-IN IMPARTIALITY

🏛️Lok SabhaSpeaker Art. 93 + Deputy Speaker + a Panel of up to 10 Chairpersons nominated by the Speaker + Speaker Pro Tem. Elected by the House from among its own members on a date fixed by the President (for the Speaker) and by the Speaker (for the Deputy Speaker).
🏛️Rajya SabhaChairman — ex-officio the Vice-President of India Art. 89(1), and thus not a member of the House + Deputy Chairman Art. 89(2) (a member, elected by the House) + a Panel of Vice-Chairpersons nominated by the Chairman.

🛡️ Safeguards of Independence

  • Security of tenure — removable only by a resolution passed by an effective majority after 14 days' notice.
  • Salaries and allowances are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India Art. 97 — not votable by Parliament.
  • Their conduct in regulating procedure cannot be discussed except on a substantive motion.
  • Their vast procedural powers are outside the jurisdiction of the courts.
  • They do not vote in the first instance — only a casting vote in a tie, to resolve the deadlock.
  • The Speaker ranks 7th in the Warrant of Precedence (with the CJI); the Deputy Chairman/Deputy Speaker rank 9th (approx.).
⚠️ Special rules during a removal motion Arts. 92 & 96: the Speaker/Chairman shall not preside, but the Speaker (being a member) may speak and vote in the first instance — though not a casting vote. The RS Chairman, not being a member, may speak and take part but cannot vote at all. 🎯 High-yield distinction.

1️⃣8️⃣👥 CONTINUITY THROUGH DISSOLUTION

  • 🔒 The Speaker does NOT vacate office on the dissolution of the Lok Sabha — he continues until immediately before the first meeting of the newly elected Lok Sabha. (The Deputy Speaker's office DOES end with dissolution.) 🎯
  • 🔒 The Rajya Sabha is never dissolved — legislative continuity is guaranteed; it can even approve an Emergency alone when the LS stands dissolved.
  • ⚡ Speaker Pro Tem — appointed by the President (by convention the senior-most member by years of service); the President personally administers the oath to him. His duties: to administer the oath/affirmation to the new members, to enable the House to elect the new Speaker, and to preside over the first sitting. His office ceases the moment the new Speaker is elected.
  • 📜 Bills, motions, resolutions, notices and petitions pending in the Rajya Sabha and pending assurances before the Committee on Government Assurances survive dissolution.
⚠️ Trap: A member of the Panel of Chairpersons cannot preside when the offices of both the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker are vacant — in that case the President appoints a member of the House for the purpose.

1️⃣9️⃣🎯 SUBJECT TO PARTY DISCIPLINE (Anti-Defection)

The 52nd Amendment (1985) inserted the Tenth Schedule. Grounds of disqualification:

  1. A member of a political party who voluntarily gives up his party membership (interpreted broadly — conduct, not just formal resignation).
  2. Who votes or abstains contrary to a whip without prior permission and without condonation within 15 days.
  3. An independent member who joins any political party after the election.
  4. A nominated member who joins a political party after 6 months of taking his seat. (Within 6 months he may join freely.)

Exceptions: ✅ Merger — if 2/3rds of a legislature party agree to merge (the "split" exception of 1/3rd was deleted by the 91st Amendment, 2003). ✅ A person elected as Speaker/Chairman/Deputy who voluntarily gives up party membership on election and rejoins afterwards.

Decision: by the Speaker/Chairman, acting as a tribunal; the decision is subject to judicial review — Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), which struck down Para 7 (the ouster clause) for want of State ratification while upholding the rest.

⚖️ Later jurisprudence: Ravi S. Naik (1994) — "voluntarily giving up" is wider than resignation and may be inferred from conduct. Rajendra Singh Rana (2007) — failure of the Speaker to decide is itself reviewable. Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Manipur (2020) — the Speaker should ordinarily decide within a reasonable period of about 3 months, and Parliament should consider replacing the Speaker with an independent tribunal headed by a retired judge.
📌 The Whip: The office of “Whip” finds no mention in the Constitution, in the Rules of the House, or in any parliamentary statute — it rests purely on convention. 🕊️ (The Leaders and Chief Whips of Recognised Parties and Groups in Parliament (Facilities) Act, 1998 gives facilities but does not create the office.)

2️⃣0️⃣🔢 QUORUM & MAJORITY DISCIPLINE Art. 100

  • Quorum = 1/10th of the total number of members of the House, including the Presiding Officer. → Lok Sabha 55 | Rajya Sabha 25. If there is no quorum, the presiding officer must adjourn the House or suspend the sitting.
  • The presiding officer does not vote in the first instance, but has a casting vote in the case of an equality of votes ⚖️.
  • Art. 100(2): a House may act notwithstanding any vacancy in its membership, and proceedings are valid even if it is later discovered that an unentitled person voted.

🧠 Memory ladder: Simple → Effective → Absolute → Special = increasing difficulty. Remember: you need a special majority to change the Constitution, but only a simple majority to change the government.

🧾 Types of Bills — The Four-Fold Classification

📄Ordinary BillAny matter other than financial. Either House; no President's recommendation; joint sitting possible; RS may delay up to 6 months.
💵Money BillArt. 110's six matters only. LS only, on the President's recommendation; Speaker certifies; RS 14 days; no joint sitting; President cannot return it.
🏦Financial BillCat. I — Art. 110 matters + other matters: like a Money Bill in introduction (LS only, President's recommendation) but like an Ordinary Bill thereafter. Cat. II — involves expenditure from the CFI: like an Ordinary Bill, but the President's recommendation is needed for consideration.
📜Constitutional Amendment BillArt. 368. Either House; no President's recommendation; no joint sitting; special majority (+ State ratification for federal matters); President must assent (24th Amendment, 1971).
⚠️ Also distinguish: Public Bills (introduced by a Minister; 7 days' notice; rejection = loss of confidence) vs Private Members' Bills (any non-Minister; 1 month's notice; discussed on Fridays; only 14 have ever become law, the last in 1970 — the Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Bill).

Part J · Chronology

🗓️ TIMELINE OF THE INDIAN LEGISLATURE

From a nominated council of 1861 to the delimitation battle of 2026.

1861

Indian Councils Act — Indians first associated (nominated) with law-making; the beginning of portfolio government.

1892

Indian Councils Act — indirect election (though the word "election" was avoided) and the power to discuss the budget and ask questions.

1909

Morley–Minto Reforms — first Indian in the Executive Council (Satyendra Prasad Sinha); introduced the poison of separate electorates.

1919

GoI Act (Montagu–Chelmsford) → Bicameral Central Legislature (Council of State + Legislative Assembly); direct election introduced; dyarchy in the provinces.

1921

Offices of President & Deputy President created → Frederick Whyte (1st President/Speaker, appointed by the Governor-General) & Sachidanand Sinha (1st Deputy President). Foundation stone of Parliament House laid (12 Feb).

1925

Vithalbhai J. Patel — the first elected Indian President of the Central Legislative Assembly 🇮🇳.

1927

Parliament House (Sansad Bhavan) formally opened on 18 January by Lord Irwin.

1935

GoI Act renamed the posts Speaker & Deputy Speaker — but the old names continued until 1947 (the federal part was never implemented).

1946–49

Constituent Assembly debates on Parliament; the Union Constitution Committee (Nehru) settles on the parliamentary system.

26 Jan 1950

Constitution comes into force; the Constituent Assembly becomes the Provisional Parliament until 1952.

1951–52

First General Election (Oct 1951 – Feb 1952) under Sukumar Sen; First Lok Sabha (1952–57).

1952

Rajya Sabha first constituted 3 April 1952; first sitting 13 May 1952 — the same day as the first Lok Sabha sitting. G.V. Mavalankar 1st Speaker 🏅; M. Ananthasayanam Ayyangar 1st Deputy Speaker; Dr. S. Radhakrishnan 1st Chairman of the RS.

1953

Short Duration Discussion introduced; the first Delimitation Commission (1952) completes its work.

1954

Names “Lok Sabha” (14 May) & “Rajya Sabha” (23 Aug) adopted; the Calling Attention Motion introduced; Salary & Allowances of MPs Act enacted.

1961

1st Joint Sitting — the Dowry Prohibition Bill.

1962

Zero Hour emerges as a practice — a purely Indian innovation, still unwritten in the Rules.

1963

Official Languages Act — English continues indefinitely; second Delimitation Commission.

1965

UT (Direct Election to the House of the People) Act.

1967

Convention begins that the PAC Chairman is drawn from the Opposition.

1969

Leader of the Opposition first recognised in the Lok Sabha.

1971–77

The 5th Lok Sabha's term extended twice during the Emergency — the only LS to sit beyond 5 years.

1976

42nd Amendment — froze seat allocation on the 1971 census till 2000; LS term extended 5 → 6 years; MPs' pension introduced.

1977

LoP given statutory recognition (Salary & Allowances of Leaders of Opposition Act); 2nd Joint Sitting (Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill, 16 May 1978 — passed in 1978).

1978

44th Amendment — LS term restored to 5 years; privileges reference to the House of Commons removed; safeguards against Emergency misuse. 2nd Joint Sitting held.

1985

52nd Amendment — Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule).

1988

61st Amendment — voting age lowered 21 → 18 (effective 28 March 1989).

1992

Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu — the Speaker's decision on defection is subject to judicial review; Para 7 struck down.

1993

Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) established — 17 initially, expanded to 24 in 2004. A landmark in parliamentary scrutiny.

1998

Leaders & Chief Whips (Facilities) Act. Recognised Party: 55 (LS) / 25 (RS); recognised Group: 30 (LS) / 15 (RS).

2001

84th Amendment — freeze extended to the first census after 2026.

2002

3rd Joint Sitting — the Prevention of Terrorism Bill (POTA), 26 March 2002; the fourth Delimitation Commission constituted (orders finalised 2008). NCRWC report submitted.

2003

87th Amendment — delimitation on the 2001 census within States. 91st Amendment — CoM capped at 15%; the "split" exception in the Tenth Schedule deleted. RPA Amendment — open ballot & no domicile requirement for the RS.

2014

RS Question Hour shifted 11 am → 12 noon (Chairman Hamid Ansari) so that Zero Hour issues could be raised first.

2017

Budget advanced to 1 February; the Railway Budget merged with the General Budget (Bibek Debroy Committee) — the Railway Budget had been separated in 1924 on the recommendation of the Acworth Committee (1921); Plan/Non-Plan classification abolished.

2019

104th Amendment — SC/ST reservation extended to 2030; Anglo-Indian nomination not extended (lapsed 25 Jan 2020). The Deputy Speaker's post falls vacant on 26 May 2019.

2023

New Parliament Building inaugurated (28 May); the old building renamed Samvidhan Sadan; the 106th Amendment — Women's Reservation (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam) passed in a Special Session in September.

2024

Sita Soren v. Union of India (7 judges) overrules P.V. Narasimha Rao — no immunity for bribe-taking legislators. 18th Lok Sabha constituted (June); Om Birla re-elected Speaker; Rahul Gandhi becomes Leader of the Opposition (9 June) after a 10-year vacancy.

2025

C.P. Radhakrishnan sworn in as the 15th Vice-President and ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha on 12 September 2025, following the mid-term resignation of his predecessor. NEW

2026

🔥 A landmark parliamentary year: Census 2027 operations begin (house-listing from April 2026); a no-confidence motion against Speaker Om Birla is moved by the Opposition and rejected in the Lok Sabha on 11 March 2026 — an extremely rare event that spotlighted the Deputy Speaker vacancy; the Delimitation Bills are introduced on 16 April; the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 is DEFEATED on 17 April 2026 (298 ayes v. 230 noes, short of the required ~352); Harivansh Narayan Singh re-elected Deputy Chairman of the RS for a third term, unopposed, on 17 April 2026. Part L · Exam Smart Zone

🎯 TRAPS, ANGLES & QUOTES

Where the examiner hides the knife — and how to write the answer that scores.

⚠️ Top 20 Prelims Trap-Points

  1. President is part of Parliament ✅ but not a member of either House ❌.
  2. Parliamentary privileges do NOT extend to the President — they extend to Ministers and the Attorney-General Art. 88.
  3. Max LS strength is 550 (530 + 20); the two Anglo-Indians made it 552 only before 25 Jan 2020.
  4. Max RS strength is 250, but 238 is the elected ceiling — current sanctioned strength is 245 (233 + 12).
  5. An RS member's 6-year term is fixed by the RPA 1951 — ❌ not by the Constitution.
  6. Art. 83(1) says only that 1/3rd retire every 2 years; it does not state a six-year term.
  7. No minimum sitting days are prescribed; the rule is a 6-month gap — not "three sessions a year."
  8. The President may summon Parliament at any place he thinks fit ✅.
  9. Under FPTP the winner needs only a plurality — ❌ not 50% of the votes.
  10. There is no constitutional provision that the Deputy Speaker must come from the Opposition — it is only a convention.
  11. The Speaker fixes the date of the Deputy Speaker's election; the President fixes the date of the Speaker's election.
  12. Removal of the Speaker → effective majority + 14 days' notice; during that motion the Speaker may speak and vote in the first instance but has no casting vote. The RS Chairman may speak but cannot vote at all.
  13. The Speaker does not vacate office on dissolution; the Deputy Speaker does.
  14. A member of the Panel of Chairpersons cannot preside when both the Speaker's and Deputy Speaker's offices are vacant — the President appoints a member for that purpose.
  15. A Bill pending in the RS and not yet passed by the LS does NOT lapse on dissolution.
  16. Prorogation does not kill Bills in India (unlike Britain), but pending notices lapse — except notices for the introduction of Bills.
  17. Only a Money Bill Art. 110 carries the exclusive "only" test; a Financial Bill (Category I) has Art. 110 matters plus other general legislation.
  18. Joint sitting: ❌ Money Bills, ❌ Constitutional Amendment Bills; ✅ Ordinary and Financial Bills.
  19. Zero Hour, Whip, Leader of the House, Leader of the Opposition, "Budget" — none of these words appears in the Constitution.
  20. The Quorum includes the presiding officer — LS 55, RS 25.
🧠 Bonus traps: Nominated MPs cannot vote for the President but can vote for the Vice-President · MLCs vote in neither · a nominated member may become Deputy Chairman of the RS (Harivansh) · the Estimates Committee has no RS members · the PAC Chairman is an Opposition MP only by convention (since 1967), not by law.

✍️ Mains Angles — Value-Added Points

📉Declining DeliberationSitting days down from ~120+ in the 1950s to often under 70; Bills passed amid din with minimal scrutiny; falling reference to Standing Committees (from ~60–70% in the 15th LS to under 20% in recent Houses). Remedy: a statutory minimum of 120 sitting days (NCRWC) and mandatory Committee referral.
🏛️"Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker"(UPSC 2020) The British convention — the Speaker resigns from the party and is returned unopposed. India has not adopted it. It would strengthen impartiality, especially in defection adjudication, but needs cross-party consensus and a safe-seat guarantee.
⚖️The Anti-Defection Paradox(UPSC 2013 & 2019) Meant to curb horse-trading, the Tenth Schedule has silenced the individual MP, converting Parliament into a chamber of parties rather than of persons. Reform: confine the whip to confidence motions, Money Bills and manifesto commitments (Dinesh Goswami, Law Commission 170th Report, NCRWC).
💵The Money Bill RouteCertification of Bills as Money Bills to bypass the Rajya Sabha (Aadhaar Act, Finance Act 2017 tribunal provisions, PMLA amendments). Rojer Mathew (2019) referred the correctness of Puttaswamy's Aadhaar holding to a larger bench — still pending. Raises the question whether the Speaker's certificate is truly beyond review.
🕰️The Deputy Speaker VacancyArt. 93's "shall… as soon as may be" is arguably mandatory. A vacancy since 2019 leaves no constitutional fallback if the Speaker's office falls vacant — an issue thrown into sharp relief by the 2026 motion against the Speaker.
🗺️Delimitation: the Federal FlashpointReconciling democratic equality (one person, one vote, one value) with federal fairness to States that succeeded in population control. Options: expand the House while freezing State shares; equalise Rajya Sabha representation; devolve fiscal and legislative power; or a phased, negotiated re-proportioning.
👩Women's RepresentationThe 106th Amendment is passed but dormant. India's LS has ~14% women against a global average near 27%. Debate: rotation of reserved seats weakens incumbency and constituency-nurturing; the alternative is intra-party quotas for candidature.
🔧The Reform BasketMinimum sitting days · an independent tribunal for defection cases (Keisham Meghachandra, 2020) · mandatory Committee referral · pre-legislative consultation policy · legislative impact assessment · a shadow-cabinet-style Opposition · codification of privileges · a Parliamentary Budget Office.
