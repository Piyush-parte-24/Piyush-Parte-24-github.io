🧬 The Design Philosophy — Why this shape?

🎩 Westminster Borrowing

India adopted the British parliamentary (Cabinet) system — an executive drawn from and answerable to the legislature — but grafted it onto a Republican head of State (elected, not hereditary) instead of a monarch. Hence: “A crowned republic without a crown.”

⚖️ Nominal vs Real

The Constitution deliberately splits de jure authority (President, Art 53) from de facto authority (PM + CoM, Art 74–75). This is the “dignified” vs “efficient” distinction of Walter Bagehot.

🔗 Fusion of Powers

Unlike the USA’s separation of powers, India has a fusion: Ministers must be MPs (Art 75(5)), the President is part of Parliament (Art 79), and the executive lives only while it holds the Lok Sabha’s confidence (Art 75(3)).

🌍 The Constituent Assembly Choice — Why not a US-style President?

💡 The Ambedkar formulation: The Assembly weighed stability (US presidential model — fixed term, strong executive) against responsibility (British model — daily answerability). Ambedkar declared that India needed a government “more responsible than stable,” because a nascent democracy must be continuously accountable. Hence the parliamentary system was chosen for both the Union and the States.
🧠 Remember: USA = Stability (fixed 4-yr term, no daily accountability). India/UK = Responsibility (daily Question Hour, no-confidence motions, but risk of instability). Ambedkar: “The daily assessment of responsibility is far more effective than the periodic assessment.”

💡 Add-on you must know — Art 73 (Extent of Union executive power): The Union’s executive power is co-extensive with Parliament’s legislative power — it extends to all matters in the Union List, to the exercise of rights/authority under any treaty or agreement, and (with State consent or where a law of Parliament expressly provides) to Concurrent List matters. 📝 Nomination formalities — Presidential & Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952

  • 50 proposers + 50 seconders — raised from 10+10 by amendment in 1997 to curb frivolous candidatures (in 1957 there were 3 candidates; by 1974 candidate numbers had ballooned).
  • Security deposit ₹15,000 — forfeited if the candidate fails to secure 1/6th of the votes required for return (the quota).
  • Returning Officer: Secretary-General of the Lok Sabha / Rajya Sabha, by rotation.
  • Conducting authority: Election Commission of India — Art 324(1) expressly gives the ECI superintendence of Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections.
  • An elector may propose/second only one candidate; violating this invalidates the later subscription.
⚠️ Trap: A person who is already an MP or MLA can contest for President. If elected, his seat is deemed vacated on the date he enters upon office (Art 59(1)) — he is not required to resign beforehand. Example: V.V. Giri resigned as Vice-President in 1969 only because a VP contesting would otherwise conflict with acting as President; and Neelam Sanjiva Reddy resigned as Speaker in 1977 to contest.

⚠️ CRITICAL CONTRAST: Nominated members CAN vote in the Vice-Presidential election and CAN participate in the President’s impeachment — but CANNOT vote in the Presidential election. Reason: the Presidential election is meant to reflect the “will of the people” expressed through directly-derived mandates; impeachment, by contrast, is a purely parliamentary function.
💡 Why an indirect election? Three reasons debated in the Constituent Assembly: (i) direct election would give the President a popular mandate rivalling the PM’s, destabilising the parliamentary scheme; (ii) an all-India direct poll would be enormously costly and disruptive; (iii) indirect election through State legislatures gives the office a federal character. Ambedkar: the President is elected “indirectly by a system of proportional representation” precisely to keep him a figurehead.
⚖️ Federal balance in the formula: Art 55(2) ensures (a) uniformity in the scale of representation among States (a citizen in Bihar and a citizen in Kerala count equally) and (b) parity between the Union and the States as a whole (total MLA votes ≈ total MP votes). This makes the President the head of the federation, not merely of the Centre. 🤝

🔍 Key facts & insights

  • Population = 1971 Census figures, frozen by the 42nd Amendment (1976), extended by the 84th Amendment Act, 2001 until “the first census taken after the year 2026” is published. The freeze rewards States that performed on population control (otherwise UP/Bihar would gain and Kerala/TN would lose weight). 📉
  • ⚠️ It is called “Proportional Representation by Single Transferable Vote” even though only ONE seat is filled — strictly this is the Alternative Vote / Instant-Runoff. Constitutional purists call it a drafting anomaly, but the constitutional text governs.
  • Other low values: Arunachal Pradesh (8), Mizoram (8), Nagaland (9). Other high values: Jharkhand & Tamil Nadu (176), Maharashtra (175), Bihar (173).
  • 2022 Election: MP vote value fell from 708 → 700 because the J&K Assembly was not in existence. Droupadi Murmu polled ≈64.03% of the value of valid votes.
  • Secret ballot is mandatory → the Anti-Defection Law (10th Schedule) does NOT apply; no party whip can be issued. Cross-voting is legal and common. 🤫
  • Voting is by marking preferences in figures (1, 2, 3…) only — marking a “✓” or writing in words invalidates the ballot. Marking the 1st preference is compulsory; the rest are optional.
  • MPs vote with a green ballot paper, MLAs with a pink one. Polling is normally at Parliament House and State Assembly premises; an elector may seek prior permission to vote elsewhere.
🧮 Worked numerical example (exam-style)

Q. A State has a 1971 population of 5,00,00,000 and 250 elected MLAs. Find the value of one MLA’s vote.

A. 5,00,00,000 ÷ 250 = 2,00,000 → ÷ 1000 = 200. So each MLA’s vote = 200. Total State value = 250 × 200 = 50,000.

Q. If the total value of all MLAs’ votes in India is 5,49,495 and the elected MPs number 776, find the MP vote value.

A. 5,49,495 ÷ 776 = 708.1… → rounded to 708 (the 2017 figure). In 2022, with J&K’s assembly dissolved the numerator fell, giving 700.

⚖️ 1.5 Article 61 — Impeachment: The Only Way Out

   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  GROUND: "VIOLATION OF THE CONSTITUTION" — deliberately UNDEFINED    │
   └───────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   ▼
   ①  CHARGE preferred by EITHER House (LS or RS — no priority)
       • Resolution signed by ≥ 1/4th of the TOTAL members of that House
       • 14 days' written NOTICE given to the President
                                   ▼
   ②  Resolution PASSED by ≥ 2/3rd of the TOTAL MEMBERSHIP of that House
       (Special Majority — not "members present and voting")
                                   ▼
   ③  The OTHER House INVESTIGATES the charge
       • President has the RIGHT to appear & be REPRESENTED (by counsel/AG)
                                   ▼
   ④  Investigating House passes resolution by ≥ 2/3rd of TOTAL MEMBERSHIP
                                   ▼
   ⑤  ✅ PRESIDENT REMOVED from the DATE the resolution is passed
🔢 “1/4 → 14 → 2/3 → 2/3” — One-fourth sign, fourteen days wait, two-thirds twice, and out the gate!

💡 Key insights

  • It is a quasi-judicial procedure — the only process in the Constitution where the President is effectively a party with a right to defend himself.
  • NOMINATED members participate (unlike the election) — impeachment is a parliamentary, not electoral, function.
  • ELECTED MLAs do NOT participate — even though they elected him. ⚠️ A famous constitutional asymmetry: those who elect cannot remove.
  • No President of India has ever been impeached. 🕊️
  • A President elected to fill a casual vacancy gets a full fresh 5-year term, not the residue of the predecessor’s term — unlike US succession.

⚖️ Why “violation of the Constitution” is undefined

The Constituent Assembly deliberately left the phrase open so that Parliament, not a court, determines its content politically. Since the President acts on ministerial advice, a genuine “violation” would arise mainly where he acts against or without advice, or refuses to discharge a mandatory duty.

Practical reality: because a 2/3rd special majority of the total membership of both Houses is required, and the President is elected by the same political configuration that would need to remove him, impeachment is a theoretical safeguard. 🔒

🧮 Numerically: With LS = 543 and RS = 245, a resolution needs roughly 362 votes in the LS and 164 in the RS — regardless of absentees.
⚔️ Contrast — impeachment of the President vs removal of a Judge: A Judge of the SC/HC is removed under Art 124(4) for “proved misbehaviour or incapacity”, on an address by both Houses passed by a majority of total membership and 2/3rd of those present and voting, following an inquiry under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968. The President is removed under Art 61 for “violation of the Constitution” by 2/3rd of the total membership — a stricter numerical threshold and a looser substantive ground.

📌 Historical facts (high-frequency exam material)

1969 ⚫ — Dr. Zakir Husain died in office: the first President to die in office. VP V.V. Giri became Acting President; he then resigned to contest, so Justice M. Hidayatullah (CJI) acted — the only CJI ever to act as President. 🏛️
1977 ⚫ — Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed died in office; VP B.D. Jatti acted as President.
1977 🏵️ — Neelam Sanjiva Reddy: the only President elected unopposed; also the only person to lose a presidential election (1969) and later win one.
1950–62 🏅 — Dr. Rajendra Prasad: the only President to serve two full terms.
2022 🌟 — Smt. Droupadi Murmu, 15th President, sworn in 25 July 2022: first tribal President, second woman, youngest ever, first born in independent India. (Term to July 2027 — verify current status.)
💡 No “interregnum” doctrine: The proviso to Art 56(1)(c) guarantees that the incumbent continues to hold office until his successor enters upon office. Read with Art 62 and Art 65, India can never be without someone discharging the President’s functions — a deliberate design against a constitutional vacuum. 🔗
⚠️ Never confuse: The Vice-President does NOT automatically become President on a vacancy (unlike the US VP). He only acts, for a maximum of 6 months, within which a fresh election must be held. The Indian VP is not a “President-in-waiting.”

🅰️ Executive Powers

  • All executive action of the Government of India is expressed to be taken in the name of the President (Art 77(1)).
  • Appoints: Prime Minister & other Ministers (75), Attorney General (76), CAG (148), Governors (155), Judges of the SC & HCs (124, 217), Chief Election Commissioner & ECs (324 r/w the CEC and Other ECs (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023 — on the recommendation of a Selection Committee of the PM, a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM, and the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha), Chairman/Members of the UPSC (316), Finance Commission (280), Administrators of UTs (239), Chairpersons of NCSC/NCST/NCBC (338, 338A, 338B), Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (350B), Inter-State Council (263), Ambassadors & High Commissioners.
  • Can seek information from the PM about the administration of the Union (Art 78).
  • Can appoint a Commission to investigate the conditions of SC/ST/OBC/backward classes (Art 340 — e.g., Kaka Kalelkar 1953, Mandal 1979).
  • Directly administers Union Territories through Administrators (239) and can declare any area a Scheduled Area (5th Schedule) with powers of administration over Scheduled/Tribal Areas.
  • Can appoint inter-State river water dispute tribunals (262 r/w statute) and set up the Inter-State Council (263).

🅲 Financial Powers

  • A Money Bill can be introduced only with his prior recommendation (Art 117(1)) and only in the Lok Sabha (Art 109).
  • Causes the Annual Financial Statement (Budget) to be laid before both Houses (Art 112).
  • No demand for a grant can be made except on his recommendation (Art 113(3)).
  • Contingency Fund of India is at his disposal — he makes advances for unforeseen expenditure, later regularised by Parliament (Art 267) 💰.
  • Constitutes a Finance Commission every 5 years (or earlier) to recommend the distribution of taxes between the Union and the States (Art 280).
  • Can make rules for Consolidated Fund custody (283) and decides on the charged expenditure laid before Parliament (112(3)).

🅳 Judicial Powers

  • Appoints the CJI and Judges of the SC/HCs — bound in practice by the Collegium after the Second Judges Case (1993) and the Third Judges Case (1998); the NJAC (99th Amendment, 2014) was struck down in Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India (2015) as violating judicial independence, a basic-structure feature. ⚖️
  • Art 143 — Advisory Jurisdiction: may refer (a) a question of law or fact of public importance that has arisen or is likely to arise — the SC may decline to answer; or (b) a dispute over a pre-Constitution treaty/agreement — here the SC must report. The opinion is advisory, not binding, and is not “law declared” under Art 141 in the strict sense, though it carries the highest persuasive value.
  • Art 72 — Pardoning power (see below).
  • Enjoys immunity from judicial process under Art 361.

🎖️ Military & 🕊️ Diplomatic Powers

⚔️ Military

Supreme Commander of the Defence Forces (Art 53(2)). Appoints the Chiefs of the Army, Navy and Air Force. Declares war or concludes peace — but subject to the law made by Parliament and, in reality, on the Cabinet’s advice. Confers military honours (Param Vir Chakra etc.).

🌐 Diplomatic

International treaties and agreements are negotiated and concluded in his name — subject to ratification by Parliament. He represents India in international fora, sends and receives Ambassadors, High Commissioners and diplomatic envoys, and accredits foreign envoys.

Remission ✂️Reduces the QUANTUM/period of the sentence — the character of the sentence remains the same (e.g., 2 yrs RI → 1 yr RI)
Respite ⏸️Awards a LESSER sentence in place of the original due to SPECIAL FACTS — e.g., pregnancy of a woman convict, physical disability, advanced age
Reprieve ⏳A TEMPORARY stay of execution of a sentence (especially death) — to let the convict seek pardon or commutation

📌 Scope under Art 72 — the three heads

  1. Punishment/sentence for an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the Union’s executive power extends (Union-law offences).
  2. Punishment/sentence by a COURT-MARTIAL (military tribunal).
  3. ALL cases where the sentence is a SENTENCE OF DEATH — irrespective of whether the law is Union or State.

Rationale (as stated by the SC): (a) to keep the door open for correcting judicial error — no human system is infallible; and (b) to allow relief where the punishment appears unduly harsh on humanitarian grounds. It is an act of grace, not a right of the convict.

⚔️ President (Art 72) vs Governor (Art 161) — The Three Differences

💡 Also distinguish — statutory remission: Sections on remission/commutation in the criminal procedure code (now the BNSS, 2023, replacing the CrPC, 1973) confer a statutory power on the appropriate Government. The constitutional power under Arts 72/161 is wider, unfettered by statutory conditions, and cannot be curtailed by legislation — Maru Ram and Kehar Singh both affirm this.

🚫 Limits & character

  • Cannot amend the Constitution — Art 368 requires an Act of Parliament with special majority.
  • Subject to the same constitutional limitations as an Act — an ordinance violating Fundamental Rights is void.
  • May be retrospective; may amend/repeal an Act of Parliament or another ordinance; may amend a tax law.
  • Issued only on the advice of the Council of Ministers — never a personal exercise.
  • It is co-extensive but not co-equal with Parliament’s legislative power: same subject-matter reach, but a subordinate, temporary and conditional instrument.
  • Unique to India — no such power exists with the British Crown or the US President; it descends from the Government of India Act, 1935 (s.42).

⚖️ Landmark cases

  • R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970, Bank Nationalisation): the President’s “satisfaction” is justiciable on grounds of mala fide — overturning the earlier view that it was wholly immune. (The 38th Amendment, 1975 tried to make it non-justiciable; the 44th Amendment, 1978 deleted that protection.)
  • A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982): upheld the ordinance power, but observed that judicial review is available where satisfaction is based on wholly extraneous grounds.
  • D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987): successive re-promulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature is a “fraud on the Constitution” and a subversion of the democratic process. (Bihar had re-promulgated 256 ordinances between 1967–81, some kept alive for 14 years.)
  • Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017, 7-judge): laying before Parliament is mandatory; re-promulgation is unconstitutional; an ordinance that lapses does not create enduring rights — only “irreversible consequences” and compelling public-interest situations are protected. The theory that a lapsed ordinance has permanent effects (from State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose) stands substantially diluted.
💡 Ordinance vs Governor’s ordinance (Art 213) — the difference: A Governor cannot issue an ordinance without the President’s instructions in three cases: (i) if a Bill on the same subject would have required the President’s previous sanction for introduction; (ii) if the Governor would have reserved such a Bill for the President’s consideration; (iii) if an Act of the State Legislature on the same subject would have been invalid without the President’s assent. The President has no such fetter. 🎗️
⚠️ “Ordinance Raj” critique: Ordinances are meant for emergencies, but have been used to bypass a hostile Rajya Sabha or an inconvenient session. Reformers suggest: a constitutional bar on re-promulgation, a requirement of reasons in writing, and compulsory parliamentary vote within a fixed period. (Note: an ordinance is “laid” before Parliament; it need not be affirmatively voted upon — silence is enough to let it lapse.)

🧠 1.13 The Great Debate: Is the President a Rubber Stamp?

🎭 ARGUMENT: NOMINAL HEAD

  • Art 74(1) — “shall act in accordance with” ministerial advice (42nd Amendment, 1976).
  • Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974, 7-judge) — the President is a formal/constitutional head; his “satisfaction” means the satisfaction of the Council of Ministers. The Indian system is a “Cabinet system,” not a presidential one.
  • Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955) — executive power is really exercised by the Council of Ministers; the President is the formal repository.
  • U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971) — Art 74(1) is mandatory; there must ALWAYS be a Council of Ministers, even after the Lok Sabha is dissolved.
  • Dr. Ambedkar: “He is the head of the State but not of the Executive. He represents the Nation but does not rule the Nation. He is the symbol of the Nation.”
  • Nehru: “We did not give him any real power, but we have made his position one of great authority and dignity.”
⚔️
VS

💪 ARGUMENT: REAL AUTHORITY

  • 44th Amendment (1978) proviso — can require reconsideration ONCE; and there is no time limit for doing so.
  • Art 78 — right to be informed, to seek information, and to require a Minister’s solitary decision to be placed before the full Council.
  • Situational discretion: hung Parliament (choice of PM), dismissal of a CoM that has demonstrably lost confidence, refusal of dissolution to a “spent” PM, and the timing of assent.
  • Veto powers (absolute, suspensive, pocket) — Zail Singh 1986, Kalam 2006.
  • K.R. Narayanan returned Union Cabinet advice for President’s Rule in UP (1997) and Bihar (1998) — the government backed down both times. 🔥
  • The 2025 advisory opinion re-affirmed that no court may impose timelines on the President’s assent function — a de facto expansion of his discretionary space.
🎯 THE BALANCED VERDICT: The President is a constitutional head with no discretionary policy power but substantial situational and moral authority — best captured by Bagehot’s three rights of a constitutional monarch: the right to be consulted, the right to encourage, and the right to warn. Post-1990s coalition politics vastly expanded the practical significance of the office; the return of single-party majorities after 2014 has again narrowed it. The office is therefore elastic — it expands in proportion to political fragmentation. 🎈

🧑‍💼 Part 3 — The Prime Minister

🏛️ 3.1 The Constitutional Foundation

📜 Article 75(1): “The Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President and the other Ministers shall be appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.”

That is all the Constitution says about the appointment of the PM. There is no prescribed qualification, no prescribed procedure, no mention of “majority.” Everything else is CONVENTION — the single most important insight about this office. 🔑

🏛️ Conventions surrounding the PM (the ones examiners love)

  1. The PM is normally a member of the LOK SABHA — but this is NOT a constitutional requirement. Rajya Sabha PMs: Indira Gandhi (1966, briefly), H.D. Deve Gowda (1996), I.K. Gujral (1997), and Dr. Manmohan Singh (2004–2014, the longest-serving RS Prime Minister).
  2. The PM must resign the moment he loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha — and his resignation automatically dissolves the entire Council of Ministers; individual Ministers cannot survive him.
  3. Only the PM can advise the President to dissolve the Lok Sabha. (A defeated PM’s right to advise dissolution is contested — precedent suggests the President may refuse and first explore alternative governments; e.g., 1979, 1990, 1997.)
  4. A caretaker PM must not take major policy decisions or make major appointments.
  5. “Deputy Prime Minister” is an extra-constitutional post — the holder takes the oath as a Minister, not as Deputy PM. (Precedents: Sardar Patel, Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, Jagjivan Ram, Y.B. Chavan, Devi Lal, L.K. Advani.) In K.M. Sharma v. Devi Lal (1990) the SC upheld Devi Lal’s oath, treating “Deputy PM” as merely descriptive, since the oath’s substance was that of a Minister.
  6. Cabinet decisions are collective and secret — no Minister may publicly dissent while remaining in office.
  7. The PM chairs the Cabinet, sets its agenda, and by convention the Cabinet Secretary is its custodian of record.

⚙️ 3.3 Article 78 — The PM’s Three Duties to the President

ClauseDuty

💡 INSIGHT: Art 78 is the umbilical cord between the nominal and the real executive. ⚠️ Zail Singh’s grievance (1986–87): President Giani Zail Singh publicly complained that PM Rajiv Gandhi was not fulfilling Art 78 — he wrote formally seeking information (e.g., on the Bofors matter and the Sri Lanka accord) and received little. This episode is the classic illustration that Art 78 has no enforcement machinery.

⚖️ No legal responsibility

Unlike Britain, India has no provision for the counter-signature of a Minister on the President’s orders. Therefore a Minister bears no legal responsibility for the President’s acts, and courts cannot compel production of the record of ministerial advice (Art 74(2)). Accountability is political, not legal.

🧾 The two limbs distinguished

Collective responsibility (Art 75(3)) → the Council stands or falls together before the Lok Sabha. Individual responsibility (Art 75(2)) → a single Minister may be dropped at the PM’s instance without the government falling. The Indian Constitution expressly recognises only collective responsibility; individual responsibility flows from the “pleasure” doctrine and convention.

⚖️ Case law on collective responsibility: Common Cause v. Union of India (1996) — Ministers are accountable for discretionary allotments; State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) — Cabinet responsibility to the legislature is a basic feature of the parliamentary system; Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) — the 10th Schedule upheld, reinforcing party discipline that underpins collective responsibility; R.K. Jain v. Union of India (1993) — the Court will not sit in judgment over policy choices made collectively by the Cabinet.

🍳 “Kitchen Cabinet” / Inner Cabinet

An extra-constitutional, informal body of the PM’s 2–4 most trusted colleagues, and sometimes friends and advisers from outside government. Merits: small, efficient, quick decisions, absolute secrecy. Demerits: reduces the Cabinet’s authority, bypasses statutory decision-making, allows non-accountable outsiders to influence policy. Historically prominent under Nehru and Indira Gandhi.

🗂️ Cabinet Committees

Extra-constitutional but set up under the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, by the Prime Minister, who decides their number, composition and functions. Standing committees include: Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS), Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA), Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC), and committees on Political Affairs, Parliamentary Affairs, Accommodation, Investment & Growth, and Employment & Skill Development. The PM chairs the most important ones; the Home Minister typically chairs others in the PM’s absence. Ad hoc Groups of Ministers (GoMs) are also constituted.

🧾 Part 6 — Conventions of the Union Government (Master List)

📖 DEFINITION: Constitutional conventions are non-legal, unwritten rules of political practice considered binding by those who operate the Constitution. They are not enforceable by courts (though courts may recognise them — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn., 1993). Dicey called them “the conventions of the Constitution,” distinguishing them from “the law of the Constitution.” Jennings’ three-part test: (i) Is there a precedent? (ii) Did the actors believe they were bound by a rule? (iii) Is there a good constitutional reason for the rule?
🧠 “A-C-R-D-S” → Appointment • Collective functioning • Resignation • Dissolution • Secrecy

🅰️ Conventions of Appointment

  1. The President appoints the leader of the majority party/coalition in the Lok Sabha as PM.
  2. In a hung Parliament, the President invites in the order of the four-step preference (pre-poll alliance → single largest party → post-poll coalition inside → post-poll coalition with outside support), followed by a floor test.
  3. Ministers are appointed only on the PM’s recommendation; the President does not question the list.
  4. The Finance Minister and Home Minister are ordinarily in the Cabinet and, by strong practice, drawn from the Lok Sabha where possible.
  5. The CJI-designate is the senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court (the seniority convention — departed from in the 1973 and 1977 supersessions, restored thereafter).
  6. Governors are appointed after consulting the Chief Minister of the State concerned (a Sarkaria Commission recommendation, honoured more in the breach).

🅱️ Conventions of Collective Functioning

  1. Cabinet unanimity — Ministers speak with one voice in public.
  2. Cabinet proceedings are secret; minutes are not published.
  3. Major policy is settled in Cabinet, not by individual Ministers acting alone.
  4. The President’s Address (Art 87) is drafted by the government and states its policy — the President reads it out whatever his personal view.

🅲 Conventions of Resignation & Responsibility

  1. A PM who loses a no-confidence motion resigns immediately with the whole Council.
  2. A Minister whose department is guilty of a serious lapse resigns — individual ministerial responsibility (e.g., Lal Bahadur Shastri’s 1956 resignation after the Ariyalur rail disaster — the gold standard, though rarely followed since).
  3. A Minister who publicly disagrees with Cabinet policy must resign first, criticise later.
  4. The outgoing Council continues as a caretaker until the successor takes charge, avoiding major policy decisions.

🅳 Conventions of Parliament & Dissolution

  1. Dissolution of the Lok Sabha is advised only by the PM; but the President is not obliged to accept advice from a PM who has already lost the House’s confidence.
  2. Money Bills originate only in the Lok Sabha (this is constitutional, Art 109 — but the practice of respecting the Speaker’s Money Bill certificate is a convention, now qualified by Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019), which referred the scope of judicial review over such certification to a 7-judge bench).
  3. Question Hour, Zero Hour and Calling Attention — Zero Hour is a purely Indian innovation, unmentioned in the Constitution and in the Rules; Calling Attention is an Indian innovation mentioned in the Rules of Procedure.
  4. The Public Accounts Committee is chaired by a member of the Opposition — a convention since 1967.
  5. The Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha is, by convention, offered to the Opposition; the Speaker, once elected, severs active party ties.

🅴 Conventions surrounding the President

  1. The President does not publicly criticise the government or express partisan views.
  2. The President severs formal party ties on assuming office.
  3. The President’s discretion is exercised sparingly and always with a written record of reasons.
  4. The President may return advice once (Art 74 proviso) — but by convention does so only in exceptional cases (K.R. Narayanan, 1997 & 1998).
  5. By practice, the President is consulted on major foreign-policy and national-security decisions even where no constitutional duty exists.
💡 Why conventions matter more in India than the text suggests: The Constitution is the lengthiest written constitution in the world, yet the entire working of the executive — Cabinet, Kitchen Cabinet, PMO, coalition-formation, caretaker governments, floor tests, portfolio allocation — rests on convention. Ivor Jennings’ formulation applies squarely: “The Constitution provides the flesh and bones; the conventions provide the blood which makes it live.” 🩸
⚠️ Weakness of conventions: They are self-enforcing only so long as political actors value institutional reputation. India has seen conventions break repeatedly — supersession of judges (1973, 1977), misuse of Art 356 before Bommai, Money Bill route for non-money legislation, and long vacancies in the Deputy Speaker’s office. This is why the judiciary has increasingly converted conventions into enforceable constitutional principles (e.g., the floor test).

⚡ Part 9 — Current & Emerging Issues (as of mid-2026)

1️⃣ The Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025 — the “30-Day Removal Bill”

Introduced in the Lok Sabha on 20 August 2025 by the Ministry of Home Affairs and referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee (Chair: Aparajita Sarangi). It seeks to amend Article 75 (Union), Article 164 (States) and Article 239AA (Delhi), with companion Bills for Puducherry and Jammu & Kashmir.

The proposal: a Minister accused of an offence punishable with five years or more who is arrested and detained for 30 consecutive days may be removed by the President/Governor on the PM’s/CM’s advice — or is automatically removed on the 31st day. A Prime Minister or Chief Minister must resign by the 31st day, failing which he automatically ceases to hold office. A removed Minister may be re-appointed upon release.

Critics (including PRS Legislative Research) argue it may violate four basic-structure features: the parliamentary form of democracy (only the Lok Sabha may unseat a PM through loss of confidence), separation of powers (investigative agencies under executive control could topple elected governments), federalism (one level of government influencing the removal of another), and the rule of law (removal on mere accusation and custody, before any judicial finding even on framing of charges — offending the presumption of innocence).

Supporters cite the criminalisation of politics, the difficulty of securing convictions against powerful politicians, ADR data on MPs with declared criminal cases, and the moral untenability of governance from custody.

⚠️ Status: As of my information, the JPC was finalising its report around mid-2026, reportedly retaining the 30-day clause. This is still a Bill, not law — verify its current status on sansad.in / PRS, as it may well have moved since.

2️⃣ The 16th Presidential Reference (Nov 2025) — the assent question settled (for now)

Discussed in §1.9. Its significance for the Union Executive is threefold: it has meaningfully re-expanded the discretionary space of the President and Governors under Arts 111, 200 and 201; it has restricted the judiciary’s use of Art 142 to fashion remedies that displace express constitutional text; and it has reaffirmed the advisory jurisdiction (Art 143) as a live instrument of constitutional dialogue between the executive and the judiciary. It is the most consequential constitutional development on the Union/State executive in years. ⚖️

3️⃣ Ongoing structural debates 🧩

  • Direct vs indirect election of the President — periodically revived, consistently rejected as incompatible with parliamentary government.
  • Whether the President’s role in coalition formation should be codified by statute (the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions recommended written guidelines; nothing binding exists).
  • The absence of any time limit on Presidential assent (Art 111) — a gap the 2025 opinion has now largely constitutionalised as discretionary.
  • The AG’s freedom to hold private practice — a conflict-of-interest debate raised repeatedly by the Law Commission.
  • Constitutional silence on qualifications and criminal antecedents of Ministers (Manoj Narula) — the 130th Amendment Bill is the political answer to a judicial abstention.
  • Post-2026 delimitation — once the first census after 2026 is published, the 1971 freeze ends, which will re-weight the Presidential Electoral College and reopen North–South federal tensions. 🗺️
  • The growth of the PMO and the decline of full-Cabinet deliberation — Crossman’s “Prime Ministerial Government” thesis applied to India.
  • Ordinance frequency and the demand for a constitutional bar on re-promulgation beyond the Krishna Kumar Singh judicial rule.

🧭 The One-Line Essence

🇮🇳 The Indian Constitution vests de jure executive power in the President (Art 53) but transfers de facto power to the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers (Art 74–75), binding them to the Lok Sabha through collective responsibility (Art 75(3)) — a design Ambedkar summed up as: the President “represents the Nation but does not rule the Nation.”

Office-holders (President, Vice-President, Attorney General) should likewise be verified before an exam.
