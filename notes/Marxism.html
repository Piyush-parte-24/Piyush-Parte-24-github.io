🧭 Editorial precision · How to read this atlas

A theory in debate. Not a single fixed formula.

The following notes retain the structure and substantial teaching content of the uploaded Marxism file. Where a familiar textbook shorthand risks becoming misleading, read it as an interpretation or mnemonic—not as a verbatim statement by Marx or an uncontested law of history.

⚖️

Method, not slogan

Marx’s analysis combines material conditions, social relations, historical change and political struggle. Avoid reducing it to “economics alone mechanically determines everything.”

📚

Distinguish authorship

Marx and Engels developed a shared intellectual project, but later Marxists—including Lenin, Luxemburg, Gramsci and Althusser—added distinct arguments. Do not attribute every later doctrine directly to Marx.

🧩

Beware of neat formulas

“Thesis–antithesis–synthesis” is a popular teaching shorthand, not a universal formula Marx consistently used to explain dialectics.

🕰️

History is contested

Stage models of historical development are interpretive reconstructions; historical societies do not all follow one identical, inevitable sequence.

Accuracy note: Marxism is a family of theories and political traditions. Separate Marx’s own texts, Engels’s contributions, later interpretations, historical regimes and contemporary applications. A criticism of one regime is not automatically a complete refutation of every Marxist argument; equally, invoking “true Marxism” does not settle historical criticism.

🔬 Conceptual precision: five distinctions worth mastering

Core Marxist concepts — concise, exam-safe meanings
ConceptPrecise distinctionCommon oversimplification to avoid
Mode of productionA historically specific unity of productive forces and relations of production; it is broader than a technology or a method of making goods.Reducing it to machines, factories or “the economy” alone.
Labour and labour-powerLabour is the activity; labour-power is the worker’s capacity to labour, which becomes a commodity under capitalism.Treating the two terms as interchangeable. This distinction is central to Marx’s account of surplus value.
Surplus value and profitSurplus value is the value produced by workers beyond the value represented by the value of their labour-power; profit is a transformed, competitive form in which surplus value appears.Equating surplus value with the entire sale price, or treating it as simply “unpaid wages” without explaining the labour-power distinction.

How to write a stronger exam answer: define the concept, identify the text or thinker to whom the argument belongs, explain its mechanism, then add a qualification or criticism. This prevents a slogan from replacing analysis.

🧠 PART 1: Foundational Layer — The DNA of Marxism

🔷 1.1 What is Marxism? (Precise Definition)

Marxism is a broad intellectual and political tradition associated with Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. It analyses capitalist production, class relations, exploitation and historical change, and explores the possibility of a society beyond class domination.

In Marx’s framework, communism is the envisaged classless form of social organisation beyond capitalism. “Marxism” is not simply the literal phrase “the doctrine of Marx”, nor is every later Marxist theory identical to Marx’s own writings.

AspectPrecise description
DefinitionA critical analysis of capitalist society, class relations and historical transformation.
NaturePhilosophical, economic, historical and political arguments developed by Marx, Engels and later thinkers.
Core questionHow are social relations, production, power and inequality historically organised—and how might they change?
MethodHistorical and materialist analysis; “dialectical materialism” is a later systematised label in Marxist traditions.

FactorReality of 1840s–1860s

Exploitation is not theft (illegal taking) — it is systemic extraction built into the wage-labor relationship itself. The worker agrees to be exploited because the alternative is starvation. The contract appears free; the underlying compulsion is invisible.

🔴 2.3 Alienation — The Psychological Damage

Alienation is the process by which workers become severed from their human essence (Gattungswesen — species-being) under capitalism.

🔗 The 4 Types of Alienation

TYPE 1

🏭 From the Product

The worker doesn't own what they make. The assembly-line worker never sees the final car they helped build; it belongs to someone else from the moment it exists.

TYPE 2

⚙️ From the Process

The worker has no control over how work is done. Told exactly when to press the button, when to take breaks, when to speak.

TYPE 3

👥 From Fellow Workers

Competition replaces cooperation. Co-workers fight for shifts, promotions, scraps — turning natural allies into rivals.

TYPE 4

🧠 From Self / Human Potential

Work is forced, not creative. "My job doesn't use my brain or passion." The worker becomes less than human at work.

🔄 PART 4: Dialectical Materialism — The Method

🔷 4.1 Definition

Dialectical Materialism: In the later Engelsian and Marxist tradition, dialectical materialism describes material reality as changing through processes, relations and contradictions. It should not be presented as a single formula that Marx and Engels jointly codified in this exact form. E.g., heat water gradually → it suddenly boils into steam (a qualitative jump from a quantitative buildup)

Historical Materialism: Historical materialism analyses how material production, social relations and class conflict shape historical change. It does not require every society to pass through one identical sequence of stages. E.g., growing tension between feudal lords and serfs, plus the rise of trade, eventually broke feudalism and gave birth to capitalism.

One-line difference: A common textbook distinction is that dialectical materialism concerns a general materialist-dialectical outlook, while historical materialism applies materialist analysis to social history. The terminology and its systematisation developed over time, especially after Marx.

In short: the application of Hegelian dialectic to materialist philosophy. Reality is understood through the interaction of opposing forces, with contradictions driving change.

📊 Hegel vs Marx — The Critical Inversion

AspectHegel (German idealist philosopher)Marx (historical and dialectical materialist orientation)
Subject of changeSpirit, concepts and social-historical formsMaterial social relations and historical practice
Driving forceDialectical development of concepts and SpiritContradictions within material and social relations
DirectionDevelopment of Spirit and forms of knowledgeCritique of capitalism and possible communist transformation

Marx famously said he found Hegel "standing on his head" and put him "back on his feet." Marx’s 1873 afterword describes his dialectical method as opposed to Hegel’s mystified form; their relationship is more complex than a simple flip from ideas to economics.

🔷 4.2 Three dialectical themes often systematised in Engelsian and later Marxist teaching

📐 DIALECTICAL THEME 1: Quantitative and Qualitative Change

Gradual quantitative changes accumulate until they produce a sudden qualitative leap.

🌡️ PhysicsAt standard atmospheric pressure, water boils near 100°C; the example illustrates a phase transition, not a universal social law.
🏛️ SocietyEconomic hardship may intensify social conflict, but political outcomes depend on many contingent factors and are not automatic.
🏭 CapitalismIndustrial concentration can contribute to monopoly tendencies; the timing and extent vary historically.

📐 DIALECTICAL THEME 2: Interconnection and Contradiction

Everything contains internal opposing forces; their struggle drives development.

ExampleCapitalist and worker exist IN the same system but AGAINST each other
ExampleSupply and demand interact in markets; this is not, by itself, a complete illustration of Marx’s dialectical method.
Key InsightOpposites are interpenetrating, not separate

🔥 PART 5: Revolution Theory

🔷 5.1 Marxist Debates on Revolution and Reform

Marxist writers argue that capitalism contains structural conflicts involving ownership, wage labour, accumulation and class power. They differ, however, over whether political change is likely to come through reforms, revolutionary breaks, or a combination of both.

Treat claims that capitalism “cannot reform itself” or that ruling groups “never” relinquish power as arguments within particular Marxist traditions, not as universal historical laws. Reforms can produce material gains while leaving major ownership relations intact.

🔷 5.2 Preconditions for Revolution

Historical scholarship examines a range of conditions associated with periods of major political transformation: economic crisis, state legitimacy, social inequality, organisation, political ideas, international pressures and broad participation.

These are analytical categories, not a checklist, threshold or practical guide. No single factor is necessary or sufficient, and outcomes differ sharply across countries and periods.

🔷 5.3 The Revolutionary Process

Marxist accounts discuss varied historical patterns, including reform, mass movements, revolution, civil conflict, constitutional change and the formation of new institutions. These experiences do not follow one universal sequence.

Use the concepts of class conflict, state power and social transformation to interpret historical cases. Do not treat a diagram as a prediction, a timetable or a set of instructions.

🏁 PART 6: Dictatorship of the Proletariat & Communism

🔷 6.1 Dictatorship of the Proletariat (Most Misunderstood Concept)

In Marx’s vocabulary, “dictatorship of the proletariat” refers to political class rule during a transition beyond capitalism. Marx’s brief use of the term does not settle one institutional model, nor does it by itself establish that every member of the bourgeoisie must be excluded from all political participation.

FormPossible forms discussed by different Marxist traditions include councils or soviets; democratic centralism is associated especially with later Leninist party practice, not a universal institutional definition supplied by Marx.
PurposeSuppress counter-revolution, expropriate capitalists, organize production

🌿 PART 7: Branches & Evolutions of Marxism

🌳 7.0 The Family Tree of Marxism — Why So Many Branches?

This is a simplified map of intellectual currents, not a strict family tree: branches overlap, disagree internally and do not all descend in a single line.

Marxism is not a frozen doctrine — it is a living tradition that has evolved across 175+ years, three continents, and countless revolutions. Each branch arose from a real historical crisis: a failed revolution, a new form of capitalism, a colonial struggle, or a theoretical impasse. To understand Marxism is to understand its splits, debates, and mutations.

🎯Big Picture: Every branch keeps Marx's core (class struggle, materialism, critique of capital) but updates one or more specific elements: the agent of revolution, the role of culture, the meaning of the state, or the path to socialism.

📕 7.1 Classical Marxism ☭ FOUNDATION
Era1840s–1890s
FoundersMarx, Engels
GeographyGermany, Britain, France
Key TextsCapital, Manifesto, German Ideology

The original Marxism — the philosophy as Marx and Engels themselves built it. It treats history as scientifically explainable through the laws of historical materialism and sees the industrial proletariat of advanced capitalist countries as the revolutionary subject.

Classical Marxism 📕 — Original Marxism of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels

Core Idea 🎯 — 💰 Economy shapes society.
⚔️ Class struggle (workers vs capitalists) drives history.
🌹 Capitalism will eventually be replaced by socialism.

Easy Memory 🧠 — 🏠 "The original blueprint."

🎯 Core Tenets

Historical Materialism Labor Theory of Value Class Struggle Surplus Value Proletarian Revolution Withering of the State

💡 Key Insight

Some classical Marxist interpretations expected capitalism’s contradictions to create the conditions for socialist transformation. This is a theoretical expectation, not a guaranteed historical law.

⚠️ Limitation

Assumed revolution would happen in the most advanced capitalist countries (Britain, Germany, USA). History proved otherwise — revolutions broke out in backward peripheral countries (Russia, China, Cuba).

📘 7.2 Orthodox Marxism ☭ (Second International) DETERMINIST
Era1889–1914
ThinkersKautsky, Plekhanov, Labriola
GeographyGermany, Russia, Italy
Key TextsThe Class Struggle (Kautsky)

Some Second International currents were criticised for deterministic readings of historical development; the tradition itself was diverse.

Orthodox Marxism 🏭 — Strict followers of classical Marxism.

Core Idea 🎯 —⚔️ Revolution as one debated path of social transformation.
👷 Industrial workers are the main force of change.
📈 Capitalism contains contradictions and crises; whether they produce collapse or socialist transformation is contested.

Easy Memory 🧠 —📏 "Marxism without modification.".

🧬 Core Beliefs

Historical tendencies (not iron laws) Tendency toward crisis (debated) Mass Party Politics Scientific Socialism Productivist Optimism

⚔️ The Great Split (1899): Reform vs Revolution

PositionChampionArgument
🔧 RevisionismEduard BernsteinCapitalism is reforming itself; socialism via the ballot box
🌹 RevolutionRosa LuxemburgLuxemburg defended mass strikes as an important form of class struggle; her argument should not be reduced to one exclusive tactic.
⚖️ CentreKarl KautskyKautsky’s position changed over time; he defended parliamentary democracy and argued over the relation between political conditions and socialist transformation.
💥The Collapse: The Second International shattered in 1914 when socialist parties voted to support their own governments in WWI — betraying internationalism. This catastrophic failure paved the way for Leninism.
🚩 7.3 Marxism–Leninism REVOLUTIONARY
Era1902–present
ThinkersLenin, Stalin, Bukharin
GeographyRussia, USSR, global communist movement
Key TextsWhat is to be Done?, Imperialism, State & Revolution

Lenin's adaptation of Marxism to agrarian and unevenly industrialised Russia — and after 1917, the official doctrine of the world communist movement. It became the most politically influential branch in history.

🔑 Lenin's 5 Key Innovations

InnovationMeaning
⚔️ Vanguard PartyLenin argued for a disciplined revolutionary party and distinguished trade-union consciousness from socialist political consciousness; this is Lenin’s argument, not a neutral sociological fact.
🌍 ImperialismLenin’s influential formulation: imperialism as a stage of capitalism characterised by monopoly and finance capital; the phrase is Lenin’s analysis, not a neutral consensus description — monopolies export capital, exploit colonies, bribe a "labor aristocracy" at home
⛓️ Weakest LinkRevolution breaks where imperialism's chain is weakest (Russia, not Germany)
🗳️ Democratic CentralismA Leninist organisational principle: internal discussion followed by collective implementation; actual practices and interpretations have varied.
🤝 Worker-Peasant AllianceIn agrarian countries, proletariat must ally with poor peasantry

📜 Stalinist Codification (1924–1953)

After Lenin's death, Stalin codified "Marxism-Leninism" as state ideology with key additions: Socialism in One Country (rejecting permanent revolution), Five-Year Plans (state-led industrialization), forced collectivization of agriculture, and one-party rule. Critics call this Stalinism, a distortion of Lenin.

The Big Debate: Was Stalinism a natural outgrowth of Leninism (Trotsky, anarchists) or a betrayal of it (orthodox Leninists, Maoists)? This remains the most charged question in Marxist politics.
⚔️ 7.4 Trotskyism INTERNATIONALIST
Era1905–present
FounderLeon Trotsky
OrganizationFourth International (1938)
Key TextThe Revolution Betrayed (1936)

The principled anti-Stalinist wing of revolutionary Marxism. Trotsky was Lenin's co-leader in 1917, lost the power struggle to Stalin, was exiled, then assassinated in Mexico (1940).

⚡ Core Theses

Permanent Revolution Combined & Uneven Development Degenerated Workers' State Political Revolution vs Stalinism World Revolution

Permanent Revolution: Trotsky’s theory argued that in countries with uneven development, democratic and socialist tasks could become intertwined and that socialist transformation would face international constraints.

The USSR Analysis: Stalin's USSR was a "degenerated workers' state" — socialist property forms exist, but a bureaucratic caste rules. Solution: political revolution by workers to overthrow the bureaucracy without restoring capitalism.

Trotskyism's organizational weakness (small parties) is offset by its theoretical influence: many academic Marxists, the British SWP tradition, and the U.S. ISO/Socialist Alternative drew from Trotsky.
🐉 7.5 Maoism (Mao Zedong Thought) PEASANT REVOLUTION
Era1927–present
FounderMao Zedong
GeographyChina, then Peru, Nepal, India, Philippines
Key TextsOn Contradiction, On Practice, Little Red Book

Marxism adapted to agrarian, semi-colonial China — where industrial workers were a tiny minority. Mao made the peasantry the engine of revolution, breaking with classical Marxism's urban focus.

🌾 Core Innovations

ConceptMeaning
🌾 Peasant role in the Chinese RevolutionMao placed the peasantry at the centre of the Chinese revolutionary experience; the applicability of this analysis elsewhere is debated.
🏞️ Protracted People's WarA historical theory of protracted conflict developed in the Chinese revolutionary context; this is a subject of political history, not a practical guide.
🔄 Mass Line"From the masses, to the masses" — leadership distills popular wisdom and returns it as policy
🎭 Cultural RevolutionClass struggle continues under socialism; must permanently revolutionize culture
🗡️ Continuing class struggle under socialism (Maoist thesis)Mao argued that bureaucratisation and new inequalities could emerge after a revolution; his proposed mass campaigns had highly contested and harmful historical consequences.
⚖️ Contradiction AnalysisDistinguish "principal" from "secondary" contradictions; "antagonistic" from "non-antagonistic"

🌍 Global Influence

Maoism inspired the global "Third World" revolutionary movements of the 1960s–80s: the Naxalites in India, Shining Path in Peru, New People's Army in the Philippines, and the Nepali Maoists (who took power 2006). It also shaped Western "New Left" radicalism.

Critical Note: The Cultural Revolution (1966–76) caused widespread persecution, disruption and suffering, and the Great Leap Forward (1958–62) produced one of history's deadliest famines. Modern Marxists fiercely debate whether these were errors of Maoism or distortions of it.
🎭 7.6 Western Marxism ☭ CULTURAL TURN
Era1920s–1970s (broad, contested periodisation)
ThinkersLukács, Korsch, Gramsci, Frankfurt School
GeographyGermany, Italy, France
Key TextsHistory & Class Consciousness; Prison Notebooks

Born from a crisis: revolutions failed in Germany, Italy, and Hungary (1918–23) despite Marx's prediction. Why did workers not revolt? Western Marxists turned away from economics toward culture, ideology, philosophy, and consciousness.

Western Marxism 🎭 — Developed mainly in Western Europe after revolutions failed there.

Core Idea 🎯 — Ideas, culture, media, education, and beliefs matter too.
📺 People can be controlled not only by money but also by culture.

Easy Memory 🧠 — 🎬 "Why don't workers revolt? Look at culture!"

🎨 The Shift

Reification Hegemony Culture Industry One-Dimensional Man Class Consciousness Praxis

🌟 Key Figures

ThinkerConceptInsight
György LukácsReificationCapitalism makes humans see social relations as "thing-like" — frozen, natural
Antonio GramsciCultural HegemonyRuling class rules through consent, not just force; capture culture before politics
Karl KorschMarxism as MethodMarxism is not a closed system but a critical method
Henri LefebvreEveryday LifeAlienation extends from work into leisure, urban space, consumption
🧠 7.7 Frankfurt School & Critical Theory CRITICAL THEORY
EraEarly twentieth century–present
ThinkersAdorno, Horkheimer, Marcuse, Benjamin, Fromm, Habermas
InstitutionInstitute for Social Research, Frankfurt
Key TextsDialectic of Enlightenment; One-Dimensional Man

A highly influential branch of Western Marxism. The Frankfurt School fused Marx + Freud + Weber to explain why advanced capitalism was so stable, why fascism happened, and how mass culture pacified resistance.

🔬 Core Concepts

🎬 Culture Industry(Adorno/Horkheimer) Adorno and Horkheimer analyse how commercial cultural production can standardise culture and encourage conformity; their thesis remains debated and should not be reduced to a claim that audiences are simply passive.
📺 One-Dimensional Man(Marcuse) Capitalism integrates dissent into consumerism; workers buy into the system
🌪️ Dialectic of EnlightenmentExamines how instrumental reason, bureaucracy and technological power can be turned toward domination; do not describe Auschwitz simply as “rational.”
🧠 Authoritarian Personality(Fromm/Adorno) Psychological roots of fascism in family + capitalism
💬 Communicative Action(Habermas) Emancipation through undistorted communication, not just revolution
🌌 Negative Dialectics(Adorno) Reject any closed positive system; thought must remain critical
The Frankfurt School essentially became the intellectual backbone of New Left politics, cultural studies, media theory, and contemporary critical scholarship in universities worldwide.
📓 7.8 Gramscian Marxism HEGEMONY
Era: Prison Notebooks written mainly 1929–1935; published posthumously

Written during Gramsci’s imprisonment under the Fascist regime and subject to censorship, his Prison Notebooks are perhaps the a major and influential Marxist work of the twentieth century. He shifted focus from state seizure to cultural leadership.

🎯 Core Concepts

ConceptMeaning
🎭 Cultural HegemonyGramsci analyses how leadership and consent can help reproduce power, alongside coercion and institutional structures.
⚔️ War of manoeuvreA concept for rapid political confrontation; its applicability depends on historical conditions and is not a universal strategy.
🛡️ War of PositionA concept for prolonged political and cultural struggle within civil society; Gramsci’s distinction is analytical, not a universal formula for every country.
🧑‍🏫 Organic IntellectualsGramsci argues that social groups develop intellectuals who help organise and articulate their worldviews.
🏛️ Civil SocietySchools, churches, media, family — the "trenches" of capitalist rule
🤝 Historic BlocAlliance of classes united by a shared worldview, not just economic interest
🌀 Passive RevolutionRuling class absorbs reform demands to prevent real change
🌍Massive Modern Influence: Gramsci shaped Cultural Studies (Hall), post-Marxism (Laclau/Mouffe), Eurocommunism, Latin American Pink Tide, and even right-wing thinkers who study how to wage cultural war.
🏗️ 7.9 Structural Marxism ☭ SCIENTIFIC
Era1960s–1980s
ThinkersAlthusser, Poulantzas, Balibar
GeographyFrance
Key TextsFor Marx; Reading Capital

Louis Althusser fused Marxism with French structuralism (Lévi-Strauss, Lacan) to create a "scientific" Marxism stripped of humanism, alienation, and Hegelian residues. He famously argued for an "epistemological break" between the young (humanist) Marx and the mature (scientific) Marx.

Structural Marxism 🏗️ — Associated with Louis Althusser

Core Idea 🎯 — 🏛️ Society is made of structures:-
Economy 💰
State 🏢
Education 🎓
Family 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦
🐣 These structures shape people's behavior.

Easy Memory 🧠 — 🏗️ "Social structures shape action, while individual and collective agency can also reproduce or transform them."

🔬 Core Concepts

🚔 RSAs (Repressive State Apparatuses)Institutions through which the state can exercise coercive power; their operation is historically and politically specific.
🏫 ISAs (Ideological State Apparatuses)Schools, churches, family and media can help reproduce social relations through ideology and everyday practices.
📢 InterpellationIdeology "hails" you into a subject position ("Hey, you!") — you become who the system needs
🌀 OverdeterminationHistorical events have multiple causes, not a single economic one
🌳 Relative AutonomySuperstructure has its own logic; not just reflection of base
📐 Symptomatic ReadingRead texts for what they cannot say — their gaps reveal their ideology

⚔️ Poulantzas's State Theory

Nicos Poulantzas extended Althusser to political theory: the capitalist state is structurally biased toward capital but maintains "relative autonomy" — it's not a simple tool of the ruling class. This shaped contemporary state theory.

🔬 7.10 Analytical Marxism (Rational-Choice Marxism) ANGLO-ANALYTIC
Era1978–present
ThinkersG.A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer, Erik Olin Wright
GeographyUK, USA — the "September Group"
Key TextKarl Marx's Theory of History (Cohen, 1978)

"No-Bullshit Marxism" — applied rigorous analytic philosophy, game theory, and methodological individualism to Marxist claims. Stripped away dialectical mysticism, kept what could survive logical scrutiny.

⚙️ Key Contributions

G.A. CohenDefended historical materialism with crystalline analytic precision
Jon ElsterApplied game theory and rational choice to class struggle
John RoemerRebuilt exploitation theory using mathematical economics
Erik Olin WrightMapped contemporary class structures; "real utopias" project
Adam PrzeworskiWhy social-democratic compromise persists under capitalism
Critics call it "Marxism with the politics removed." Defenders say it's the only branch that meets contemporary standards of academic rigor.
🌐 7.11 Neo-Marxism ☭ SYNTHESIS
Era1960s–present
ThinkersHabermas, Marcuse, Offe, Wallerstein, Harvey
ApproachMarx + Weber + Freud + Geography + Sociology
FocusLate capitalism, legitimation, globalization

An umbrella for late-20th-century Marxists who broadened classical Marxism with insights from other social sciences. Less doctrinal, more empirical.

Neo-Marxism 🌍 — Modernized Marxism

Core Idea 🎯 — 💰 Economy is important, but so are:
📺 Media
🎭 Culture
🌐 Globalization
👩‍🦰 Gender
🌱 Environment

Easy Memory 🧠 — 🔄 "Marxism updated for the modern world."

♀ 7.12 Marxist & Socialist Feminism GENDER + CLASS
Era1970s–present (roots in Engels 1884)
ThinkersFederici, Mitchell, Hartmann, Vogel, Davis, Fraser
Key IssueReproductive labor, patriarchy + capital
Key TextsCaliban & the Witch; Capitalist Patriarchy

Asks the question Marx underdeveloped: how does capitalism depend on women's unpaid labor (childcare, housework, emotional labor)? The "social reproduction" of labor power is invisible in Capital but essential.

🌹 Key Concepts

Social Reproduction Wages for Housework Dual Systems Theory Primitive Accumulation of Bodies Reproductive Labor Care Crisis

📚 Pillar Thinkers

Friedrich EngelsOrigin of the Family (1884) — patriarchy tied to private property
Silvia FedericiCaliban & the Witch — witch-hunts as primitive accumulation that disciplined women's bodies
Juliet MitchellWomen's oppression has 4 structures: production, reproduction, sexuality, socialization
Lise VogelSocial Reproduction Theory — women's labor reproduces the working class itself
Nancy FraserCapitalism's crises now include care crises, ecological crises, political crises
Angela DavisRace + gender + class as intersecting axes of capitalist oppression
🌍 7.13 Postcolonial & Black Marxism RACE + EMPIRE
Era1930s–present
ThinkersC.L.R. James, Fanon, Cabral, Robinson, Du Bois, Hall
Key IssueColonialism, race, imperial extraction
Key TextsBlack Jacobins; Wretched of the Earth; Black Marxism

Marxists from the Global South and African diaspora pushed Marxism to take colonialism and race seriously, not as side issues but as constitutive of capitalism itself.

🌟 Core Insights

C.L.R. JamesThe Black Jacobins — Haitian slaves as world-historical revolutionary subject
Frantz FanonWretched of the Earth — colonial violence creates "natives"; decolonization is necessarily violent
Amílcar CabralNational liberation as cultural reconquest; "return to the source"
Cedric Robinson"Racial Capitalism" — capitalism developed THROUGH racial slavery, not despite it
W.E.B. Du BoisU.S. Civil War as a "general strike" of slaves; abolition democracy
Walter RodneyHow Europe Underdeveloped Africa — colonial extraction as the engine of European wealth
🔥Big Idea: "Racial capitalism" (Cedric Robinson) — capitalism has always been racial. Slavery, colonialism, and racism are not historical accidents but structural features of how capital accumulates.
💚 7.14 Eco-Marxism (Green Marxism) CLIMATE + CAPITAL
Era1970s–present (booming since 2000s)
ThinkersJames O'Connor, Foster, Burkett, Moore, Saito, Malm
Key IssueCapitalism & ecological catastrophe
Key TextsMarx's Ecology; Fossil Capital; Capital in the Anthropocene

Marxism for the climate emergency. Argues capitalism's infinite growth imperative is fundamentally incompatible with a finite biosphere — and that Marx himself, properly read, was already an ecological thinker.

🌱 Key Concepts

🌊 Metabolic Rift(Foster) Capitalism ruptures the metabolic exchange between humans and nature (soil depletion, climate change)
⚡ Second Contradiction(O'Connor) Capitalism undermines its own ecological conditions of production
🛢️ Fossil Capital(Malm) Coal/oil wasn't adopted because cheap — it was adopted because it disciplined labor
🌍 Capitalocene(Moore) It's not the "Anthropocene" — humans didn't do this; capital did
🌳 Degrowth Communism(Kohei Saito) Late Marx was a degrowth communist — sustainability requires shrinking the economy
Eco-Marxism is arguably the fastest-growing branch in the 21st century, as climate crisis forces a reckoning with capitalism's growth imperative.
🏴 7.15 Autonomist Marxism WORKERS' POWER
Era1960s–present
ThinkersTronti, Negri, Hardt, Bifo, Cleaver
GeographyItaly (operaismo movement)
Key TextsWorkers and Capital; Empire; Multitude

An Italian current rejecting both Stalinism and reformism. Argued workers' struggles drive capitalism's development — capital is always reacting to worker resistance, not vice versa. Famously inverts the orthodox "base → struggle" sequence.

⚒️ Key Concepts

Workers' Inquiry Refusal of Work Class Composition Social Factory Immaterial Labor Multitude Empire

Negri & Hardt's Empire (2000): Argued capitalism has entered a new stage — "Empire" — a decentered global network of power, opposed by the "Multitude" (a new revolutionary subject beyond the industrial working class).

🚀 7.16 Post-Marxism & 21st-Century Marxism CONTEMPORARY
Era1985–present
ThinkersLaclau, Mouffe, Žižek, Badiou, Jameson, Harvey, Wood
Key IssueMarxism after the Soviet collapse
Key TextsHegemony & Socialist Strategy; The Sublime Object of Ideology

How does Marxism survive after 1991? Two paths emerged: Post-Marxism moves beyond Marx's framework, while 21st-century Marxism defends a renewed, updated Marxism for the age of neoliberalism and ecological crisis.

🎭 Post-Marxism (Laclau & Mouffe)

Argued class is just one identity among many; politics is the construction of "chains of equivalence" between diverse struggles (workers + feminists + LGBTQ + greens + anti-racists). Inspired left-populism (Podemos, France Insoumise, Sanders campaign).

⚡ Žižek's Lacanian Marxism

Slovenian philosopher Slavoj Žižek revived Marxism through Lacanian psychoanalysis. Ideology isn't false belief — it's what we do while knowing better ("they know not, but they do it"). Hugely influential in cultural theory.

🌎 Modern Defenders of Classical Marxism

David HarveyThe most-read Marxist of our time; geographer of capital, neoliberalism, urbanism
Ellen Meiksins WoodPolitical Marxism — capitalism's origins, defending class analysis against post-Marxism
Fredric Jameson"Postmodernism is the cultural logic of late capitalism"
Vivek ChibberDefends universalism of capital against postcolonial particularism
Wolfgang Streeck"How will capitalism end?" — capitalism is unraveling without socialism replacing it

🏆 FINAL SYNTHESIS

Marxism is the scientific theory that the economic base determines the social superstructure, that contradictions drive historical change through class struggle, that exploitation is built into wage labor through the extraction of surplus value, and that only a proletarian revolution abolishing private property can achieve true human freedom in a classless, stateless, communist society.
