🎬 Act I · The Rules of Constitutional Change

🏛️ Article 368 — Power, Procedure & Limits

An amendment changes the constitutional text through a constitutionally authorised process. India combines flexibility with rigidity: some changes need only an ordinary legislative majority, most need a special majority, and changes touching the federal compact also need State ratification. The key distinction is between ordinary legislative power and constituent power. Article 368 sits in Part XX.

📜 Constitutional changeWhich procedure does the Constitution prescribe?
① Simple majoritySpecified changes outside Article 368
② Special majorityThe Article 368 parliamentary route
③ Special + StatesFederal provisions need ratification

① 🟢 Simple majority — outside Article 368

Some provisions expressly let Parliament alter constitutional consequences by ordinary law. Examples: admission, formation and boundary changes of States (Arts. 2–4), creation or abolition of Legislative Councils (Art. 169), citizenship law (Art. 11), and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules. By express declaration these are not treated as amendments for Article 368.

Special conditions still apply: Art. 169 needs a prior special-majority resolution of the State Assembly, and Art. 3 needs a Presidential reference to the State Legislature.

② 🔵 Special majority — Article 368

Each House must pass the Bill by a majority of its total membership and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Both tests apply together; abstentions are not votes cast. Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and most institutional provisions can be amended this way, subject to the basic-structure limit.

③ 🟣 Special majority + State ratification

Changes to the protected federal fields in the proviso to Art. 368(2) also need ratification by the Legislatures of at least one-half of the States, before the Bill goes to the President. States ratify by ordinary majority; no general deadline is prescribed, and Union Territories do not count.

🚨 Six procedure traps:
  • There is no joint sitting (Art. 108 does not apply) to resolve disagreement on an Article 368 Bill.
  • No prior Presidential recommendation is needed merely because it is an amendment Bill.
  • The Rajya Sabha has full co-equal authority here.
  • The President cannot withhold assent or return a duly passed Article 368 Bill (24th Amendment, 1971).
  • “Half of the States” means States only, and half — not all.
  • A State Legislature cannot initiate an amendment, and no referendum is provided.

🧠 Majority memory key

“More than half the whole; at least two-thirds who poll.” The minimum “Yes” vote is the higher of the two thresholds. “Total membership” is the House’s full strength, as parliamentary practice reckons it, not just who turns up.

Worked example: total 245, voting 180 → $$\lfloor 245/2\rfloor+1=123$$ and $$\lceil 2\times180/3\rceil=120$$ → need $$\max(123,120)=123$$ affirmative votes.

⚖️ Act II · Identity Beneath the Text

⚖️ Basic Structure — Amend, Do Not Destroy

In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (24 April 1973) a thirteen-judge Bench held, by a 7–6 majority on the central limitation, that Parliament may amend every part of the Constitution but cannot damage or destroy its basic structure. The doctrine limits the substance of the amending power; Article 368 also imposes procedural limits.

🏛️ Constitutional supremacy

The Constitution, not unlimited parliamentary sovereignty, is supreme. A limited amending power is itself a basic feature.

🗳️ Democratic republic

Representative government and free and fair elections cannot be hollowed out by amendment.

⚖️ Rule of law & review

Judicial review, judicial independence and the rule of law protect every other limit.

🤝 Federal & secular order

Federalism and secularism are recognised basic features; their application depends on the issue before the Court.

🧭 Separation & balance

Separation of powers and harmony between Parts III and IV developed through Minerva Mills and later cases.

🕊️ Dignity & unity

Dignity, unity and integrity of the nation, and essential equality commitments recur in the Court’s reasoning.

🔍 No closed catalogue: the Supreme Court has never issued one exhaustive list; features have emerged case by case. Not every infringement of a Fundamental Right is automatically a destruction of the basic structure.

🔥 Act IV · The Mini-Constitution

🔥 42nd Amendment, 1976 — Constitutional Power Expanded

Enacted during the 1975–77 Emergency, partly on the Swaran Singh Committee’s recommendations, the 42nd Amendment was extraordinarily wide-ranging. It combined enduring social and institutional additions with attempts to concentrate power, restrict judicial review and remove limits on amendment. Its provisions did not all commence together or share the same later fate.

⚖️ Article 31C: the constitutional balance

The 25th Amendment inserted Art. 31C for laws giving effect to Art. 39(b)–(c); it shields them from challenge under Arts. 14 and 19 (and, originally, 31). Kesavananda did not preserve the clause making a legislature’s declaration conclusive. The 42nd tried to extend the shield to every Directive Principle; Minerva Mills invalidated that expansion, so only the narrower 39(b)–(c) protection survives. In Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024) the Court confirmed that position and held that not every privately owned resource is automatically a “material resource of the community” under Art. 39(b).

🏛️ Tribunals without extinguishing judicial review

Art. 323A covers administrative tribunals (Parliament legislates); Art. 323B covers tribunals for specified subjects such as taxation and industrial disputes. L. Chandra Kumar (1997) held that constitutional review under Arts. 226/227 and 32 cannot be wholly excluded, so tribunals supplement rather than replace the superior courts.

🚨 Never write “the 44th Amendment struck down the Art. 31C expansion.” The Supreme Court did, in 1980. Legislative reversal and judicial invalidation are different events.
🛡️ Act V · Liberty After the Emergency

🛡️ 44th Amendment, 1978 — Safeguards Restored

Enacted by the post-Emergency Janata-era Parliament, the 44th strengthened democratic and civil-liberty safeguards. Its title year is 1978; assent came in 1979. Enacted text, notified commencement and current operative law are not always identical.

🏠 Property: from Fundamental Right to constitutional right

“No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law.”

Art. 300A protects persons, not only citizens. Executive action without legal authority cannot deprive anyone of property. Writ relief is ordinarily sought under Art. 226; Art. 32 is generally unavailable for Art. 300A alone because it is no longer a Fundamental Right. K.T. Plantation (2011) is key on public purpose and compensation scrutiny.

④ 🔀 Anti-defection: stability versus deliberation

The 52nd Amendment inserted the Tenth Schedule. Disqualification follows voluntarily giving up party membership or defying the whip; an independent member who joins a party is covered, and a nominated member has a six-month window to join. A valid merger needs at least two-thirds of the legislature party. The 91st Amendment removed the old one-third split exception but kept merger. The Speaker or Chairman ordinarily decides, subject to judicial review; Arts. 103 and 192 govern different disqualification routes. Kihoto Hollohan (1992) upheld the scheme but invalidated paragraph 7 (jurisdiction bar) for want of State ratification.

🧮 Ministry-size calculator (91st Amendment)

🧠 Method

Compute $$0.15 \times N$$, round down, include the PM or CM. A State ministry has a floor of 12; the Union has none. Examples: MP (230) → 34; Lok Sabha (543) → 81; a 40-member Assembly → 12 (floor applies).

⑥ 🗺️ Territory: reorganisation is not always cession

Art. 3 allows internal reorganisation by ordinary law; the affected State’s views must be sought but it holds no veto. Cession of Indian territory to a foreign State needs a constitutional amendment (Berubari, 1960), whereas settling an uncertain boundary by agreement is different (Maganbhai Patel, 1969). Connect the 9th and 100th Amendments with this distinction.

✨ Act VIII · Contemporary Constitutional Design

✨ GST, Backward Classes, EWS & Women’s Representation

🧾 101st Amendment, 2016: fiscal federalism

246A · Power

Special GST competence for Parliament and State Legislatures; Parliament alone for inter-State supply.

269A · Inter-State GST

Levied and collected by the Union, apportioned as provided; imports count as inter-State supply.

279A · GST Council

Chaired by the Union Finance Minister; coordinates rates, exemptions and thresholds by recommendation.

Council decisions need three-fourths of weighted votes of members present and voting: the Union holds one-third weight, States together two-thirds; quorum is half the members. Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022) held the Council’s recommendations are not binding on the Union or States as legislation. Alcohol for human consumption is outside GST, and petroleum products have a deferred-levy arrangement (Art. 279A(5)). The amendment (2016) is distinct from the main rollout on 1 July 2017.

🎯 103rd Amendment, 2019: EWS

Arts. 15(6) and 16(6) enable special provisions for economically weaker sections outside the classes covered by the existing reservation clauses, of up to 10% in addition. Art. 15(6) covers admission (including private aided/unaided institutions, excluding Art. 30(1) minority institutions); Art. 16(6) covers appointments. Janhit Abhiyan (2022) upheld the amendment by 3–2. The income and asset criteria are not written in the Constitution.

🗳️ 104th Amendment, 2019

Art. 334 extended SC/ST legislative reservation to eighty years from the Constitution’s commencement (January 2030). Anglo-Indian nomination was not extended beyond seventy years, so the Lok Sabha’s maximum strength fell from 552 to 550.

🔎 Verify the Text — Then Trust the Notes

Use the official Constitution for operative clauses, the original Amendment Acts for precise textual changes, gazette notifications for commencement, and official judgments for holdings. Coverage here ends at the 106th Act (2023); later developments need separate checking.

  • Legislative Department: official Constitution of India.
  • India Code: Acts and statutory records.
  • Supreme Court of India: judgments and orders.
  • e-Gazette: Acts and commencement notifications.
🔎 Four final accuracy checks: title year ≠ assent date · enactment ≠ commencement · constitutional permission ≠ automatic implementation · legislative reversal ≠ judicial invalidation.
