🌊 Why Did Europeans Come to India?

ERA FILE / THE AGE OF OCEANIC EXPANSION · c. 1450–1700

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

India was already a major node in an interconnected Afro-Eurasian commercial system. European entry did not create this trade; it redirected and militarised parts of it by seeking direct access to Asian goods and bypassing intermediaries.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

High demand for pepper, spices, textiles and other luxury goods; competition among European monarchies; advances in navigation, shipbuilding and cartography; and the search for direct maritime routes after the Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453. The 1453 event was an important context, not the sole cause of exploration.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Portuguese voyages around Africa opened a sea route to the Indian Ocean; the Dutch, English, Danish and French later formed chartered companies. These companies combined commerce with armed shipping, fortified factories, diplomacy and, increasingly, territorial ambitions.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The shift from overland and intermediary-based commerce to armed oceanic competition connected Indian coastal politics to European state rivalry. The decisive transformation was gradual: trading privileges and coastal enclaves preceded large-scale territorial conquest.

🚢 European Trading Companies in India

ERA FILE / THE COMPANIES AND THE INDIAN OCEAN · 16TH–18TH CENTURIES

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

European trading companies were not identical institutions. They differed in ownership, state support, commercial priorities and territorial ambitions, but all operated within an Indian Ocean world already shaped by Asian merchants, port cities and established trading networks.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

European demand for Asian commodities, competition for maritime routes, chartered monopolies, access to capital and naval technology encouraged the creation of companies. Their rulers granted privileges that allowed them to wage war, build forts and negotiate treaties in addition to trading.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Portugal established a Crown-directed Estado da Índia after 1498; the Dutch VOC was chartered in 1602; the English East India Company received its charter in 1600; the Danish East India Company was established in 1616; and the French East India Company was founded in 1664. Their factories and settlements became centres of commercial rivalry.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The companies transformed coastal commerce into a field of armed competition. The English company’s eventual success depended not simply on trade, but on military organisation, revenue extraction, diplomacy and the political conditions of 18th-century India.

🇵🇹 Portuguese in India

ERA FILE / THE PORTUGUESE MARITIME EMPIRE · 1498–1961

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Portuguese were the first Europeans of the early modern period to establish a durable maritime presence in the Indian Ocean. Their Estado da Índia was a network of ports, forts and sea-lanes rather than a uniformly controlled territorial empire across India.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The Crown sought direct access to spices, control over strategic maritime chokepoints and a Christian imperial presence. Naval artillery, fortified ports and the cartaz pass system helped Portugal challenge existing commercial networks.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. Francisco de Almeida, the first viceroy (1505), emphasised naval supremacy. Afonso de Albuquerque captured Goa in 1510, which became the principal Portuguese base. Portuguese influence later extended to Diu, Daman and other coastal settlements, while rivals and Indian powers steadily reduced it.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Portuguese legacies included new crops and foods, printing at Goa (1556), missionary activity and new forms of maritime warfare. Their political power declined under pressure from the Dutch, English and Indian powers; Goa, Daman and Diu were incorporated into India in 1961.

🇳🇱 Dutch East India Company (VOC)

ERA FILE / THE DUTCH COMMERCIAL-IMPERIAL PHASE · 1602–18TH CENTURY

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Dutch East India Company (VOC), chartered in 1602, was a powerful joint-stock corporation with state-backed trading and military privileges. In India, its principal interest lay in commercial procurement and the wider Asian spice network.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The Dutch sought to break the Portuguese monopoly and secure profitable access to pepper, textiles, indigo and other commodities. Their corporate structure, pooled capital and naval power enabled long-distance trade on a large scale.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The Dutch established early factories on the Coromandel Coast, including Masulipatnam, and developed centres such as Pulicat, which became an important headquarters in India. They competed with the Portuguese and English but increasingly prioritised the Indonesian spice trade. Their Indian position weakened after conflicts with the English, including the Battle of Colachel (1741) against Travancore.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The Dutch did not build a major territorial empire in India. Their experience demonstrates that commercial strength and naval reach did not automatically translate into durable political supremacy on the subcontinent.

🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 English East India Company

ERA FILE / THE COMPANY STATE EMERGES · 1600–1858

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The English East India Company began as a chartered trading corporation and gradually acquired sovereign functions: it raised armies, collected revenue, made treaties, administered territories and exercised judicial and political authority. This transition was neither inevitable nor immediate.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The Company initially sought secure trading privileges and access to Indian textiles, spices and other goods. Competition with European companies, conflicts with local authorities, the weakening of Mughal central power and the Company’s growing military-fiscal capacity shaped its expansion.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The 1600 royal charter created the Company; early factories followed at Surat and elsewhere. The 1717 farman of Farrukhsiyar strengthened its commercial privileges in Bengal. After Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764), the Company gained decisive political leverage; the 1765 Diwani grant gave it revenue-collection rights in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Parliamentary regulation followed through the Regulating Act (1773) and Pitt’s India Act (1784).

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The Company state fused private profit with public power. Its abuses and fiscal-military expansion prompted British parliamentary oversight, and the Revolt of 1857 led to the transfer of rule to the Crown under the Government of India Act 1858.

🇫🇷 French East India Company & Carnatic Wars

ERA FILE / THE ANGLO-FRENCH STRUGGLE IN SOUTH INDIA · 1740s–1763

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Carnatic Wars were a series of conflicts in which European rivalry became entangled with succession disputes and regional politics in South India. They were not simply wars fought by France and Britain on Indian soil; Indian rulers and claimants were active participants.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The War of Austrian Succession and Seven Years’ War spilled into the Indian Ocean. Both companies sought influence over the Carnatic and Hyderabad, where contested successions offered opportunities to support rival claimants and obtain political-commercial privileges.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The First Carnatic War (1746–48) ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle. The Second (1749–54) centred on succession struggles and the rivalry between Dupleix and the English. In the Third (1756–63), the English victory at Wandiwash (1760) and the fall of Pondicherry (1761) decisively weakened French political ambitions; the Treaty of Paris (1763) restored some settlements for commerce but restricted French fortification and political role.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The wars demonstrated the effectiveness of disciplined European-style infantry, artillery and alliances with Indian powers. British success at Wandiwash removed France as a serious contender for political supremacy in India.

🏰 New Chapter — Regional Successor States

🏰 Rise of Regional Powers After Mughal Decline

ERA FILE / THE REGIONALISATION OF POWER IN 18TH-CENTURY INDIA · c. 1707–1761

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The weakening of Mughal central authority after Aurangzeb’s death did not create a political vacuum. It produced a plural political landscape in which successor states, warrior confederacies, zamindars and regional elites asserted autonomy while retaining, adapting or contesting Mughal institutions.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Succession conflicts, fiscal strain, jagirdari pressures, provincial military-fiscal growth, invasions and the difficulty of governing a vast empire weakened central control. Regional elites converted provincial offices and local resources into more autonomous states.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Important powers included Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad as successor states; the Marathas as a confederacy; Mysore under the Wodeyars and later Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan; the Sikhs in Punjab; the Jats around Bharatpur; and the Rohillas in Rohilkhand. The invasions of Nadir Shah (1739) and Ahmad Shah Abdali further destabilised North India.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The 18th century should be understood as a period of political reorganisation, not merely decline. Regional states developed sophisticated administrations and armies, but competition among them created openings for European companies to intervene.

📅 Hyderabad State

🕌 Hyderabad — Nizam ul-Mulk (Asaf Jah)

ERA FILE / THE ASAF JAHI STATE IN THE DECCAN · 1724–1948

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Hyderabad emerged as a major successor state to the Mughal Empire under Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah I. The Nizam established a durable dynasty while formally retaining a relationship with the Mughal emperor and later navigating the growing power of the East India Company.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The weakening of Mughal central authority enabled provincial governors to consolidate hereditary and regional power. The Deccan’s strategic location, revenue resources and military politics made Hyderabad central to 18th-century competition.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Asaf Jah I consolidated authority in the Deccan from 1724. His successors faced Maratha pressure, internal factionalism and European intervention. Hyderabad became closely tied to the British through subsidiary alliance arrangements under Nizam Ali Khan in 1798–1800, which restricted its independent foreign and military policy while preserving the dynasty.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Hyderabad remained the largest princely state in British India. After 1947, the Nizam initially resisted accession; Indian military action in September 1948 led to its integration into the Indian Union. The state illustrates the complex relationship between indirect rule and princely sovereignty.
  • Founded by Nizam ul-Mulk (Asaf Jah I) in 1724, also known as Kilich Khan
  • Was one of the leading nobles of post-Aurangzeb era
  • Received Deccan Viceroyalty in 1720; defeated all rivals 1720–22
  • Served as Mughal Wazir 1722–24, frustrated by Emperor Muhammad Shah's incapacity

🔥 Battle of Plassey (1757) & Buxar (1764)

ERA FILE / THE TURNING POINT FROM COMMERCE TO RULE · 1757–1765

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Plassey and Buxar marked successive stages in the Company’s rise in Bengal and North India. Plassey created political leverage through a change of nawab; Buxar confirmed the Company’s military superiority against a broader coalition and strengthened its claim to fiscal authority.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Disputes over Company fortifications, misuse of trade privileges, the sheltering of political opponents and the Company’s growing interference sharpened tensions with Siraj-ud-Daulah. Later, resistance to Company power brought Mir Qasim into alliance with Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh and Mughal emperor Shah Alam II.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

At Plassey (23 June 1757), Robert Clive defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah after the defection of key commanders, notably Mir Jafar’s faction. Mir Jafar became nawab. At Buxar (22 October 1764), Hector Munro defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah and Shah Alam II. The Treaty of Allahabad (1765) granted the Company the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The Diwani gave the Company a major revenue base to finance its army and further expansion. The dual system in Bengal exposed the destructive separation of revenue power from direct administrative responsibility and contributed to the Bengal crisis and famine of 1770.

💰 Economic Impact of British Rule

💰 Drain of Wealth — De-Industrialisation — Land Revenue

ERA FILE / THE COLONIAL ECONOMIC TRANSFORMATION · 1757–1947

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Colonial rule reoriented India’s economy toward imperial priorities. Historians debate the pace and regional variation of de-industrialisation, but the expansion of colonial revenue extraction, unequal trade structures and the transfer of resources to Britain became central themes in nationalist economic critique.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Company revenue needs, free-trade ideology, industrialisation in Britain, tariff policy, changing global demand and the transformation of land rights reshaped production and exchange. Heavy or inflexible revenue assessments increased vulnerability among cultivators, especially during crop failure.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The Permanent Settlement (1793) fixed the state demand with zamindars in Bengal; the Ryotwari system assessed cultivators directly in much of Madras and Bombay; the Mahalwari system operated in parts of North India. Dadabhai Naoroji articulated the Drain Theory; R.C. Dutt analysed colonial economic policy. Indian handicrafts faced competition from machine-made imports, while commercialisation of agriculture expanded.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The economic critique of colonialism linked poverty, famine, indebtedness and industrial decline to imperial structures. It helped transform nationalism from a demand for administrative reform into a critique of the economic foundations of colonial rule.
"The economic bleeding of India was not accidental — it was the deliberate policy of a colonial power. India went from having 27% of global GDP in 1700 to just 3% by 1950." — Dadabhai Naoroji's "Drain of Wealth" Theory (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901)

⚔️ Mysore Kingdom

🐯 Mysore — Hyder Ali & Tipu Sultan

ERA FILE / THE MYSORE RESISTANCE TO COMPANY EXPANSION · 1761–1799

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Mysore rose as a major military-fiscal power under Hyder Ali and his son Tipu Sultan. Their state challenged British expansion in South India and pursued military, administrative and commercial reforms while seeking diplomatic support against the Company.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The weakening of the Wodeyar court, military entrepreneurship and regional competition enabled Hyder Ali’s rise. Mysore’s strategic position and access to resources brought it into conflict with the Company, the Marathas, Hyderabad and Travancore.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The four Anglo-Mysore Wars occurred in 1767–69, 1780–84, 1790–92 and 1799. The Treaty of Madras (1769) ended the First War; the Treaty of Mangalore (1784) concluded the Second. The Treaty of Seringapatam (1792) imposed territorial losses on Tipu. Tipu was killed during the British assault on Seringapatam on 4 May 1799.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The fall of Tipu removed a major opponent of British power in southern India. Mysore was restored to the Wodeyar dynasty under British paramountcy, while the wars demonstrated the importance of military technology, alliances and control of revenue.

ERA FILE / THE AWADH SUCCESSOR STATE AND ITS ANNEXATION · 1722–1856

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Awadh developed from a Mughal province into a wealthy successor state under the Nawabs. Its fertile agricultural base, strategic location and court culture made it important to both North Indian politics and the East India Company.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

As Mughal authority weakened, provincial governors consolidated local control. The Company sought influence over Awadh because it bordered Bengal and provided revenue, troops and strategic depth. Financial demands and subsidiary alliance obligations steadily reduced the Nawab’s autonomy.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Saadat Khan founded the autonomous line in 1722. Shuja-ud-Daulah fought at Buxar in 1764 and signed the Treaty of Allahabad in 1765. Successive treaties increased British influence. Lord Dalhousie annexed Awadh in 1856 on the stated ground of misgovernment, not under the Doctrine of Lapse. The annexation alienated soldiers, taluqdars and sections of the population and contributed to the context of 1857.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Awadh’s annexation illustrates the expansion of indirect control into direct rule. Its political and social dislocation helped make the region a major centre of the 1857 uprising.

🐎 Anglo-Maratha Wars

ERA FILE / THE STRUGGLE FOR SUPREMACY IN WESTERN AND CENTRAL INDIA · 1775–1818

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Maratha Confederacy was a powerful but decentralised political order. The Peshwa, Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle and Gaekwad houses possessed varying degrees of autonomy. Internal rivalries made coordinated resistance difficult, but the confederacy remained a formidable power.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The succession dispute after Peshwa Madhavrao I, rival claims to the Peshwaship, and British ambitions to expand influence in western India triggered the wars. The Company used treaties, subsidiary alliances and military campaigns to exploit divisions among Maratha chiefs.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The First War (1775–82) ended with the Treaty of Salbai (1782), broadly restoring the status quo. The Second (1803–05), following the Treaty of Bassein (1802), saw major British victories over Scindia and Bhonsle. The Third (1817–18) ended Peshwa Baji Rao II’s rule and broke the confederacy’s political power.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · By 1818 the Company had become the paramount power over most of the subcontinent, although several princely states remained. The wars illustrate how diplomacy, internal fragmentation and military force worked together in colonial expansion.

📜 British Constitutional Acts

📜 Evolution of Constitutional Governance 1773–1947

ERA FILE / THE CONSTITUTIONAL EVOLUTION OF BRITISH INDIA · 1773–1947

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

British India’s constitutional framework evolved through statutes enacted by the British Parliament. These measures gradually centralised administration, separated or restructured executive and legislative functions, introduced limited representation and provincial autonomy, but retained ultimate imperial control.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Parliamentary intervention arose from Company corruption, territorial expansion, administrative inefficiency, political pressure in Britain and Indian demands for representation. Constitutional reforms were often designed to preserve imperial authority while accommodating limited participation.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The Regulating Act (1773) initiated parliamentary supervision; Pitt’s India Act (1784) created dual control. The Charter Acts (1813, 1833, 1853) altered trade and administration. The Government of India Act (1858) transferred rule to the Crown. The Indian Councils Acts (1861, 1892, 1909) expanded councils in stages. The Government of India Acts (1919, 1935) introduced dyarchy and later provincial autonomy, while the Indian Independence Act (1947) ended British rule and created two dominions.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The constitutional trajectory was gradual but not linear. Reforms widened institutions without establishing responsible government at the centre; nationalist pressure, mass movements and negotiations ultimately made transfer of power unavoidable.

⭐ Government of India Act 1935 — Most Important Pre-Independence Act

  • Provincial Autonomy introduced — provinces got full responsible govt
  • All-India Federation proposed (never implemented due to Princely States)
  • Dyarchy abolished in provinces; introduced at Centre
  • Bicameral legislatures in 6 provinces
  • Federal Court established (1937)
  • Reserve Bank of India established under this act
  • Separate electorates extended to more communities
  • Burma separated from India
  • Election of 1937: Congress won majority in 8 of 11 provinces
  • This act became basis of Indian Constitution (significant parts adopted)
  • REJECTED by Congress — "machine with strong brakes but no engine"
  • Foundation on which independent India's Constitution was built

ERA FILE / THE COLONIAL ADMINISTRATIVE STATE · 1773–1947

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The offices of Governor-General and later Viceroy embodied the centralising structure of British rule. The office evolved through parliamentary statutes, administrative reforms, wars and changing imperial priorities; the Governor-General of Bengal became Governor-General of India in 1833, and after 1858 the same office also represented the Crown as Viceroy.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The British Parliament sought to regulate Company misrule and coordinate an expanding territorial state. Revenue extraction, legal codification, military consolidation, infrastructure, education and political control shaped successive administrations.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Warren Hastings operated under the Regulating Act framework; Cornwallis reorganised administration and revenue arrangements; Wellesley expanded subsidiary alliances; William Bentinck pursued administrative and social reforms; Dalhousie accelerated annexation and infrastructure; Canning served during the 1857 Revolt and the transfer to Crown rule. Later viceroys confronted nationalism, constitutional reform, mass movements, war and partition.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The office became the apex of an all-India bureaucratic and coercive state. Its policies generated institutions and infrastructure but also entrenched racial hierarchy, economic extraction and limited Indian participation, eventually provoking organised demands for self-government.

🌅 19th Century Reform Movements

🌅 Social & Religious Reform Movements

ERA FILE / THE AGE OF SOCIAL AND RELIGIOUS REFORM · 19TH–EARLY 20TH CENTURIES

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Reform movements responded to social practices, religious debates and new intellectual currents under colonial rule. They were diverse: some sought reinterpretation of inherited traditions, others promoted rationalism, education, caste reform or a return to scriptural authority.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The spread of modern education, print, missionary critiques, Orientalist scholarship, urban associations and debates over social customs encouraged reform. Reformers addressed sati, widow remarriage, child marriage, women’s education, caste discrimination, religious practice and community identity.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Brahmo Samaj (1828), Prarthana Samaj (1867), Arya Samaj (1875), the Ramakrishna Mission (1897), the Aligarh movement, the Deoband movement (1866), the Satyashodhak Samaj (1873) and the Self-Respect movement (1925) represented different approaches. Reform legislation included the abolition of sati (1829), Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act (1856) and Age of Consent Act (1891).

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Reform movements reshaped public debate, education and social organisation, and contributed to modern political consciousness. They also differed sharply in theology, social priorities and ideas of community; they should not be treated as one unified reform programme.

🔥 Revolt of 1857 — First War of Independence

ERA FILE / THE GREAT REBELLION AND THE END OF COMPANY RULE · 1857–1858

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The uprising of 1857 began among Bengal Army sepoys and spread to several north and central Indian regions, drawing in dispossessed rulers, landed groups, peasants and townspeople in different ways. Its geography, leadership and objectives varied; it was a major anti-colonial rebellion, but not a uniform nationwide movement.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Immediate anger over the greased-cartridge controversy interacted with military grievances, racial discrimination, annexations, the Doctrine of Lapse, the annexation of Awadh, revenue pressures and anxieties over social and religious interference.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The revolt began at Meerut on 10 May 1857 and rebels reached Delhi, proclaiming Bahadur Shah Zafar as symbolic emperor. Major centres included Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Bareilly and parts of central India. Leaders included Rani Lakshmibai, Nana Sahib, Tantia Tope, Begum Hazrat Mahal, Kunwar Singh and others. British forces suppressed the major centres by 1858.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The Government of India Act 1858 ended Company rule and transferred authority to the British Crown. The army was reorganised, princely states were treated more cautiously, and colonial policy increasingly emphasised divide-and-rule and non-interference rhetoric. The revolt became a lasting reference point in Indian nationalism.

🏛️ Indian National Congress — Formation & Phases

ERA FILE / THE RISE OF ORGANISED NATIONALISM · 1885–1947

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Indian National Congress provided an enduring all-India political platform. Its character changed from a body of educated political petitioners to a mass nationalist organisation, while debates over methods, representation, social reform and the meaning of freedom continued within and around it.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Western education, the press, railways, communications, economic critique of colonialism and the growth of political associations fostered a shared public sphere. The Ilbert Bill controversy and racial discrimination strengthened political consciousness. A.O. Hume helped convene the first session, but Indian political leaders supplied the movement’s substance and leadership.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The first session met in Bombay in December 1885 under W.C. Bonnerjee. The Moderates pursued constitutional methods and economic criticism; assertive nationalists promoted swadeshi and passive resistance. The 1905 Bengal Partition catalysed the Swadeshi movement; the Surat split occurred in 1907. The Lucknow Pact (1916), Gandhi’s mass politics, the Civil Disobedience phase and the 1942 movement transformed the Congress into a mass organisation.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Congress helped build an all-India nationalist vocabulary and political network. Its internal diversity, negotiations with the British, relations with other political organisations and the communal question shaped the final transfer of power and partition.

⚡ The Revolutionaries — Bhagat Singh & Others

ERA FILE / REVOLUTIONARY NATIONALISM · c. 1905–1931 AND AFTER

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Revolutionary nationalists believed that colonial rule would not end through petitions alone. Their methods ranged from secret societies and political assassinations to armed raids, underground organisation, public trials and revolutionary propaganda. Their ideologies evolved, and many later articulated socialist and anti-imperialist goals.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The partition of Bengal, repression of nationalist activity, racial discrimination and the perceived limits of constitutional politics encouraged secret revolutionary groups. International revolutionary networks and anti-colonial movements influenced activists in India and abroad.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Early networks included Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar in Bengal, Abhinav Bharat in western India and the Ghadar movement overseas. The Hindustan Republican Association was reorganised as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association in 1928. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were executed in 1931 after the Lahore Conspiracy Case; Chittagong activists led by Surya Sen carried out the 1930 armoury raid.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Revolutionary actions did not overthrow colonial rule militarily, but they inspired youth, challenged imperial legitimacy and shaped political debate. Bhagat Singh’s writings and courtroom statements gave revolutionary nationalism a pronounced anti-imperialist and socialist vocabulary.
"They may kill me, but they cannot kill my ideas. They can crush my body, but they will not be able to crush my spirit." — Bhagat Singh (1907–1931) — executed March 23, 1931

ERA FILE / THE MANY STREAMS OF ANTI-COLONIAL RESISTANCE · 19TH–20TH CENTURIES

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Indian nationalism was never confined to one organisation or method. Peasant, tribal, worker, student, socialist, revolutionary, regional and princely-state movements challenged colonial power and local hierarchies, often pursuing distinct immediate aims.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Land revenue demands, forest restrictions, forced labour, plantation conditions, industrial exploitation, caste oppression and political exclusion produced different forms of resistance. Global currents such as socialism, anti-imperialism and revolutionary nationalism also influenced activists.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Examples include tribal uprisings such as the Santhal rebellion and Munda Ulgulan; peasant struggles such as Champaran, Kheda, Bardoli and Tebhaga; workers’ organising; the Ghadar movement; revolutionary networks in Bengal, Punjab and northern India; and the Praja Mandal movements in princely states. Their chronology and leadership differed by region.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · These movements widened the social base of resistance and brought questions of land, labour, caste, forest rights and social equality into public debate. They should be studied as connected but distinct movements, not as a single uniform campaign.

🕊️ Gandhi's Major Movements

ERA FILE / THE GANDHIAN PHASE OF NATIONALISM · 1915–1942

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Gandhi transformed anti-colonial politics by combining mass mobilisation with satyagraha, non-violence, non-cooperation and constructive work. His approach linked political freedom to ethical discipline and social reform, although it also generated debate among revolutionaries, socialists, communal organisations and other nationalists.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Gandhi’s South African experience shaped satyagraha. In India, local struggles over indigo, taxation and labour offered early opportunities to test mass methods. The First World War, the Rowlatt legislation and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre widened opposition to colonial rule.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad (1918) and Kheda (1918) were formative campaigns. The Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919) and Khilafat alliance preceded the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22). After its withdrawal, constructive programmes and local campaigns continued. The Civil Disobedience Movement began with the Salt March in 1930; the Quit India call came in 1942.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Gandhian methods brought peasants, workers, women, students and merchants into national politics on an unprecedented scale. Movements also revealed limits of unity, uneven participation and tensions between mass action and negotiated constitutional politics.

✊ Chapter 11b — First Mass Movement

✊ Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922)

ERA FILE / THE FIRST NATIONWIDE GANDHIAN MASS CAMPAIGN · 1920–1922

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Non-Cooperation Movement marked a decisive shift from elite constitutional agitation toward sustained mass politics. Gandhi argued that British rule depended on Indian cooperation and that disciplined withdrawal of that cooperation could undermine colonial authority.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Khilafat question created a climate of anger. The Congress adopted the programme at the Calcutta special session (September 1920) and confirmed it at Nagpur (December 1920), reorganising itself for wider participation.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The programme included surrender of titles, boycott of government schools, courts and legislatures, foreign cloth and official functions, alongside promotion of khadi, national education and Hindu-Muslim unity. It spread through students, lawyers, traders, peasants and workers, though local aims and intensity varied. The Chauri Chaura violence on 5 February 1922 led Gandhi to suspend the movement.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The movement did not achieve swaraj, but it expanded Congress organisation and political participation on a mass scale. Its withdrawal triggered debate over strategy, leading among other developments to the Swaraj Party’s formation in 1923.

🧂 Chapter 11c — Dandi March

🧂 Civil Disobedience Movement (1930) — Dandi March

ERA FILE / THE SALT SATYAGRAHA AND CIVIL DISOBEDIENCE · 1930–1934

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Civil Disobedience marked a new phase of mass resistance in which people openly broke selected colonial laws. Salt became a powerful symbol because it was used by all classes and the colonial monopoly burdened everyday life.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The Simon Commission, the Nehru Report controversy, the Lahore Congress declaration of Purna Swaraj (December 1929) and the failure of constitutional concessions sharpened the demand for complete independence. Gandhi selected salt as a simple, widely understood issue capable of uniting diverse groups.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Gandhi began the march from Sabarmati on 12 March 1930 and reached Dandi on 5 April; he broke the salt law on 6 April. The campaign spread through salt-law violations, forest-law breaches, no-tax campaigns and boycotts. The Dharasana raid and mass arrests drew international attention. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 March 1931) led to participation in the Second Round Table Conference; civil disobedience resumed in 1932 and was eventually withdrawn in 1934.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The campaign drew women and rural communities into direct action and exposed the coercive character of colonial law. It internationalised the freedom struggle and forced negotiations, though it did not secure immediate independence.
"With this, I am shaking the foundations of the British Empire." — Mahatma Gandhi, picking up salt at Dandi beach, April 6, 1930 — From the transcript: Dandi March significance

🎯 Why SALT as the Issue?

  • Salt — the most universal necessity — rich, poor, Hindu, Muslim ALL need it
  • Salt Tax was most unjust — taxing a basic survival necessity
  • Visible, symbolic, easily understood by masses
  • Easy to participate — anyone could make salt on any coast
  • No violence required — just law-breaking
  • Could not be countered without extreme repression (bad PR for British)

📊 Mass Participation in CDM

  • 🚺 Women: Massive participation — first time in this scale; picketing shops
  • 👨‍🎓 Students & Youth: Left schools, joined marches
  • 🏕️ Tribes: Central Provinces, Maharashtra, Karnataka
  • 👷 Workers: Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Sholapur
  • 🌾 Peasants: UP, Bihar, Gujarat — No Revenue Campaign
  • 🕌 Muslims: Less than NCM (Muslim League advised against)

📜 Gandhi-Irwin Pact (5 March 1931) — Terms

British Concessions:
  • All non-violent political prisoners released
  • Fines not yet collected — remitted
  • Confiscated land not yet sold — returned
  • Government servants who resigned — lenient treatment
  • Coastal villagers allowed to make salt for domestic use
Congress Concessions:
  • Suspend Civil Disobedience Movement
  • Participate in Round Table Conference (2nd)
  • No demand for inquiry into police excesses
Critics: Gandhi gave up too much. Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, Sukhdev executed March 23, 1931 despite Pact — Gandhi could not save them.
🔥 Chapter 14b — Final Mass Movement

🔥 Quit India Movement (1942) — "Do or Die"

ERA FILE / THE FINAL GREAT MASS UPRISING OF THE FREEDOM STRUGGLE · 1942

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Quit India Movement was launched during the Second World War when Britain refused to grant immediate independence. It demanded an end to British rule and became a broad, decentralised uprising after the arrest of national leaders.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The failure of the August Offer and the Cripps Mission, wartime inflation and shortages, Japanese advances in Southeast Asia and the threat of invasion intensified political urgency. Congress rejected the idea that India should remain in the war without freedom or meaningful consent.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The All India Congress Committee adopted the Quit India resolution in Bombay on 8 August 1942. Gandhi gave the call of “Do or Die”. The next day, major Congress leaders were arrested. Protests, strikes, sabotage of communications and parallel governments emerged in places such as Ballia, Tamluk and Satara. Underground networks, including the Congress Radio associated with Usha Mehta, helped sustain communication.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The British suppressed the movement through mass arrests and force, and it did not immediately end colonial rule. Yet it demonstrated the depth of popular opposition and made the prospect of restoring stable British rule increasingly untenable.
"Here is a mantra, a short one, that I give you. You may imprint it on your hearts and let every breath of yours give expression to it. The mantra is: 'Do or Die.' We shall either free India or die in the attempt." — Mahatma Gandhi, August 8, 1942 — Gowalia Tank, Bombay

ERA FILE / WOMEN AND THE EXPANSION OF POLITICAL PARTICIPATION · 19TH–20TH CENTURIES

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

Women participated in Indian public life as organisers, political thinkers, satyagrahis, revolutionaries, writers, fundraisers, workers and leaders. Their role expanded over time, although access to leadership and participation differed by class, caste, region, religion and education.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Social reform debates, women’s education, print culture and the growth of nationalist organisations created new public opportunities. Anti-partition agitation, swadeshi, Gandhian mass movements and revolutionary networks drew women into political action.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Figures included Sarojini Naidu, Annie Besant, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kripalani, Kasturba Gandhi, Usha Mehta, Madam Bhikaji Cama, Basanti Devi, Durgawati Devi, Kalpana Dutta and Pritilata Waddedar. Women joined picketing, marches, underground communication, relief work and armed revolutionary activity.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Women’s participation challenged colonial authority and gendered restrictions in public life. Yet political mobilisation did not automatically produce gender equality; debates over education, property, representation and social reform continued beyond independence.

⚔️ Indian National Army (INA) — Subhas Chandra Bose

ERA FILE / THE EXTERNAL MILITARY FRONT OF THE FREEDOM STRUGGLE · 1942–1945

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The Indian National Army sought to secure Indian independence through armed struggle in cooperation with powers fighting Britain during the Second World War. Its history involved prisoners of war, expatriate Indian communities, Japanese strategy and competing visions of liberation.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

The fall of Singapore in 1942 brought large numbers of Indian soldiers into Japanese captivity. Mohan Singh helped form the first INA; Rash Behari Bose and the Indian Independence League provided organisational links. Subhas Chandra Bose later rebuilt and led the force.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

Bose reached Southeast Asia in 1943, assumed leadership and proclaimed the Provisional Government of Azad Hind in October 1943. The INA fought alongside Japanese forces in the Imphal-Kohima campaign of 1944. The military campaign failed, but the Red Fort trials of INA officers in 1945–46 generated powerful public sympathy.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · The INA did not militarily liberate India, but its trials and the wider unrest among sections of the armed forces weakened the legitimacy of continued British control. Its legacy remains significant and historically debated.

🏆 Final Chapter

🇮🇳 Road to Independence (1945–1947)

ERA FILE / THE FINAL TRANSFER OF POWER AND PARTITION · 1945–1947

Understanding the Era — Historical Setting & Development

The end of the Second World War accelerated Britain’s withdrawal from India. Independence emerged through mass pressure, constitutional negotiations, political deadlock, communal violence and decisions by British, Congress, Muslim League and other leaders.

HISTORICAL DRIVERS

Britain faced post-war economic weakness and changing international conditions. The INA trials, the Royal Indian Navy uprising (1946), labour unrest, provincial politics and mass mobilisation increased pressure. Congress and the Muslim League remained divided over the constitutional future and the question of Pakistan.

CHRONOLOGY & DEVELOPMENT

The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) proposed a union with a weak centre and provincial groupings; its failure deepened the deadlock. Direct Action Day and subsequent communal violence intensified the crisis. The Mountbatten Plan was announced on 3 June 1947; the Indian Independence Act received royal assent on 18 July. India and Pakistan became independent on 14–15 August 1947, accompanied by Partition and mass displacement.

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE · Independence ended British paramountcy, but Partition caused immense human suffering and reshaped borders, citizenship and state-building. The integration of princely states and the making of the Constitution became urgent tasks for the new Indian Union.
