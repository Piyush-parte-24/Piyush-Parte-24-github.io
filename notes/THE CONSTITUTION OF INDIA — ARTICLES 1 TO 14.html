Article 4 — Supplemental & Consequential Provisions ⚙️

Art. 4(1): Any law made under Art. 2 or 3 must include provisions to amend the First & Fourth Schedule as needed, and may also include supplemental, incidental, and consequential provisions (such as changes to parliamentary or state legislative representation).


Supplemental: Additions needed to fill gaps in the main law (e.g., adjusting schedules).
Meaning: Additional or extra; something that supports or adds to the main provision.

‎
Incidental: Secondary changes naturally arising from the main change (e.g., updating official names or boundaries).
Meaning: Something naturally connected with or arising from a main provision, but not its primary purpose.

‎
Consequential: Follow-up adjustments required as a direct result (e.g., reallocating Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha seats).
Meaning: Changes/modifications (Adjustments) that become necessary because of a particular action/decision
In short: Adjustments needed as a direct consequence of something.

⭐ Golden Rule — Art. 4(2): Such a law is NOT deemed an amendment under Article 368 → passed by simple majority, no State ratification needed! ✂️

Article 239 governs the administration of Union Territories in India. Its core provisions are:


  • Presidential Control: Every Union Territory is administered directly by the President of India, unless Parliament passes a law stating otherwise.

  • Appointed Administrator: The President exercises this power through an appointed administrator (such as a Lieutenant Governor) with a designation the President specifies.

  • Neighboring State Governor: The President can appoint the Governor of a state as the administrator of an adjoining Union Territory.

  • Independent Functioning: When acting as a UT administrator, a Governor operates independently of their state's Council of Ministers.

    How an Acquired Territory Becomes a Union Territory or a State

    The Constitution makes an important distinction between the acquisition of territory, its constitutional status and its subsequent administrative arrangements.

    01 Recognition of Acquired Territory — Article 1(3)(c) Territory of India · Acquisition · Article 366(30)

    Article 1(3) states:

    “The territory of India shall comprise—
    (a) the territories of the States;
    (b) the Union territories specified in the First Schedule; and
    (c) such other territories as may be acquired.”

    Meaning: When India acquires a territory, it becomes part of the territory of India under Article 1(3)(c).

    Article 1(3)(c) recognises the acquired territory as part of India; it does not itself establish a State or provide the administrative machinery for governing it. Read it with Article 366(30): the constitutional definition of “Union territory” includes any other territory comprised within the territory of India but not specified in the First Schedule. Therefore, an acquired territory that is part of India and is not specified in the First Schedule falls within this inclusive definition of a Union territory.

    Article 366(30): “Union territory” means any Union territory specified in the First Schedule and includes any other territory comprised within the territory of India but not specified in that Schedule.
    Keep the functions separate: Article 366(30) is a definition, not a power to acquire territory or a complete code for its administration. Article 239 concerns UT administration; applicable legislation and other constitutional provisions may also be needed to organise governance.
    02 First Step: Acquisition — Article 1(3)(c) Territory of India · Article 366(30) · Berubari Union

    If the acquired territory is to become a Union Territory

    First step: Acquisition — Article 1(3)(c)

    Article 1(3)(c) states:

    “Such other territories as may be acquired.”

    When India acquires foreign territory through its sovereign power, that territory becomes part of the territory of India.

    The Supreme Court explained this principle in In Re: Berubari Union (1960): Article 1(3)(c) recognises territories acquired by India; it does not itself confer the power to acquire them.

    At this stage: The territory belongs to India under Article 1(3)(c). If it is not specified in the First Schedule, it is included in the definition of “Union territory” by Article 366(30). This definition does not itself prescribe every detail of its administration.
    03 Second Step: Understanding Its Constitutional Status Articles 2, 3, 366(30) and 368

    The constitutional definition and the available legislative routes perform different functions:

    • Article 366(30): Defines “Union territory” to include any other territory comprised within India but not specified in the First Schedule. It is a definition, not an acquisition power or a complete administrative code.
    • Article 2: Parliament may admit into the Union or establish new States by law.
    • Article 3: Parliament may form new States and make specified territorial changes, including the UT-related powers covered by its explanations.
    • Article 368: Parliament may amend the Constitution, including the First Schedule, where the constitutional amendment procedure is used or required.
    The crucial distinction is that Article 2 speaks of States, not a general power to create Union Territories. Article 3's explanations also have specific limits. Article 366(30) must be considered separately when examining territory not specified in the First Schedule.
    04 When Can Article 3 and Article 4 Be Used Without Article 368? Explanations I and II · Schedule amendments

    This is where the distinction becomes important.

    Article 3, Explanation I says:

    “In this article, in clauses (a) to (e), ‘State’ includes a Union territory...”

    Therefore, for clauses (a) to (e), the word State also covers Union Territories. Explanation I separately says that, in the proviso, “State” does not include a Union territory.

    Historical note: Explanations I and II were inserted into Article 3 by the Constitution (Eighteenth Amendment) Act, 1966, which came into force on 27 August 1966.

    Parliament can therefore make the specified territorial changes involving UTs through an ordinary law under Article 3. This does not turn Explanation II into a general power to create a separate UT from any acquired foreign territory: Explanation II concerns forming a new State or UT by uniting a part of a State or UT to another State or UT.

    For example:

    • Article 3(b): Increase the area of a State (including a UT).
    • Article 3(c): Diminish its area.
    • Article 3(d): Alter its boundaries.

    Article 3, Explanation II clarifies that the power under clause (a) includes forming a new State or Union Territory by uniting a part of any State or Union Territory to any other State or Union Territory. This is a specific territorial-unification power, not a general standalone power to create a UT from any acquired foreign territory.

    An Article 3 Bill requires the President's recommendation. Where the proviso applies, the President refers the Bill to the Legislature of each affected State for its views within the period specified by the President (or an extended period). The Legislature's views are not binding on Parliament. Because the proviso's “State” excludes a UT, this reference requirement does not apply to a UT as such.

    What does Article 4 do?

    Article 4(1) provides that a law under Article 2 or Article 3 shall contain the provisions necessary to amend the First and Fourth Schedules to give effect to that law. It may also contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions:

    • First Schedule — names and territories of States and UTs.
    • Fourth Schedule — allocation of seats in the Council of States.

    Thus, the necessary Schedule amendments are mandatory where needed to give effect to the law; supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions are permissive.

    Article 4(2) expressly states:

    “No such law as aforesaid shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368.”
    Meaning: When Parliament makes a valid law under Article 2 or Article 3, the necessary changes to the First and Fourth Schedules, together with supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions, can be included in that same law. Under Article 4(2), those changes are not treated as a constitutional amendment for Article 368 purposes.
    05 How Is Acquired Territory Treated as a UT? Existing UT · New UT · Separate recognition

    Consider three situations:

    Situation A: An acquired territory is added to an existing UT

    Article 3 route available

    • Article 1(3)(c): The acquired territory becomes part of India.
    • Article 3(b), read with Explanation I: Parliament can increase the area of an existing UT.
    • Article 4: The necessary First Schedule amendment can be included in the same law without Article 368.

    Situation B: A new UT is formed by combining territory from existing States or UTs

    Article 3 route available

    • Article 3(a), read with Explanations I and II: Parliament can form a new State or UT by uniting a part of any State or UT to any other State or UT, as specified in Explanation II.
    • Article 4: Necessary First Schedule changes can be made through the same law, without Article 368.

    Situation C: Acquired territory is separately recognised as a UT

    Goa precedent

    • Article 1(3)(c): The territory becomes part of India.
    • Article 366(30): Because the acquired territory is part of India and is not specified in the First Schedule, it falls within the inclusive constitutional definition of a UT. This is a definition; it is not itself an acquisition power or a complete administrative scheme.
    • In the Goa example, the Government recorded that the territory was being administered as a UT through the President and an Administrator under Article 239.
    • A constitutional amendment can subsequently include the territory specifically in the First Schedule.

    The 12th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962 specifically listed Goa, Daman and Diu in the First Schedule and amended Article 240. The Government's Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded that they were already being administered as a UT under Article 239 from the date of acquisition. The amendment therefore gave specific Schedule recognition; it should not be described as having created UT status from nothing. It does not establish that an Article 368 amendment is always a prerequisite for UT status.

    06 Actual Example: Goa, Daman and Diu Acquisition · Interim administration · 12th Amendment

    What actually happened?

    20 December 1961 — Acquisition

    India acquired Goa, Daman and Diu on 20 December 1961.

    Under Article 1(3)(c), these territories became part of India. Because they were not yet specified in the First Schedule, Article 366(30)'s inclusive definition of “Union territory” is also relevant. The definition and the arrangements for actual administration are distinct questions.

    Interim administration

    The Government's Statement of Objects and Reasons to the 12th Amendment records that, from the date of acquisition, the territories were being administered as a Union Territory by the President through an Administrator under Article 239. This records the constitutional-administrative position; it should not be confused with the later Ordinance and Act that supplied further legislative arrangements.

    The Government made arrangements for their administration, including the later Goa, Daman and Diu (Administration) Ordinance, 1962, promulgated under Article 123.

    27 March 1962 — 12th Constitutional Amendment

    Enacted on 27 March 1962, the 12th Amendment amended the First Schedule to specifically list Goa, Daman and Diu as a Union Territory and amended Article 240 to include them. It did not create their UT status from scratch: the Government had recorded their administration as a UT under Article 239 from the date of acquisition.

    Section 1(2) of the Amendment stated:

    “It shall be deemed to have come into force on the 20th day of December, 1961.”

    Therefore, the amendment operated retrospectively from 20 December 1961.

    So, what is the answer to the question?

    Article 366(30) and the retrospective operation of the 12th Amendment are both important to understanding the sequence.

    • Article 1(3)(c) recognised the acquired territory as part of India.
    • Article 366(30) includes territory comprised within India but not specified in the First Schedule in the definition of a UT.
    • Interim administrative arrangements were made.
    • The 12th Amendment subsequently gave specific First Schedule recognition to Goa, Daman and Diu as a UT, effective retrospectively from 20 December 1961.
    • Article 239 provides the constitutional framework for UT administration.
    Important: Do not conclude that Article 239 itself converts an acquired territory into a UT. Article 239 concerns administration; Article 366(30) supplies the constitutional definition.

    The Goa example involved acquisition, administration as a UT under Article 239 from the date of acquisition, subsequent legislative arrangements, and specific First Schedule inclusion through the 12th Amendment with retrospective effect from 20 December 1961.

    07 If the Acquired Territory Is to Become a State Article 2 · Article 3 · Article 4

    Article 2 — Admission or establishment of new States

    Article 2 states:

    “Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit.”

    Meaning: Article 2 has two distinct limbs: Parliament may admit a State into the Union or establish a new State, by law and on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. In Berubari Union, the Court discussed Article 2 as one possible route for assimilating acquired foreign territory into the Union. Article 2 is not a general power to establish a Union Territory.

    Berubari Union (1960): Acquired territory and State formation

    In In Re: Berubari Union (1960), the Supreme Court explained that foreign territory acquired by India under the principle reflected in Article 1(3)(c) may be constitutionally assimilated through: (i) Article 2, by establishing a new State; (ii) Article 3(a), by forming a new State through the specified separation or unification of territory; or (iii) Article 3(b), by increasing the area of an existing State. The chosen route depends on the proposed territorial arrangement.

    Scope of the opinion: Berubari primarily concerned the proposed transfer (cession) of Indian territory to Pakistan. Its discussion of Articles 2 and 3(a)/(b) appears in the Court's explanation of how territory newly acquired by India could be assimilated into the Union and a State. The case was not a decision on the creation of a Union Territory.
    Chronology clarification: The Supreme Court delivered the Berubari opinion on 14 March 1960. Article 3 Explanations I and II were inserted later, by the Constitution (Eighteenth Amendment) Act, 1966, which came into force on 27 August 1966. Therefore, Berubari's discussion must be read against the constitutional text as it stood in 1960; it did not rely on the later Explanations.

    Further historical distinction: In paragraph 45, the Court observed that, under the then text of Article 3, Union Territories were outside the scope of clauses (b) to (e). The Court expressly held that UTs were outside clauses (b) to (e), but left open whether they were included in the last limb of clause (a). The Eighteenth Amendment later expressly settled the broader point by inserting Explanation I: “State” includes a UT in clauses (a) to (e), but not in the proviso. The present reach of Article 3 must therefore not be projected backwards onto the 1960 opinion.

    Article 3 deals with the formation of new States and specified territorial changes. As clarified in Berubari Union, acquired territory may also be assimilated through Article 3(a) or Article 3(b), depending on the proposed arrangement.

    Article 4(1) provides that a law made under Article 2 or Article 3 shall contain the provisions necessary to amend the First and Fourth Schedules to give effect to that law. It may also contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions.

    Article 4(2) expressly states:

    “No such law as aforesaid shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368.”

    A law under Article 2 or Article 3 can amend the First Schedule as permitted by Article 4 without following the Article 368 amendment procedure.

    How the acquired territory may be assimilated

    • Article 2: Admit the acquired territory into the Union or constitute it into a new State, as the parliamentary law provides.
    • Article 3(a): Form a new State by the specified separation or unification of territory.
    • Article 3(b): Increase the area of an existing State by adding the acquired territory.

    These alternatives were recognised by the Supreme Court in In Re: Berubari Union (1960). Article 4 permits the necessary Schedule amendments and related provisions in a law validly made under Article 2 or Article 3.

    Do not confuse the routes: Article 2 deals with admission or establishment of States. Article 3 deals with formation and specified reorganisation of States and UTs. Article 4 is consequential; it is not an independent power to acquire or create territory.

    Additional historical illustration: Chandernagore

    Chandernagore, formerly a French possession, came under India's de facto administration on 2 May 1950 and was transferred de jure to India on 9 June 1952. Original Article 243(1) then applied to its administration, and a Presidential regulation under Article 243(2) came into force on 30 June 1952. Parliament passed the Chandernagore (Merger) Act, 1954 on 29 September 1954; it took effect on 2 October 1954, when Chandernagore merged with West Bengal. The Supreme Court discussed this sequence in Berubari as an illustration of acquisition and subsequent assimilation under Article 3. It is a historical illustration, not a universal procedure for every acquired territory.

    08 Important Distinction: Article 3, Explanation II Formation by territorial unification

    Article 3, Explanation II states:

    “The power conferred on Parliament by clause (a) includes the power to form a new State or Union territory by uniting a part of any State or Union territory to any other State or Union territory.”

    Meaning: This provision specifically deals with forming a new State or UT by uniting a part of any State or UT to any other State or UT. It is not a general power to create a standalone UT out of any acquired foreign territory.

    It is not the sole explanation for the status of newly acquired territory. Article 366(30) also defines Union territory.

    11 Interim Administration of Goa — Legal Basis Article 1(3)(c) · Article 123 · Administration legislation

    The interim administration of Goa involved more than a simple reference to Article 239. Constitutional status, executive arrangements and legislative measures must be considered separately.

    The legal framework included Articles 1(3)(c), 366(30), 239 and 123, as well as parliamentary legislation and the 12th Constitutional Amendment.

    1. What was the legal basis?

    After Goa was acquired on 20 December 1961:

    • Article 1(3)(c): Goa became part of the territory of India.
    • Articles 366(30) and 239: The definition of a UT and the framework for its administration are relevant to the arrangements.
    • Article 123(1): The President could promulgate an Ordinance when both Houses were not simultaneously in session, and the President was satisfied that circumstances required immediate action.
    • Goa, Daman and Diu (Administration) Ordinance, 1962: Promulgated on 5 March 1962 to provide for the administration of the acquired territories.
    • Goa, Daman and Diu (Administration) Act, 1962 (Act 1 of 1962): Enacted on 27 March 1962, it replaced the Ordinance and was deemed to have come into force on 5 March 1962.

    The Ordinance identified Article 1(3)(c) in relation to the territories becoming part of India and Article 123(1) as the power under which it was promulgated. Arrangements before the Ordinance must not be attributed to an Ordinance that had not yet been promulgated.

    12 Article 123 and Final Constitutional Understanding Ordinance · Administration Act · 12th Amendment

    2. Why was Article 123 important?

    Article 123(1) states that when both Houses of Parliament are not simultaneously in session, and the President is satisfied that circumstances require immediate action, the President may promulgate an Ordinance.

    Article 123(2) gives such an Ordinance the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but only subject to the Constitution. It ordinarily ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament reassembles; if the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six-week period is reckoned from the later date. It may cease earlier if both Houses pass resolutions disapproving it, or it may be withdrawn by the President at any time.

    The 1962 Administration Ordinance provided a legislative basis for administrative arrangements from its promulgation. It did not itself provide the basis for the earlier period.

    3. What happened afterwards?

    Two important measures received assent on 27 March 1962:

    • Goa, Daman and Diu (Administration) Act, 1962 (Act 1 of 1962): Enacted on 27 March 1962, it replaced the Ordinance and provided for administration and continuity of existing laws. It was deemed to have come into force on 5 March 1962.
    • 12th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962: Specifically inserted Goa, Daman and Diu into the First Schedule as a Union Territory and amended Article 240.

    The 12th Amendment was enacted on 27 March 1962 and, under Section 1(2), was deemed to have come into force on 20 December 1961. Keep the enactment date distinct from its retrospective effective date.

    4. Final constitutional understanding

    20 December 1961 — Acquisition

    Article 1(3)(c): the territory became part of India. Article 366(30): because it was not specified in the First Schedule, it fell within the inclusive definition of a Union territory.

    Interim administrative arrangements

    Executive and UT administrative arrangements; subsequently, Article 123(1) → Administration Ordinance, 1962.

    27 March 1962 — Parliamentary measures

    Goa, Daman and Diu (Administration) Act, 1962 (Act 1 of 1962), enacted on 27 March 1962 and deemed effective from 5 March 1962.

    12th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962, enacted on 27 March 1962 and deemed effective from 20 December 1961.

    The 12th Amendment specifically amended the First Schedule and Article 240.

    Constitutional recognition as a UT

    Article 366(30) defines UT; Article 239 provides the framework for its administration.

    IMPORTANT: Do not interpret Article 239 alone as authorising every aspect of the interim arrangement. The Administration Ordinance under Article 123 was an important subsequent legislative measure. The Government's Statement of Objects and Reasons also recorded that the territories were being administered as a UT under Article 239 before their specific inclusion in the First Schedule.

    13 Constitutional Toolkit for Acquired Territory Articles 1(3)(c), 53, 73, 123, 239, 240, 246(4), 366(30) and 368

    These provisions have different roles in the acquisition, administration and constitutional status of territory. Some concern Union executive action; others concern parliamentary legislation, ordinances, constitutional amendment or the administration of Union Territories.

    Provision Constitutional role
    Article 1(3)(c) Recognises acquired territories as part of the territory of India. It does not itself confer the power to acquire territory or establish a new State.
    Article 366(30) Defines Union territory to include territory comprised within India but not specified in the First Schedule. Specific First Schedule listing is therefore not always a prerequisite to UT status.
    Articles 53, 73 & 74 Article 53 vests the Union's executive power in the President; Article 73 defines its extent; Article 74 provides for the Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President. These provisions form part of the Union executive framework, while Article 239 specifically addresses administration of Union Territories.
    Article 246(4) Empowers Parliament to make laws on any matter for any part of India's territory not included in a State, including matters in the State List. This legislative power remains subject to the Constitution.
    Article 123 Allows the President to promulgate an Ordinance when both Houses of Parliament are not simultaneously in session and the President is satisfied that immediate action is necessary. An Ordinance has the force of an Act of Parliament, subject to Article 123's conditions and limitations.
    Article 368 Provides the constitutional amendment procedure. It was used for the 12th Amendment, which specifically added Goa, Daman and Diu to the First Schedule as a Union Territory and amended Article 240 with retrospective effect.
    Article 239 Provides for Union Territory administration by the President through an Administrator, to the extent specified by the President. It does not itself create or convert territory into a UT.
    Article 240 Permits the President to make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of the Union Territories specified in that Article, subject to its conditions and limitations. It is not a general regulation-making power for every UT.
    Original Article 243 Before its omission by the Seventh Amendment, clause (1) provided for administration of Part D and qualifying unscheduled territory; clause (2) empowered the President to make regulations for its peace and good government. See Dossier 14.

    Executive power and interim administration

    Article 53 vests the Union's executive power in the President. Article 73 defines the extent of that power, subject to the Constitution and laws made by Parliament. Under Article 74, the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (with the constitutionally permitted reconsideration mechanism). Article 239 specifically provides for administration of Union Territories through an Administrator, subject to the Constitution and parliamentary law. Article 246(4), by contrast, concerns Parliament's legislative competence; it is not an executive power.

    Article 123 supplies an ordinance-making power when its constitutional conditions are met. An Ordinance ordinarily ceases six weeks after Parliament reassembles (reckoned from the later reassembly date if the Houses meet on different dates), unless it ceases earlier through disapproval by both Houses or is withdrawn. In the Goa example, the 1962 Administration Ordinance provided a legislative basis for administrative arrangements and was followed by parliamentary legislation.

    Interim arrangements and UT status

    The 12th Amendment is an example of Parliament using the Article 368 procedure to make specific First Schedule provision for an acquired territory. It should not be treated as proof that Article 368 is necessary for every territorial change involving a UT: valid laws under Articles 2 and 3 can make the Schedule changes permitted by Article 4 without following the Article 368 procedure.

    Article 366(30) includes any other territory comprised within India but not specified in the First Schedule in the definition of a UT. Therefore, an Article 368 amendment is not an automatic prerequisite for such territory to fall within the constitutional definition of a UT. The definition does not, by itself, supply every law or administrative arrangement needed to govern it.

    Important distinction: Articles 239, 240 and 368 do not all perform the same function. Article 368 provides an amendment procedure; Article 239 concerns UT administration; and Article 240 concerns regulations for specified UTs. In the Goa example, the Government's Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded administration as a UT under Article 239 before specific First Schedule inclusion, which was later given retrospective effect.
    Remember: Article 1(3)(c) brings acquired territory within the territory of India; Article 366(30) includes unscheduled Indian territory in the definition of a UT; Article 239 concerns UT administration; Article 240 concerns regulations for specified UTs; Article 2 and Article 3 provide State-related legislative routes; Article 4 deals with consequential Schedule changes; Article 123 concerns Ordinances; and Article 368 provides the constitutional amendment procedure.
    14 7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956 — Impact on Acquired Territories Original Article 243 · Articles 239 and 240 · Article 366(30)

    The Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956 changed India's territorial classification and the provisions governing administration. Its impact on acquired territory is best understood by comparing the position before and after 1956.

    Before 1956: Parts A, B, C and D

    The original First Schedule divided constituent territories into Parts A, B, C and D. Different constitutional arrangements applied to those categories.

    Original Article 243 contained two distinct clauses. Clause (1) provided that every territory specified in Part D of the First Schedule, and any other territory comprised within India but not specified in that Schedule, would be administered by the President, to such extent as he thought fit, through a Chief Commissioner or other authority appointed by him. Clause (2) empowered the President to make regulations for the peace and good government of such territory. Those regulations could repeal or amend applicable parliamentary or existing laws and, when promulgated, had the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament applying to that territory.

    Scope: Original Article 243(1) dealt with administration, while clause (2) supplied the special regulation-making power. The provision applied to Part D territories and other territory within India not specified in the First Schedule; it was not a general power to acquire foreign territory.

    The 1956 changes

    The Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956 changed the constitutional classification from the former Part A–D arrangement to States and Union Territories, substituted the relevant constitutional provisions (including Articles 239 and 240), and omitted the original Part IX and its Article 243. The separate States Reorganisation Act, 1956 provided for the territorial reorganisation of States. These were distinct but related measures; both took effect on 1 November 1956.

    • Article 239: Provides the general constitutional framework for administration of Union territories. It is not limited to UTs individually named in Article 239.
    • Article 240: Provides a presidential regulation-making power for the UTs specified in that Article, subject to its conditions.
    • Article 366(30): Defines “Union territory” to include any other territory comprised within India but not specified in the First Schedule. It is a definition, not an independent acquisition power or a complete administrative code.

    After 1956: Status, administration and regulation

    The omission of original Article 243 removed that particular regulation-making provision. Article 240 does not automatically authorise regulations for every acquired territory: the territory must fall within the UTs specified in that Article and the applicable constitutional conditions.

    This does not mean that newly acquired territory necessarily lacked UT status until a later First Schedule amendment. Article 366(30) includes territory comprised within India but not specified in the First Schedule in the definition of a UT. Article 239 concerns administration; it does not itself create the territory's constitutional classification.

    Depending on the circumstances, administration and legislation may involve the Union's executive powers under Articles 53 and 73, UT administration under Article 239, parliamentary legislative competence under Article 246(4), or an Ordinance under Article 123 when its conditions are met. These provisions are not interchangeable and are not a single mandatory sequence. Article 246(4) concerns Parliament's legislative competence, while Article 123 is subject to specific conditions.

    Goa and Puducherry: Constitutional amendments

    The 12th Amendment specifically listed Goa, Daman and Diu in the First Schedule and added it to Article 240, retrospectively from 20 December 1961. The 14th Amendment specifically included Pondicherry, now Puducherry, in the First Schedule and added it to Article 240. These amendments illustrate the constitutional measures chosen in those cases.

    Do not infer a universal Article 368 requirement: These examples do not establish that every newly acquired territory must first be added to the First Schedule by an Article 368 amendment before it can be a UT or be administered under Article 239. Equally, UT status alone does not automatically place a territory within Article 240.
    Article number and chronology: The original Article 243 was omitted by the Seventh Amendment with effect from 1 November 1956. The present Part IX on Panchayats, including the present Article 243 (Definitions), was inserted by the Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act, 1992, with effect from 24 April 1993. The two provisions are entirely different in subject and operation.
    Remember: Before 1956, original Article 243(1) dealt with administration of Part D and qualifying unscheduled territory, while clause (2) supplied the presidential regulation-making power. After its omission, distinguish the definition of a UT under Article 366(30), administration under Article 239, and regulations for specified UTs under Article 240. The present Article 243 on Panchayats dates from 1993.

    Final conceptual flow

    Acquisition → Article 1(3)(c) → Territory becomes part of India.

    If not specified in the First Schedule → Article 366(30) includes it in the definition of a UT → administrative arrangements under Article 239 and applicable laws.

    If a State is to be formed → Article 2, or the relevant Article 3 route (including Article 3(a) or (b), as clarified in Berubari Union) → Article 4 consequential provisions where applicable.

    Specific constitutional change outside Article 4 → Article 368, where required by the nature of the amendment.

    Insight

    Articles 1–4: Admission, Establishment and Formation of States

    Separate a territory’s historical identity from the constitutional process through which it became an Indian State.

    1. The historical question

    What was the territory’s political status before integration: a colony, a princely state or a kingdom?

    2. The constitutional question

    How did it become an Indian State? Was it brought into the Union or formed from territory already within India?

    1. Colony vs. Sovereign or Autonomous Entity

    A territory’s earlier political status explains its history. It does not, by itself, determine the constitutional provision used to make it an Indian State.

    Basis Colony or externally ruled territory Sovereign or autonomous entity
    Control Ruled by an external power; major authority lies with that power. Has its own government or ruler and a distinct political identity. The degree of independence varies.
    Examples Goa under Portugal; Puducherry under France. Hyderabad under the Nizam; Sikkim under the Chogyal.
    Administration Has colonial administrative institutions, but is subordinate to the external power. Has its own political and administrative institutions, although another authority may restrict its powers.
    Relevance to India A former colony may become Indian territory without immediately becoming an Indian State. An existing political entity may be integrated into India, but its particular historical route must be examined.

    Sovereignty and autonomy are different. A fully sovereign State is independent in its external affairs; an autonomous entity has some self-government but may remain subject to another power.

    Hyderabad was subject to British paramountcy before 1947, while Sikkim was an Indian protectorate before becoming an Indian State. Neither should be treated as fully sovereign throughout its history.

    Goa’s position: Goa was under Portuguese colonial rule; Portugal described it as an overseas province. Goa was not a princely state with its own monarch. It came under Indian control in 1961, but did not immediately become an Indian State.

    Important: Colonial or princely status explains historical background. It does not automatically decide which constitutional provision is used to form or admit a State.

    2. Admission vs. Establishment vs. Formation

    Article 2 says Parliament may “admit into the Union, or establish, new States.” Article 3 separately deals with the formation and reorganisation of States.

    Term Simple meaning Illustration
    Admission Bringing an existing political entity into the Indian Union as a State. Sikkim is commonly described as an admission in 1975, subject to the amendment caveat below.
    Establishment Creating a new State; this is one of the terms used in Article 2. Conceptually, the emphasis is on creating a new State rather than receiving an existing one.
    Formation Creating a new State through reorganisation of existing Indian territory under Article 3. Goa was formed from a Union Territory in 1987; Telangana was formed from Andhra Pradesh in 2014.

    Core difference: Admission emphasises the entry of an existing political entity; establishment emphasises the creation of a new State. Formation under Article 3 describes the creation of Goa and Telangana from territory already within India.

    Exam caution: The Constitution does not define admission and establishment through a rigid rule requiring a particular previous status. The word “established” can also describe a State’s creation generally; it does not automatically mean that Article 2 was used.

    3. Constitutional Framework: Articles 1–4

    Article 1(3)(c) · Acquired territories

    India’s territory may include territories that are acquired. This clause does not itself prescribe how acquisition occurs.

    Article 2 · Admission or establishment

    Parliament may admit into the Union, or establish, new States on terms and conditions it thinks fit.

    Article 3 · Formation and reorganisation

    Parliament may form new States and alter existing States’ areas, boundaries or names. A State can also be formed from a Union Territory.

    Article 4 · Consequential changes

    Laws under Articles 2 and 3 can make necessary changes to the First and Fourth Schedules without being treated as constitutional amendments under Article 368.

    Article 3 procedure: A Bill requires the President’s recommendation. If it affects a State’s area, boundaries or name, the President refers it to that State’s legislature for its views within a specified period. Those views are not binding on Parliament.

    Article 4 mechanism: A law under Articles 2 or 3 follows the ordinary legislative process. The consequential changes covered by Article 4 do not require the special amendment procedure under Article 368.

    4. Goa: Colony → Union Territory → State

    1. Before 1961 — Portuguese rule: Goa was under Portuguese colonial administration.
    2. December 1961 — Indian control: Indian forces took control of Goa, Daman and Diu. They became part of India under Article 1(3)(c).
    3. 1961 onwards — Indian administrative control: They were administered as a Union Territory by the President through an Administrator under Article 239.
    4. 1962 — Union Territory: The 12th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962 amended the First Schedule to specifically include Goa, Daman and Diu as a UT.
    5. 1963 — Elected legislature: The Union Territory gained an elected legislature but remained a Union Territory rather than a State.
    6. 30 May 1987 — Statehood: The Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987, particularly Section 3, provided for the formation of the State of Goa. Daman and Diu remained a Union Territory.

    Why is Goa’s 1987 change called formation? Goa was already part of India as a Union Territory. Parliament created the State of Goa from that existing Indian territory. Formation under Article 3 is therefore the clearest description.

    Goa’s previous colonial status explains its historical background. It does not mean that a former colony could never be made a State through another constitutional arrangement.

    4A. Goa: Admission vs. Establishment (Article 2)

    Under Article 2 of the Indian Constitution, Parliament holds two distinct powers: to admit new States into the Union of India, or to establish new States. Goa is classified as an establishment.

    Why Goa Is NOT‎ an “Admission”

    Constitutional meaning: Admission applies strictly when Parliament brings an already-existing, organised, self-governing State into the Union of India.

    Goa’s pre-1987 status: Goa never existed as an independent or constituent State before 1987. It was a foreign colonial possession ruled externally from Lisbon during 1510–1961, and subsequently a centrally governed Indian territory during 1962–1987. Because it lacked statehood, there was no existing “State” to admit.

    Textbook contrast: Sikkim in 1975 is the classic example of admission because it was already an organised, self-governing Himalayan kingdom before joining India through the 36th Constitutional Amendment Act.

    Why Goa IS an “Establishment”

    Constitutional meaning: Establishment refers to creating a brand-new State out of a territory where no statehood previously existed.

    Goa’s transition: Goa had no statehood during its 25 years as a Union Territory. Parliament therefore had to constitute and create a brand-new State government and legislature from scratch through the Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987, making Goa the 25th State of India.

    Goa’s Transition: Acquisition to Statehood
    1. 1961 — Acquisition: Goa was liberated from Portuguese rule through Operation Vijay and absorbed as an acquired territory under Article 1(3)(c).
    2. 1962 — Union Territory: Goa was incorporated into India as a centrally administered Union Territory, alongside Daman and Diu, through the 12th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962.
    3. 1987 — Statehood: Parliament created the State of Goa through the Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987, making Goa the 25th State of India.
    Exam nuance — Article 2 vs. Article 3: Whenever a question contrasts admission and establishment under Article 2, Sikkim = Admission and Goa = Establishment.

    Goa was carved out of an existing Union Territory listed in the First Schedule. Explanation I to Article 3 clarifies that “State” includes a Union Territory. Its elevation to statehood therefore also involves Article 3 alongside Article 2, executed through an ordinary law under Article 4.

    Core Explanations & Precise Constitutional Details


    • Sikkim — Pure Article 2 "Admission" (22nd State):
      • Pre-1975 Status: An external Himalayan monarchy and Indian protectorate with its own Chogyal (monarch) and internal governance.
      • Mechanism: First made an "Associate State" via the 35th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974 (inserting Art. 2A and the 10th Schedule, later repealed), and then admitted as a full constituent State via the 36th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975 (inserting Art. 371F). Because it entered directly as an already-organized State without becoming a UT first, it is the textbook example of Admission.


    • Hyderabad — Conceptual "Admission" (Pre-1950 Integration):
      • Pre-1950 Status: A fully functioning, organized princely state ruled by the Nizam.
      • Mechanism: Integrated into the Dominion of India in September 1948 (Operation Polo) via an Instrument of Accession, before the Constitution came into force on January 26, 1950. It entered the original First Schedule directly as a Part B State. Conceptually, it represents Admission of an existing state rather than creating one from scratch.


    • Goa — "Establishment" from Colonial Enclave to UT to State (25th State):
      • Pre-1987 Status: A Portuguese overseas colony (1510–1961), liberated via Operation Vijay (Dec 19, 1961) as an acquired territory under Article 1(3)(c), and incorporated as a centrally governed Union Territory via the 12th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962.
      • Mechanism: Because Goa held no statehood during its 25 years as a UT, Parliament established a brand-new State via the Goa, Daman and Diu Reorganisation Act, 1987 (effective May 30, 1987), separating Goa as a State while retaining Daman and Diu as a UT.


    • Himachal Pradesh (18th State), Manipur (19th State) & Tripura (20th State) — "Establishment" from Part C / UTs:
      • Pre-Statehood Status: Merged princely states (1948–49) \rightarrow Centrally administered Part C States (1950) \rightarrow Converted into Union Territories on November 1, 1956 by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 and 7th Constitutional Amendment Act.
      • Mechanism: Elevated to full statehood via ordinary parliamentary statutes—the State of Himachal Pradesh Act, 1970 (effective Jan 25, 1971) and the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971 (effective Jan 21, 1972).


    • Mizoram (23rd State) & Arunachal Pradesh (24th State) — "Establishment" from Carved-Out UTs:
      • Pre-Statehood Status: Originally tribal districts/NEFA within Assam, they were first separated and constituted as Union Territories in 1972 via the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971 and the 27th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971.
      • Mechanism: Because they lacked statehood as UTs, Parliament subsequently established them as full-fledged States in February 1987 via the State of Mizoram Act, 1986 and the State of Arunachal Pradesh Act, 1986.
    Auxiliary Constitutional Insights for Exams


    • Why UT-to-State Elevations Trigger Both Article 2 and Article 3:
      • Article 2 ("Establish"): A Union Territory is a non-state federal unit under direct Union administration (Part VIII, Art. 239). Granting statehood creates a new constituent State legislature and executive where none existed.
      • Article 3(a) & Explanation I: Because UTs are already part of the territory of India listed in the First Schedule, and Explanation I to Article 3 explicitly defines "State" in clauses (a)–(e) to include a Union Territory, altering a UT's status also operates under Article 3.


    • The Article 4 Shortcut (Simple Majority):
      • Under Article 4(2), laws passed under Articles 2 and 3 are not treated as Constitutional Amendments under Article 368. Parliament passes them by a simple majority (more than 50% of members present and voting) to amend the First Schedule (territories) and Fourth Schedule (Rajya Sabha seats).
      • Exception (Sikkim, 1975): The 36th Amendment used Article 368 only because Parliament simultaneously inserted Article 371F (special constitutional provisions for Sikkim), not merely to admit the territory.


    • Acquired Foreign Territories That Remained UTs (Article 1(3)(c)):
      • Dadra & Nagar Haveli (liberated 1954; 10th Amendment, 1961), Goa, Daman & Diu (12th Amendment, 1962), and Puducherry (ceded by France in 1954, ratified 1962; 14th Amendment, 1962) all entered India first as Union Territories, never directly as States. Only Goa later graduated to statehood in 1987.


    • Pure Article 3 Internal Splits (Neither Article 2 Admission nor Establishment):
      • States carved out of existing full-fledged States—such as Maharashtra & Gujarat (1960), Nagaland (1963), Haryana (1966), Meghalaya (1972), Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand & Jharkhand (2000), and Telangana (2014)—are pure Article 3 internal reorganizations, not Article 2 admissions or establishments.

    🛡️ Article 13 — Laws Inconsistent With or in Derogation of Fundamental Rights

    If Article 12 tells us who the State is, Article 13 is the enforcement sword ⚔️ — it gives Part III real teeth by striking down any law, past or future, that clashes with your Fundamental Rights. It is rightly called the "Guardian of Fundamental Rights" and is the constitutional root of Judicial Review.

    13(1)

    Pre-Constitution Laws 📜

    All laws in force in India immediately before commencement, insofar as inconsistent with Part III, are void to the extent of that inconsistency.

    ⚠️ Important nuance: such a law is not void ab initio — it was perfectly valid when made. It only becomes void prospectively, from 26 January 1950 onward. This is why the Doctrine of Eclipse (below) can apply to it (Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay, 1951).

    13(2)

    Post-Constitution Laws 🚫

    The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges Part III rights. Any law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contravention.

    ⚠️ Here, "void" means void ab initio — the law is still-born, a nullity from the moment of its enactment (Deep Chand v. State of U.P.). The Doctrine of Eclipse does not revive it.

    13(3)

    Definitions 📖

    • (a) "Law" includes Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law.
    • (b) "Laws in force" includes laws passed before commencement even if not then in operation everywhere, provided not previously repealed.
    13(4)

    The Amendment Exception 🏛️

    Inserted by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971: "Nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment of this Constitution made under Article 368." A direct legislative response to Golaknath (see timeline below).

    🧠 The Doctrine Toolbox of Article 13

    🌘 Doctrine of Eclipse

    A pre-constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right isn't dead — it's eclipsed / dormant. It can spring back to life if the relevant Right is later amended to remove the inconsistency (Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P.). Applies only to laws valid when made — not to post-Constitution void laws.

    ✂️ Doctrine of Severability

    Only the part of a statute inconsistent with Part III is struck down — the rest survives if separable (A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras, 1950).

    🙅 Doctrine of Waiver — Rejected

    A citizen cannot waive a Fundamental Right, since these rights serve public policy, not just private benefit (Basheshar Nath v. CIT, 1959; Behram Pesikaka v. State of Bombay).

    🕰️ Prospective Overruling

    The Court may declare a new legal position to operate only from a future date, to avoid unsettling past transactions (used in Golaknath, 1967).

    🧪 Reading Down

    Where a provision admits multiple interpretations, courts prefer the constitutionally compatible one over an outright strike-down.

    🔬 Presumption of Constitutionality

    Legislation is presumed valid; the burden lies on the challenger to prove a clear constitutional violation.

    ⚔️ The Great Amendment Battle — Case Law Timeline

    1950
    A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras — Narrow, compartmentalised reading of Fundamental Rights (later overruled by Maneka Gandhi).
    1951
    Shankari Prasad v. Union of India — A constitutional amendment under Art.368 is not "law" under Art.13(2) — Parliament CAN amend Fundamental Rights.
    1965
    Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan — Reaffirmed Shankari Prasad.
    1967
    I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (11-judge bench) — Reversed course: "law" in Art.13(2) includes constitutional amendments; Parliament cannot abridge Fundamental Rights, even by amendment (applied prospectively).
    1971

    🎬 Act III · The First Right

    ⚡ Article 14 — Equality Before Law & Equal Protection of Laws

    The very first Fundamental Right of Part III — the cornerstone of Indian equality jurisprudence. 🏛️✨

    "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India."

    🌓 The Two Limbs of Article 14

    ⚖️ Equality Before LawNegative concept 🇬🇧
    British origin — Dicey's Rule of Law
    No one above law; no special privileges
    🤝
    🛡️ Equal Protection of LawsPositive concept 🇺🇸
    American origin — 14th Amendment
    Equal treatment in equal circumstances; permits affirmative classification

    📚 Dicey's Rule of Law — 3 Pillars

    ⚖️ RULE OF LAW (A.V. Dicey)
    👑 Supremacy of Law
    (absence of arbitrary power)
    🤝 Equality Before Law
    📖 Predominance of Legal Spirit
    (rights secured via ordinary courts)
    🌍 Who is protected? Article 14 applies to "any person" — citizens AND foreigners, natural persons AND juristic persons like companies. This is broader than Articles 15, 16 & 19, which protect only citizens. 🧍🏢🌐

    🚫 Exceptions & Limitations

    👑 Art. 361 Immunity

    President & Governors are not answerable to courts for official acts; immune from criminal proceedings during term; civil proceedings need prior notice.

    🌐 Diplomatic Immunity

    Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors & diplomats enjoy immunity under international law and statute.

    📜 Article 31-C (Partial) Shield

    Inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971): laws implementing Art.39(b)/(c) get some protection from Art.14/19 challenges — but its blanket extension to all DPSPs was struck down in Minerva Mills (1980).

    🧪 The Twin Test — Doctrine of Reasonable Classification

    Article 14 forbids class legislation but permits reasonable classification — laid down in State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952) and elaborated in Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1959).

    1️⃣ Intelligible Differentia
    A real, discernible distinction separating the grouped persons/things from those left out
    ➕
    2️⃣ Rational Nexus
    The differentia must relate rationally to the object of the law
    =
    ✅ VALID CLASSIFICATION
    (both conditions must be satisfied together)
    📌 Ram Krishna Dalmia laid down a strong presumption of constitutionality in favour of legislation, placing the burden of proof on the party alleging discrimination.

    🔥 The New Dimension — Doctrine Against Arbitrariness

    1974
    E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu — Justice Bhagwati: "Equality is a dynamic concept... Article 14 strikes at arbitrariness in State action. Equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies." 🔥
    1978
    Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India — Articles 14, 19 & 21 read together as a "Golden Triangle." "Procedure established by law" under Art.21 must now be fair, just & reasonable, satisfying Art.14 too.
    1992
    Indra Sawhney v. Union of India — Mandal Commission case; examined reservation, the 50% ceiling, and the "creamy layer" concept under Arts.14/16.
    2017
    Shayara Bano v. Union of India (Triple Talaq case) — Extended "manifest arbitrariness" to strike down legislation itself, not just executive action.

    🌟 Final Takeaway 🌟

    🗺️ Articles 1–4 establish what India is territorially: an indestructible Union built of freely reorganisable States, changed by simple majority — except when ceding territory to a foreign nation, which demands full Article 368 rigour (Berubari, 1960).

    🪪 Articles 5–11 establish who belonged to India at the Republic's birth, then hand all future control to Parliament's plenary power (Article 11) — the foundation of the Citizenship Act, 1955 and CAA, 2019.

    ⚖️ Article 12 opens the door to Part III by defining "the State."

    🛡️ Article 13 arms Part III with real enforcement power — voiding any law, past or future, that abridges a Fundamental Right, tempered and perfected by the Basic Structure Doctrine.

    ⚡ Article 14 begins the substantive journey of rights themselves — equality before law, equal protection, reasonable classification, and the powerful modern doctrine against arbitrary State action. 🚀📚
    "Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity" — the eternal promise of the Preamble, now walked through Articles 1 to 14
