🎬 Act I · Scene 1

🗺️ The Union and Its Territory (Articles 1–4)

India's territorial personality: an indestructible Union sculpted from destructible, ever-adaptable States. 🧩

Article 1 — Name and Territory of the Union 🇮🇳

"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."
❌ NOT formed by agreement between pre-existing sovereign States
➡️
❌ No State has a right to secede
➡️
🔒 INDESTRUCTIBLE UNIONbuilt of ✂️ destructible, reorganisable States

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar deliberately chose "Union" over "Federation" to signal these two ideas. Internal State boundaries remain freely alterable by Parliament under Article 3 — a deliberate design choice favouring adaptability over rigidity. 🔧

🌍 Article 1(3) — Three Components of "Territory of India"

🌏 TERRITORY OF INDIA(wider than "Union of India")
🏘️ Territories of the States
🏙️ Union Territories (First Schedule)
🎁 Territories that may be Acquired (cession, treaty, purchase, exchange — Goa, Puducherry, Sikkim)
ConceptScope
🌐 Territory of IndiaWider term — includes future acquisitions even before formal integration
🏛️ Union of IndiaNarrower term — States, UTs, and the actual governmental machinery only
Feature🏗️ Federation (e.g. USA)🏗️ Union of States (India)
FormationBy agreement between sovereign StatesBy administrative division from one source
Right to secedeStates may retain this rightStates have zero right to secede 🔒
Source of powerCentre derives power from StatesCentre is the source; States are its creations
📌 This makes India a "holding-together federation" — a Centre comparatively stronger than in classical federations.
2️⃣

Article 2 — Admission or Establishment of New States 🆕

"Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit."

🟢 ADMIT existing territory INTO the Union
🟣 ESTABLISH a brand-new State from scratch

Concerns territory from outside the existing Union — a plenary power (e.g. Sikkim's integration via the 35th & 36th Amendments). ⚡

3️⃣

Article 3 — Formation & Alteration of States ✂️

  • (a) Form new State by separation/uniting States or parts
  • (b) Increase area of any State
  • (c) Diminish area of any State
  • (d) Alter boundaries of any State
  • (e) Alter name of any State
4️⃣

Article 4 — Supplemental & Consequential Provisions ⚙️

Any Art.2/3 law amends the First & Fourth Schedules and covers representation, assets, staff, laws, and High Court adjustments.

⭐ Golden Rule — Art. 4(2): Such a law is NOT deemed an amendment under Article 368 → passed by simple majority, no State ratification needed! ✂️

🏛️ Procedure Under Article 3

📝 Bill drafted
⬇️
🖋️ MUST have President's prior recommendation
⬇️
📩 President refers Bill to affected State Legislature (only if area/boundary/name affected)
⬇️
🗣️ State gives views within specified time
⬇️
⚠️ Views are CONSULTATIVE only — NOT binding
⬇️
✅ Parliament may proceed regardless (simple majority)
🔁 If the Bill is later amended so as to further affect the State, a fresh reference may be required.
🧾 Explanations to Art. 3: In clauses (a)–(e), "State" includes a UT ✅ — but in the proviso (legislature reference), "State" excludes a UT ❌.
🗓️ Illustrative reorganisations: Andhra State (1953) → States Reorganisation Act (1956) → Maharashtra/Gujarat (1960) → Punjab/Haryana (1966) → Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Uttarakhand (2000) → Telangana (2014) → J&K into two UTs (2019) — all WITHOUT touching Article 368! 🎯

⚖️ The Berubari Union Case (1960) — Judicial Landmark

❓ Can Article 3 CEDE Indian territory to a FOREIGN country?
⬇️
🚫 NO — said the Supreme Court
⬇️
✅ Requires a full Article 368 amendment
9th Amendment (1960) — Berubari transfer
100th Amendment (2015) — India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement
🎬 Act I · Scene 2

🪪 Citizenship (Articles 5–11)

A response to the trauma of Partition — fixing who became a citizen at the Republic's birth while permanently handing future control to Parliament. 📸

📍 1 Mar 1947 — Art.7 cut-off (India → Pakistan)
⬇️
📍 19 Jul 1948 — Art.6 cut-off (permit system begins)
⬇️
📍 26 Jan 1950 — Constitution commences, citizenship "snapshot" taken 📸
5️⃣

Art. 5 🏠 At Commencement

A person domiciled in India became a citizen if: (a) born in India, OR (b) either parent born in India, OR (c) ordinarily resident for ≥5 years before commencement.

6️⃣

Art. 6 🚂 Pakistan → India

Migrated + born (or parent/grandparent born) in undivided India:

  • Before 19 Jul 1948 + resident since ➜ Citizen ✅
  • On/after 19 Jul 1948 + registered + 6 months' residence ➜ Citizen ✅
7️⃣

Art. 7 🚪 India → Pakistan

Migration after 1 Mar 1947 generally disqualified Art.5/6 claims ❌ — except those who returned under a valid resettlement permit, who could qualify under Art.6's post-1948 rules ✅.

8️⃣

Art. 8 🌍 Indians Abroad

Born (or parent/grandparent born) in undivided India, residing abroad → could be registered by an Indian diplomatic/consular representative.

9️⃣

Art. 9 🚫 Foreign Citizenship Bar

Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship bars/ends claims under Arts 5, 6 or 8 — the constitutional basis of India's single citizenship principle.

🔟

Art. 10 ♾️ Continuance

Citizenship isn't frozen at 1950 — it continues, subject to laws Parliament may make.

1️⃣1️⃣

Article 11 👑 Parliament's Plenary Power

"Nothing... shall derogate from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship."

Foundation of the Citizenship Act, 1955 and its amendments, including the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019.

🔄 Unified Citizenship Flow

👶 Person exists at/around 1950
⬇️
🏠 Born in India (Art.5) / 🚶 Migrated from Pakistan (Art.6) / 🚪 Migrated to Pakistan (Art.7) / 🌍 Living Abroad (Art.8)
⬇️
🪙 CITIZEN OF INDIA
⬇️
Voluntarily acquires FOREIGN citizenship? (Art.9) — ✅ YES → 🚫 VOID | ❌ NO → ♾️ Continues (Art.10)
⬇️
👑 All future rules → PARLIAMENT (Art.11) → Citizenship Act 1955 / CAA 2019
🎬 Act II · The Gateway Opens

⚖️ Article 12 — Definition of "State"

The gateway to Part III — before claiming a right was violated, we must ask: violated by whom? 🚪

"In this Part, unless the context otherwise requires, 'the State' includes the Government and Parliament of India and the Government and the Legislature of each of the States and all local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India."
🚪 ARTICLE 12Who can be held to violate Fundamental Rights?
🏛️ Govt + Parliament of India
🏢 State Govt + Legislature
🏘️ Local Authorities (municipalities, panchayats, port trusts)
🏢 "Other" Authorities (via judicial tests)

🔍 Doctrinal Evolution of "Other Authorities"

1967
Rajasthan SEB v. Mohan Lal — "Other authorities" = bodies created by statute with statutory powers.
1979 / 1981
R.D. Shetty v. IAAI / Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib — Instrumentality/Agency Test: deep financial aid, pervasive government control, monopoly status, public function, departmental transfer to a corporate body.
2002
Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. IICB (7-judge bench) — Refined test: must show DEEP, PERVASIVE, FUNCTIONAL control by government — mere regulation is not enough.
🔑 This test determines whether bodies like LIC, ONGC, or CSIR fall within Article 12 as instrumentalities of the State.
🧑‍⚖️ A subtler question — is the Judiciary "the State"? Courts performing purely judicial/adjudicatory functions have generally been treated differently from Article 12's core list (see Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra), whereas the judiciary's administrative actions (framing rules, service matters) are accepted as falling within Article 12. This remains a nuanced, debated corner of doctrine. 📚
🎬 Act II · Scene 2 — The Guardian

🛡️ Article 13 — Laws Inconsistent With or in Derogation of Fundamental Rights

If Article 12 tells us who the State is, Article 13 is the enforcement sword ⚔️ — it gives Part III real teeth by striking down any law, past or future, that clashes with your Fundamental Rights. It is rightly called the "Guardian of Fundamental Rights" and is the constitutional root of Judicial Review.

13(1)

Pre-Constitution Laws 📜

All laws in force in India immediately before commencement, insofar as inconsistent with Part III, are void to the extent of that inconsistency.

⚠️ Important nuance: such a law is not void ab initio — it was perfectly valid when made. It only becomes void prospectively, from 26 January 1950 onward. This is why the Doctrine of Eclipse (below) can apply to it (Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay, 1951).

13(2)

Post-Constitution Laws 🚫

The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges Part III rights. Any law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contravention.

⚠️ Here, "void" means void ab initio — the law is still-born, a nullity from the moment of its enactment (Deep Chand v. State of U.P.). The Doctrine of Eclipse does not revive it.

13(3)

Definitions 📖

  • (a) "Law" includes Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law.
  • (b) "Laws in force" includes laws passed before commencement even if not then in operation everywhere, provided not previously repealed.
13(4)

The Amendment Exception 🏛️

Inserted by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971: "Nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment of this Constitution made under Article 368." A direct legislative response to Golaknath (see timeline below).

🧠 The Doctrine Toolbox of Article 13

🌘 Doctrine of Eclipse

A pre-constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right isn't dead — it's eclipsed / dormant. It can spring back to life if the relevant Right is later amended to remove the inconsistency (Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P.). Applies only to laws valid when made — not to post-Constitution void laws.

✂️ Doctrine of Severability

Only the part of a statute inconsistent with Part III is struck down — the rest survives if separable (A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras, 1950).

🙅 Doctrine of Waiver — Rejected

A citizen cannot waive a Fundamental Right, since these rights serve public policy, not just private benefit (Basheshar Nath v. CIT, 1959; Behram Pesikaka v. State of Bombay).

🕰️ Prospective Overruling

The Court may declare a new legal position to operate only from a future date, to avoid unsettling past transactions (used in Golaknath, 1967).

🧪 Reading Down

Where a provision admits multiple interpretations, courts prefer the constitutionally compatible one over an outright strike-down.

🔬 Presumption of Constitutionality

Legislation is presumed valid; the burden lies on the challenger to prove a clear constitutional violation.

⚔️ The Great Amendment Battle — Case Law Timeline

1950
A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras — Narrow, compartmentalised reading of Fundamental Rights (later overruled by Maneka Gandhi).
1951
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India — A constitutional amendment under Art.368 is not "law" under Art.13(2) — Parliament CAN amend Fundamental Rights.
1965
Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan — Reaffirmed Shankari Prasad.
1967
I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (11-judge bench) — Reversed course: "law" in Art.13(2) includes constitutional amendments; Parliament cannot abridge Fundamental Rights, even by amendment (applied prospectively).
1971
24th Constitutional Amendment — Inserted Art.13(4) and amended Art.368, nullifying Golaknath and restoring Parliament's power to amend any part of the Constitution.
1973
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (13-judge bench) — Upheld the 24th Amendment BUT propounded the historic Basic Structure Doctrine: Parliament cannot destroy the Constitution's basic structure even via Art.368. 🌟
1980
Minerva Mills v. Union of India — Struck down 42nd Amendment clauses granting unlimited amending power; reaffirmed limited amending power, judicial review, and FR–DPSP harmony as basic structure.
💡 Insight: Article 13(4) removes constitutional amendments from Article 13's literal reach — but the Basic Structure Doctrine ensures amendments stay subject to judicial review whenever they damage the Constitution's core identity. ⚔️🌟
📌 Fact: Article 372 separately provides for the general continuance of pre-Constitution laws — Article 13(1) is the special provision that specifically voids those inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.

🎬 Act III · The First Right

⚡ Article 14 — Equality Before Law & Equal Protection of Laws

The very first Fundamental Right of Part III — the cornerstone of Indian equality jurisprudence. 🏛️✨

"The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India."

🌓 The Two Limbs of Article 14

⚖️ Equality Before LawNegative concept 🇬🇧
British origin — Dicey's Rule of Law
No one above law; no special privileges
🤝
🛡️ Equal Protection of LawsPositive concept 🇺🇸
American origin — 14th Amendment
Equal treatment in equal circumstances; permits affirmative classification

📚 Dicey's Rule of Law — 3 Pillars

⚖️ RULE OF LAW (A.V. Dicey)
👑 Supremacy of Law
(absence of arbitrary power)
🤝 Equality Before Law
📖 Predominance of Legal Spirit
(rights secured via ordinary courts)
🌍 Who is protected? Article 14 applies to "any person" — citizens AND foreigners, natural persons AND juristic persons like companies. This is broader than Articles 15, 16 & 19, which protect only citizens. 🧍🏢🌐

🚫 Exceptions & Limitations

👑 Art. 361 Immunity

President & Governors are not answerable to courts for official acts; immune from criminal proceedings during term; civil proceedings need prior notice.

🌐 Diplomatic Immunity

Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors & diplomats enjoy immunity under international law and statute.

📜 Article 31-C (Partial) Shield

Inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971): laws implementing Art.39(b)/(c) get some protection from Art.14/19 challenges — but its blanket extension to all DPSPs was struck down in Minerva Mills (1980).

🧪 The Twin Test — Doctrine of Reasonable Classification

Article 14 forbids class legislation but permits reasonable classification — laid down in State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952) and elaborated in Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1959).

1️⃣ Intelligible Differentia
A real, discernible distinction separating the grouped persons/things from those left out
➕
2️⃣ Rational Nexus
The differentia must relate rationally to the object of the law
=
✅ VALID CLASSIFICATION
(both conditions must be satisfied together)
📌 Ram Krishna Dalmia laid down a strong presumption of constitutionality in favour of legislation, placing the burden of proof on the party alleging discrimination.

🔥 The New Dimension — Doctrine Against Arbitrariness

1974
E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu — Justice Bhagwati: "Equality is a dynamic concept... Article 14 strikes at arbitrariness in State action. Equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies." 🔥
1978
Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India — Articles 14, 19 & 21 read together as a "Golden Triangle." "Procedure established by law" under Art.21 must now be fair, just & reasonable, satisfying Art.14 too.
1992
Indra Sawhney v. Union of India — Mandal Commission case; examined reservation, the 50% ceiling, and the "creamy layer" concept under Arts.14/16.
2017
Shayara Bano v. Union of India (Triple Talaq case) — Extended "manifest arbitrariness" to strike down legislation itself, not just executive action.

🔺 The Golden Triangle

💡 Insight: Post-Maneka Gandhi, any law depriving personal liberty must satisfy Articles 14, 19 and 21 together — the beating heart of modern Indian constitutional law. 💛🔺
📌 Fact: Article 14 does not demand identical treatment for all — "equals should be treated equally, and unequals cannot be treated equally." Special provisions like Arts. 15(3)/(4)/(5) and 16(4) are facets of substantive, not merely formal, equality. ⚖️✨

🌟 Final Takeaway 🌟

🗺️ Articles 1–4 establish what India is territorially: an indestructible Union built of freely reorganisable States, changed by simple majority — except when ceding territory to a foreign nation, which demands full Article 368 rigour (Berubari, 1960).

🪪 Articles 5–11 establish who belonged to India at the Republic's birth, then hand all future control to Parliament's plenary power (Article 11) — the foundation of the Citizenship Act, 1955 and CAA, 2019.

⚖️ Article 12 opens the door to Part III by defining "the State."

🛡️ Article 13 arms Part III with real enforcement power — voiding any law, past or future, that abridges a Fundamental Right, tempered and perfected by the Basic Structure Doctrine.

⚡ Article 14 begins the substantive journey of rights themselves — equality before law, equal protection, reasonable classification, and the powerful modern doctrine against arbitrary State action. 🚀📚
"Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity" — the eternal promise of the Preamble, now walked through Articles 1 to 14
