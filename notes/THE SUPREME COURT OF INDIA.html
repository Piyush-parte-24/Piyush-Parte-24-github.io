🕰️ PART I — HISTORICAL EVOLUTION & CONSTITUTIONAL PHILOSOPHY

🎯 The Central Idea

The Supreme Court of India is the apex court of a single, integrated judiciary — an unusual design choice for a federation. Unlike the USA (where federal and state courts run in parallel), India adopted the integrated model of the Government of India Act, 1935: one pyramid, with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts below, and district/subordinate courts at the base. One hierarchy, one body of law, one final voice. 🏛️

Its DNA is a hybrid: 🇬🇧 British procedure & the writ tradition · 🇺🇸 American judicial review & a written Constitution · 🇮🇪 Irish Directive Principles · 🇨🇦 Canadian federal balance — all fused with an indigenous commitment to social transformation.

📜 Timeline — From Colonial Charter to Constitutional Guardian

1726

⚓ Mayor's Courts — Charter of 1726 i

📖 Basis: Royal Charter of George I establishing Mayor's Courts in Calcutta, Madras & Bombay.

🌟 Why it matters: First courts deriving authority from the Crown, not the Company — the seed of Crown jurisdiction in India.

↗️ Appeals: Lay to the Governor-in-Council, then to the King-in-Council (Privy Council).

1774

🏛️ Supreme Court at Fort William, Calcutta

📖 Legal Basis: Regulating Act, 1773 → Charter of 26 March 1774.

👨‍⚖️ Bench: Chief Justice Sir Elijah Impey + 3 puisne judges, appointed by the Crown.

🌟 Significance: India's first Court of Record with civil, criminal, admiralty & ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

⚠️ Limitation: Created a dual judiciary — Crown's Supreme Court vs the Company's Adalats — with constant conflict (famously the Raja Nandkumar and Patna cases), partly cured by the Act of Settlement, 1781.

👑 Crown Court⚔️ Jurisdictional Conflict
1801
1823

🌊 Supreme Courts of Madras (1801) & Bombay (1823)

📖 Basis: Royal Charters under George III (Madras charter issued 1800, court opened 1801).

🏗️ Structure: Mirror images of the Calcutta Supreme Court, replacing the Recorder's Courts.

💰 Context: Driven by the commercial expansion of the Presidency towns.

📉 Impact: Three parallel apex courts — no single final Indian authority; all roads led to the Privy Council in London.

1861

🔗 Indian High Courts Act, 1861 i

🔄 Transition: Abolished Supreme Courts + Sadar Diwani/Nizamat Adalats; created High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay & Madras (all 1862), Allahabad (1866).

✅ Result: One unified judicial administration per Presidency — the architecture we still use.

🌟 Legacy: These High Courts survive today under Article 214 — India's oldest continuous constitutional institutions.

1937

🏗️ Federal Court of India (1 Oct 1937)

📖 Basis: Government of India Act, 1935 (s.200). Sat in the Chamber of Princes, Parliament House, Delhi.

👨‍⚖️ Strength: Started with a Chief Justice + 2 judges (Sir Maurice Gwyer first CJ; Sir Harilal Kania the bridge to 1950).

⚖️ Jurisdiction: Original (federal disputes), appellate (constitutional questions from High Courts), advisory (Governor-General's reference).

⛓️ Limitation: Not final — appeals still lay to the Privy Council until the Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949 (effective 10 Oct 1949).

📅 Constituted: 26 January 1950 · 🎩 Inaugural sitting: 28 January 1950, Chamber of Princes, Parliament House.

👨‍⚖️ First CJI: Justice Harilal Jekisundas Kania (1950–51).

🔢 Original strength: 8 (CJI + 7) — Art. 124(1) allowed Parliament to increase it.

🏛️ Present building: Occupied in 1958 — Tilak Marg, New Delhi; designed by Ganesh Bhikaji Deolalikar in the shape of scales of justice ⚖️.

🧭 Constitutional Philosophy — Six Pillars of the Court's Self-Image

🛡️
Guardian of the Constitution

Ultimate custodian of constitutional values; the Constitution is supreme, not Parliament (Kesavananda).

🔍
Final Interpreter

Art. 141 makes its declaration of law binding on all courts — “not final because infallible, but infallible because final.”

🕊️
Protector of Rights

Art. 32 makes access to it a Fundamental Right itself — the “soul of the Constitution.”

⚖️
Federal Umpire

Art. 131 gives it exclusive power over Union–State and inter-State disputes.

🌱
Engine of Social Change

Transformative constitutionalism — PIL, socio-economic rights, dignity jurisprudence.

🧭
Constitutional Morality

Ambedkar's phrase revived in Naz/Navtej & Sabarimala: the Constitution's values override majoritarian morality.

If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important — an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity — I could not refer to any other article except Article 32. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it. 💗 — Dr. B. R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 9 December 1948
The Supreme Court of India is perhaps the most powerful court in the world — in terms of the range of its jurisdiction, the scope of its powers of judicial review, and the creativity of its jurisprudence. 🌍 — Comparative constitutional scholarship, widely echoed

⚙️ How a Supreme Court judge is actually appointed today

1️⃣ Initiation: The CJI-led Collegium (CJI + 4 senior-most judges) recommends names.
2️⃣ Consultation: Views of the senior-most judge from the candidate's parent High Court are taken; IB inputs sought by the Government.
3️⃣ Transmission: Recommendation goes to the Law Ministry → PM → President.
4️⃣ Return power: The Government may return a file once with objections.
5️⃣ Reiteration: If the Collegium reiterates unanimously, the appointment is binding — but there is no constitutional deadline, which is the source of endless “file-sitting” friction. ⏳
6️⃣ Warrant: President signs the warrant under Art. 124(2); oath in the Third Schedule form.

🧨 Why the NJAC fell (2015) — and the criticism it faces

⚖️ Ratio: Judicial primacy in appointments is part of the independence of the judiciary, itself a basic feature. The Law Minister's presence + two “eminent persons” with a veto of any two members violated it.
🗣️ The dissent: Justice J. Chelameswar called the Collegium's opacity “the best kept secret in the country.”
📢 Counter-criticism: No eligibility criteria, no secretariat, no reasons recorded, “judges appointing judges”, and poor social diversity.
✅ Reform steps taken: Since Oct 2017 the Court publishes Collegium resolutions online; since 1 April 2025 a Full Court resolution requires public declaration of judges' assets on the SC website. 🔓

🗺️ PART III — THE JURISDICTION MAP: SEVEN DOORS TO THE APEX COURT

🏛️ 1. ORIGINAL — FEDERAL i

📌 Art. 131 — Union v. State(s); State v. State.

✅ Must involve a legal right, not a political grievance.

🚫 Excluded: pre-Constitution treaties/agreements still in operation; inter-State water disputes (Art. 262 + ISWD Act, 1956); matters referred to the Finance Commission; Art. 280/290 adjustments.

📚 State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977); State of W.B. v. Union of India (1963); State of Jharkhand v. State of Bihar.

🕊️ 2. ORIGINAL — WRIT / RIGHTS

📌 Art. 32 — enforcement of Fundamental Rights is itself a Fundamental Right.

✍️ Five writs: Habeas Corpus 🔓, Mandamus 📢, Prohibition ✋, Certiorari 📄, Quo Warranto ❓.

🔁 Concurrent with High Courts (Art. 226 — which is wider: covers legal rights too).

⚠️ Suspendable only under Art. 359 during Emergency — but never for Arts. 20 & 21 (44th Amendment, 1978).

⚖️ 3. APPELLATE JURISDICTION

📘 Art. 132 — Constitutional appeals: needs an HC certificate that a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution is involved.

💼 Art. 133 — Civil appeals: substantial question of law of general importance needing SC decision (monetary limit of ₹20,000 deleted by the 30th Amendment, 1972).

🔒 Art. 134 — Criminal appeals as of right when: (a) HC reverses acquittal ➜ death sentence; (b) HC withdraws a case from a subordinate court, convicts & awards death; (c) HC certifies fitness. Expanded by the SC (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970 to cover life-imprisonment/10-year sentences in similar situations.

📝 Art. 134A — HC may grant the certificate suo motu or on oral application immediately after judgment (44th Amendment, 1978).

🕰️ Art. 135 — SC inherits the Federal Court's jurisdiction over pre-1950 matters.

🎯 4. SPECIAL LEAVE — THE RESIDUARY POWER

📌 Art. 136 — Discretionary leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order of any court or tribunal in India.

🚫 Sole exception: courts/tribunals under laws relating to the Armed Forces.

📊 SLPs dominate roughly 60–70% of the docket — the single biggest cause of pendency.

🧠 Self-restraint: to be used “sparingly, in exceptional circumstances” — Pritam Singh v. State (1950); N. Suriyakala v. Mohandoss (2007).

💡 Insight: Art. 136 is what turned a constitutional court into a general court of appeal — a design feature Nick Robinson calls the reason India's apex court is the world's most accessible.

🗣️ 5. ADVISORY JURISDICTION

📌 Art. 143(1) — President may refer a question of law or fact of public importance; the Court may answer (discretionary, and may decline: Ayodhya Reference, 1993).

📌 Art. 143(2) — Disputes on pre-Constitution treaties/sanads; here the Court “shall” report.

🧾 Nature: an opinion, not a binding judgment (no res judicata), but of the highest persuasive value; heard by ≥5 judges.

📚 Key references: Delhi Laws Act (1951) · Kerala Education Bill (1958) · Berubari Union (1960) · Keshav Singh (1965) · Presidential Poll (1974) · Special Courts Bill (1978) · Cauvery (1992) · 2G Spectrum (2012) · Special Reference No. 1 of 2025 (Governor's assent).

🔄 6. REVIEW & CURATIVE

📌 Art. 137 — Power to review its own judgments, subject to law and rules under Art. 145.

📋 Grounds (Order XLVII CPC): discovery of new & important evidence · error apparent on the face of the record · any other sufficient reason.

⏱️ Limitation: 30 days; normally decided by circulation in chambers by the same bench; open-court hearing only in death-sentence matters.

💊 Curative petition — a judge-made final remedy created in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002): requires certification by a senior advocate, circulated to the 3 senior-most judges + the original bench.

✨ 7. THE EXTRAORDINARY POWER — “COMPLETE JUSTICE” i

📌 Art. 142(1) — Any decree/order “necessary for doing complete justice” in any cause before it, enforceable throughout India.

📌 Art. 142(2) — Power to secure attendance of persons, discovery of documents, and to punish for its own contempt.

🌟 Uses: Bhopal Gas settlement (1989) · Vishaka Guidelines (1997) · dissolving marriages on irretrievable breakdown without the 6-month wait (Shilpa Sailesh, 2023) · Ayodhya land allotment (2019) · release of long-term undertrials.

🚧 Limits: cannot supplant substantive statutory law (Supreme Court Bar Assn. v. UoI, 1998) and cannot override express constitutional provisions. In the Presidential Reference (20 Nov 2025), a 5-judge Bench held that Art. 142 cannot be used to grant “deemed assent” to Bills in place of the Governor/President.

🏛️
Exclusive & Original

Only forum for genuine Union–State legal disputes.

📈
Widest Appellate

Art. 136 reaches every court and tribunal in the Republic.

🗣️
Unique Advisory

A consultative role most apex courts (e.g., the US SC) refuse to play.

✨
Complete Justice

Art. 142 — equity written into the Constitution itself.

🧩 Bench Strength & Case-Flow Mechanics

👥 How benches are composed

Division Bench (2 judges): the workhorse — most SLPs and appeals.
Three-judge bench: references, conflicts between two-judge benches.
Constitution Bench (≥5): mandatory under Art. 145(3) for a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, or for an Art. 143 reference.
7, 9, 11, 13 judges: to overrule earlier co-equal or larger benches. Largest ever = 13 in Kesavananda Bharati (1973).
Roster: The CJI is “Master of the Roster” — reaffirmed in Campaign for Judicial Accountability v. UoI (2017) and Asok Pande (2018).

⚖️ PART IV — DOCTRINES FORGED BY THE COURT

🏔️ The Crown Jewel: The Basic Structure Doctrine

📅 Origin: Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru v. State of Kerala, AIR 1973 SC 1461 — 13 judges, 68 days of hearing, 703 pages, decided 7:6 on 24 April 1973.

🧬 Intellectual parentage: German jurist Prof. Dietrich Conrad's 1965 lecture on “implied limitations”, carried into Indian argument by M.K. Nambyar and Nani Palkhivala.

⚡ Core holding: Parliament's amending power under Art. 368 is wide but not unlimited — it may amend any provision, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot damage or destroy the basic structure/framework of the Constitution.

🔗 Chain of cases: Shankari Prasad (1951) ➜ Sajjan Singh (1965) ➜ Golak Nath (1967, FRs unamendable) ➜ Kesavananda (1973) ➜ Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975, first application — Art. 329A struck down) ➜ Minerva Mills (1980, limited amending power + FR–DPSP balance) ➜ Waman Rao (1980, 24 April 1973 cut-off for Ninth Schedule) ➜ I.R. Coelho (2007, all post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws testable) ➜ NJAC (2015).

📖
Supremacy of the Constitution

Every organ derives power from — and is subject to — the document.

🗳️
Republican & Democratic Polity

Free and fair elections; sovereignty of the people.

🕌
Secularism

Equal respect, no State religion — S.R. Bommai (1994).

🗾
Federalism

Distribution of powers; States are not administrative agencies of the Centre.

🔱
Separation of Powers

Functional, not rigid — but no organ may usurp another's core function.

🔍
Judicial Review

Arts. 32, 226, 227 — the enforcement mechanism of every other feature.

🤝
Rule of Law & Equality

Art. 14 as a facet of the basic structure.

⚖️
FR–DPSP Harmony

Minerva Mills: “the Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV.”

🛡️
Judicial Independence

Held a basic feature in the NJAC case (2015).

🧰 The Doctrinal Toolkit — Interpretation & Federalism

✂️
Severability (Art. 13)

📚 Leading case: R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India (1957); applied earlier in A.K. Gopalan (1950) where s.14 of the Preventive Detention Act was severed.

💡 Essence: Only the offending part falls, if it can be separated without destroying the statute's purpose.

🌘
Eclipse

📚 Case: Bhikaji Narain Dhirajlal v. State of M.P. (1955).

💡 Essence: A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with FRs is not dead but dormant — it revives when the inconsistency is removed by amendment. Applies to citizens only; post-1950 laws are void ab initio (Deep Chand).

🧪
Pith & Substance

📚 Case: State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951); Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee (1947 PC).

💡 Essence: Look at the true nature and character of a law; incidental encroachment on another List is permissible.

🎭
Colourable Legislation

📚 Case: K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953).

💡 Essence: “What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly” — a legislature cannot disguise a lack of competence.

🎼
Harmonious Construction

📚 Case: Re Kerala Education Bill (1958); Qureshi v. State of Bihar.

💡 Essence: Read conflicting provisions (esp. Part III vs Part IV) so that each is given effect; avoid rendering any provision otiose.

⏭️
Prospective Overruling

📚 Case: Golak Nath (1967) — CJI Subba Rao imported it from American jurisprudence.

💡 Essence: A new ruling applies only to the future, protecting settled transactions.

🚫
No Waiver of Fundamental Rights

📚 Case: Basheshar Nath v. CIT (1959).

💡 Essence: FRs are matters of public policy; a citizen cannot contract out of them.

📡
Territorial Nexus & Repugnancy

📚 Cases: State of Bombay v. RMDC (1957); Art. 254 — M. Karunanidhi v. UoI (1979).

💡 Essence: Extra-territorial operation needs a real connection; central law prevails on Concurrent List repugnancy unless the State law has Presidential assent.

🌍 Rights, Environment & Administrative Doctrines

🏞️
Public Trust

M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1997) — air, water, forests and ecologically fragile lands are held by the State as trustee for the public; they cannot be converted to private ownership.

💥
Absolute Liability

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987, Oleum Gas Leak) — a hazardous enterprise is absolutely liable, with no exceptions, discarding Rylands v. Fletcher. Damages must correlate to the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise.

🌱
Sustainable Development, Precautionary Principle & Polluter Pays

Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. UoI (1996) — read into Art. 21 and declared part of customary international law and Indian environmental law.

📏
Proportionality & Manifest Arbitrariness

Modern Dental College (2016) & Puttaswamy (2017) laid the four-prong proportionality test; Shayara Bano (2017) revived manifest arbitrariness as a ground to strike down legislation under Art. 14.

🤲
Legitimate Expectation & Natural Justice

Maneka Gandhi (1978) & Union of India v. Hindustan Development Corp. (1993) — fairness in State action; audi alteram partem read into Art. 21.

🕉️
Essential Religious Practices (ERP)

Shirur Mutt (1954) onwards — Art. 25/26 protects only practices essential and integral to a religion. Deeply contested; its very validity is among the questions before the pending nine-judge Sabarimala Review bench.

🏳️‍🌈
Constitutional Morality & Transformative Constitutionalism

Navtej Singh Johar (2018), Joseph Shine (2018), NALSA (2014) — the Constitution as an instrument to transform an unequal society, not merely to preserve it.

📚
Stare Decisis, Per Incuriam & Merger

Art. 141 binds all courts; a larger bench overrules a smaller one; a decision rendered in ignorance of a binding statute/precedent is per incuriam and not binding. The SC is not bound by its own decisions (Bengal Immunity, 1955).

🛡️ PART V — INDEPENDENCE OF THE JUDICIARY

🎯 Independence is not a privilege of judges — it is a guarantee to litigants. It is protected structurally (how judges are chosen and removed), financially (how they are paid), and functionally (how they work). Since the NJAC judgment (2015) it is formally part of the basic structure.

🏰

Constitutional Safeguards

🔐 Security of tenure — removal only under Art. 124(4).

💰 Art. 125 — salary charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (non-votable); cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment (except during a Financial Emergency, Art. 360).

🗣️ Arts. 121 & 211 — no discussion in Parliament/State Legislature on a judge's conduct except on a removal motion.

⚖️ Art. 129 — Court of Record with power to punish for contempt.

🚫 Art. 124(7) — a retired SC judge cannot plead or act before any court or authority in India.

🎛️

Functional Autonomy

📜 Art. 145 — the Court frames its own rules (SC Rules, 2013) with Presidential approval.

🧑‍💼 Art. 146 — CJI appoints officers & staff; their expenses are charged on the CFI.

🗂️ Master of the Roster — the CJI alone allocates cases and constitutes benches.

🧭 Art. 50 (DPSP) — the State shall separate the judiciary from the executive.

🏛️ Art. 32 non-suspendable core — Arts. 20 & 21 survive even an Emergency (44th Amendment).

🚪

Post-Retirement Questions

🧾 No bar on accepting government-appointed offices (tribunals, commissions, Governorships, Rajya Sabha nominations) — a live integrity debate.

⏳ Reform proposals: a cooling-off period of 2 years; the 2nd ARC and Law Commission's 14th & 230th Reports.

💡 Counter-view: excluding experienced jurists from tribunals harms adjudication quality.

🧓 Age debate: retirement at 65 (SC) vs 62 (HC) — proposals to raise/unify remain pending.

🔓 Accountability & Transparency — The Other Half of Independence

🧭 Internal mechanisms

📜 Restatement of Values of Judicial Life (1997): a 16-point ethical code adopted by the Full Court.
🕵️ In-house Procedure (1999, revised 2014): the CJI may order a three-member judicial inquiry against a sitting judge — the mechanism used in the Justice Yashwant Varma matter (2025).
📂 RTI: CPIO, SC of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019) — the CJI's office is a public authority under the RTI Act, subject to privacy and independence balancing.
💎 Asset disclosure: Full Court resolution of 1 April 2025 — judges' asset declarations are now published on the Supreme Court website.
📺 Live streaming: Approved in Swapnil Tripathi v. SC of India (2018); Constitution Bench proceedings streamed since 27 Sept 2022, with a dedicated YouTube channel and the Court's own portal.

⚠️ Flashpoints in the Independence Debate

🗞️ 12 January 2018: Four senior-most judges (Chelameswar, Gogoi, Lokur, Joseph) held an unprecedented press conference on case allocation — “democracy is in danger.”
⛓️ ADM Jabalpur (1976): The Court's darkest hour — 4:1 holding that Art. 21 could be suspended during Emergency; H.R. Khanna's lone dissent cost him the CJI-ship. Expressly overruled in Puttaswamy (2017).
📦 “Sealed cover jurisprudence”: Criticised as antithetical to open justice; disapproved in Media One (2023).
🗄️ MoP deadlock: The Memorandum of Procedure post-NJAC has still not been finalised — appointments proceed in a rulebook vacuum.
⏱️ File-sitting: Government delay in clearing reiterated Collegium names remains the sharpest executive lever over the judiciary.

🌟 PART VI — LANDMARK JUDGMENTS THAT SHAPED THE REPUBLIC

🔄 Fate: Overruled by R.C. Cooper (1970) and Maneka Gandhi (1978).

📕 The narrow beginning
1973

🏔️ Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala i

⚡ Doctrine: Basic Structure — Parliament may amend, but not destroy.

🎯 Impact: Saved constitutional democracy during and after the Emergency; the intellectual anchor of every later constitutional challenge.

🧾 Irony: The petitioner-swamiji lost his own land case; India gained its most important doctrine.

🗳️ 7:6👨‍⚖️ 13 Judges📄 703 pages
1976

⛓️ ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla — “The Habeas Corpus Case”

💔 Held (4:1): During Emergency, no person could move any court for enforcement of Art. 21 — even against unlawful detention.

🕯️ The dissent: Justice H.R. Khanna — “detention without trial is an anathema to all those who love personal liberty.” He was superseded as CJI and resigned.

🔄 Fate: Expressly overruled in Puttaswamy (2017). The Court's own institutional memory of failure.

⚫ Darkest Hour
1978

🕊️ Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India

⚡ Doctrine: Procedure under Art. 21 must be “right, just and fair” — not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. Substantive due process enters by the back door.

🔺 Golden Triangle: Arts. 14 + 19 + 21 read together — the birth of modern rights jurisprudence.

🌊 Downstream: Right to livelihood (Olga Tellis), speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon), legal aid, clean environment, health, education (Unni Krishnan ➜ Art. 21A), privacy, dignity.

🔺 Golden Triangle🌱 Most generative
1980

🏭 Minerva Mills v. Union of India

⚖️ Held: Struck down s.4 & s.55 of the 42nd Amendment. Limited amending power is itself a basic feature; the balance between Parts III and IV is basic structure.

💬 Chandrachud CJ: “The Constitution is a precious heritage; therefore you cannot destroy its identity.”

1992
1994

🧮 Indra Sawhney (1992) & 🏛️ S.R. Bommai (1994)

🧮 Indra Sawhney: Upheld 27% OBC reservation; introduced the “creamy layer” exclusion and the 50% ceiling; no reservation in promotions (later modified by the 77th/81st/85th Amendments).

🏛️ S.R. Bommai: Judicial review of Art. 356 Presidential Proclamations; floor test in the Assembly is the only test of majority; secularism = basic structure; federalism strengthened dramatically.

🗳️ Floor Test🧮 50% Ceiling
1997

♀️ Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan

⚡ Doctrine: In a legislative vacuum, the Court framed binding guidelines against workplace sexual harassment, drawing on CEDAW (Arts. 51(c) & 253).

🎯 Impact: Governed India for 16 years until the POSH Act, 2013. The global textbook example of constructive judicial law-making.

🧠 Insight: Also the clearest illustration of Art. 142 + international law being fused into domestic rights.

🛡️ Preventive🌏 CEDAW
2014
2015

🏳️‍⚧️ NALSA (2014) & 💬 Shreya Singhal (2015)

🏳️‍⚧️ NALSA v. UoI: Recognised transgender persons as the “third gender”; self-identification of gender is part of Arts. 14, 15, 19 & 21.

💬 Shreya Singhal: Struck down s.66A of the IT Act as vague and overbroad; distinguished advocacy from incitement; the charter of online free speech in India.

2017

🔐 K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India — “The Privacy Judgment”

⚡ Held (9:0): The right to privacy is a Fundamental Right intrinsic to life, liberty and dignity under Art. 21 and Part III as a whole.

🧹 Cleared away: M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1962) to the contrary; ADM Jabalpur formally overruled.

🧪 Test: Legality + legitimate State aim + proportionality (+ procedural safeguards).

🌊 Legacy: The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023; Aadhaar (2018); decriminalisation of homosexuality (2018).

🗳️ 9:0💻 Digital Age Charter
2017
2018

💔 Shayara Bano · 🏳️‍🌈 Navtej Johar · ⚖️ Joseph Shine

💔 Shayara Bano (2017): Instant triple talaq struck down 3:2; revived manifest arbitrariness under Art. 14.

🏳️‍🌈 Navtej Singh Johar (2018): Read down s.377 IPC — consensual same-sex relations decriminalised; overruled Suresh Koushal. Constitutional morality > social morality.

⚖️ Joseph Shine (2018): Struck down s.497 IPC (adultery) — a woman is not the property of her husband.

2019
2023

🕌 Ayodhya (2019) · 🌐 Anuradha Bhasin (2020) · 🧾 EWS (2022) · 💍 Supriyo (2023)

🕌 M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das: Unanimous 5-judge verdict; title decreed to the deity; 5 acres to the Sunni Waqf Board using Art. 142.

🌐 Anuradha Bhasin: Internet access is protected under Art. 19(1)(a)/(g); indefinite suspension is impermissible; all orders must be published and reviewed.

🧾 Janhit Abhiyan (2022): Upheld the 103rd Amendment (10% EWS quota) 3:2; economic criteria alone held permissible; the 50% ceiling held not inviolable for this purpose.

💍 Supriyo v. UoI (2023): Declined to read marriage equality into the Special Marriage Act — held it to be for Parliament; unanimous on no fundamental right to marry.

2024

🗳️ Electoral Bonds · ⛏️ Mineral Rights · 🎓 AMU

🗳️ ADR v. UoI (15 Feb 2024): The Electoral Bond Scheme struck down unanimously (5:0) as violating the voter's right to information under Art. 19(1)(a); the proportionality test applied to anonymity of political funding.

⛏️ Mineral Area Development Authority v. SAIL (2024, 9 judges, 8:1): Royalty is not a tax; States retain legislative competence to tax mineral rights — a major federal-fiscal reset (applied prospectively from 1 April 2005).

🎓 AMU (2024, 4:3): Overruled Azeez Basha (1967); laid down the test for “minority educational institution” under Art. 30 — the factual determination remitted to a regular bench.

2025

🏛️ The Governor's Assent Saga — Federalism's Newest Frontier i

📅 8 April 2025 — State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu: A two-judge bench held the Governor's indefinite withholding of 10 Bills “illegal and erroneous”, ruled out a pocket veto and absolute veto, prescribed timelines, and used Art. 142 to declare the Bills “deemed assented”.

📨 22 July 2025: President Droupadi Murmu made a 14-question reference under Art. 143(1) — Special Reference No. 1 of 2025.

⚖️ 20 November 2025 — In re: Assent…, 2025 INSC 1333: A 5-judge Bench (CJI B.R. Gavai, Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, A.S. Chandurkar) opined, as an unauthored “Opinion of the Court” (111 pages), that:
• Courts cannot impose timelines on the Governor (Art. 200) or the President (Art. 201);
• “Deemed assent” is alien to the Constitution and cannot be conjured through Art. 142;
• Yet a Governor cannot sit indefinitely on Bills — inaction remains amenable to limited judicial review (a mandamus to decide, not how to decide);
• Questions 12 and 14 were returned unanswered.

🧠 Why it matters: It marks a conscious recalibration of Art. 142 and a reaffirmation of the separation of powers — the Court policing its own most powerful tool.

🤝 The PIL Revolution — India's Gift to Public Law

👨‍⚖️ Architects: Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer, late 1970s–80s.

🔑 Key move: Relaxation of locus standi — “any public-spirited person” may move the Court for those unable to do so (S.P. Gupta, 1981); even a postcard could be treated as a writ petition (“epistolary jurisdiction”).

🏆 Milestones: Hussainara Khatoon (1979 — undertrials, speedy trial) · Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984 — bonded labour) · Olga Tellis (1985 — right to livelihood) · M.C. Mehta series (environment) · Vishaka (1997) · PUCL (2001 — right to food) · Common Cause (2018 — passive euthanasia & living wills).

⚠️ The critique: “Publicity/Private Interest Litigation”, judicial overreach into policy, continuing mandamus as governance-by-court, and unpredictable outcomes. The Court itself now imposes costs for frivolous PILs (State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal, 2010, laid down guidelines).

💡 The Big Insight

India's Supreme Court is simultaneously a constitutional court (like Germany's), a court of final appeal (like the UK's), a federal umpire (like America's), and an advisory body and equity court — roles that no other apex court combines. That breadth is the source of both its extraordinary power and its crushing docket. ⚖️📈

🚧 PART VIII — CURRENT CHALLENGES & THE ROAD AHEAD

⏳

Eight Critical Challenges

1️⃣ Pendency & the docket explosion: ~94,300 cases in the SC (June 2026); ~5.6 crore across all Indian courts; filings have tripled since 2022.
2️⃣ SLP overload: Art. 136 makes the Court a general appellate forum — 60–70% of the docket, crowding out constitutional adjudication.
3️⃣ Constitution-Bench backlog: 22 five-judge, 5 seven-judge and 2 nine-judge matters pending (June 2026) — the law stays frozen while they wait.
4️⃣ Access & geography: One seat in Delhi for a subcontinent; cost, distance and elite advocacy fees create an economic filter on justice.
5️⃣ Diversity deficit: Only 2 sitting women judges out of 38 (Justices B.V. Nagarathna and V. Mohana, 2026); only 11 women in 76 years (first: Justice Fathima Beevi, 1989). SC/ST, OBC and minority representation remains thin.
6️⃣ Appointment opacity & MoP deadlock: No published criteria; no finalised Memorandum of Procedure since 2015.
7️⃣ Judicial overreach vs abdication: Criticised in one breath for governing by court, and in the next for delay in politically sensitive cases (electoral bonds timing, Art. 370, habeas corpus listings).
8️⃣ Capacity at the base: ~21 judges per million people, against the Law Commission's 1987 recommendation of 50; ~330+ High Court vacancies and ~5,000 in district courts.
🛠️

Reform Track — What's Actually Happening

👨‍⚖️ Strength expansion: 34 ➜ 38 (Ordinance, May 2026; Bill, July 2026) — explicitly to allow more Constitution Benches.
💻 eCourts Phase III (2023–27): outlay of ₹7,210 crore — digitisation of legacy records, cloud infrastructure, paperless and virtual courts, AI-assisted translation (SUVAS) and transcription.
📊 National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG): the SC was onboarded in 2023 — real-time, public case statistics; an open-data first for an apex court.
📺 Live streaming & e-SCR: Constitution Bench hearings streamed since Sept 2022; e-SCR offers free access to 30,000+ reported judgments; translations into 16+ Indian languages.
🤝 Mediation Act, 2023 & ADR push: CJI Surya Kant has made mediation and “predictability of outcomes” a stated priority; a call for a national judicial policy so that judgments “sound like notes in the same rhythm”.
🗂️ Listing reforms (Dec 2025 onwards): Oral mentioning restricted to personal-liberty matters; urgency to be circulated by letter; automated listing being expanded.
🔓 Transparency: Public asset declarations (1 April 2025); publication of Collegium resolutions.
🏛️ Structural proposals (pending): Regional/Cassation Benches (Law Commission's 95th, 125th & 229th Reports) — a Constitution Bench in Delhi + four regional appellate benches; the Court has consistently resisted, citing unity of the apex court.

🕊️ CONCLUSION — THE SENTINEL ON THE QUI VIVE

This Court is thus the sentinel on the qui vive. 🛡️ — Patanjali Sastri J., State of Madras v. V.G. Row (1952)

In seventy-six years the Supreme Court of India has travelled from a modest bench of eight, sitting in the Chamber of Princes, to an institution of thirty-eight judges whose reasoning shapes how a fifth of humanity lives, prays, speaks, works and dies. 🌏

It has been, by turns, timid (Gopalan), complicit (ADM Jabalpur), audacious (Kesavananda), compassionate (the PIL era), transformative (Puttaswamy, Navtej) and, most recently, self-limiting (the Presidential Reference of 2025). That oscillation is not weakness — it is the record of a court learning, in public, how much power a constitutional democracy can safely place in unelected hands. ⚖️

Its greatest strength — accessibility — is also its greatest affliction: a docket of ninety-four thousand cases is the price of a Constitution that invited every citizen to knock at the highest door. The task of the coming decade is not to become more powerful, but to become more focused: fewer appeals, faster constitutional answers, greater diversity on the bench, and a jurisprudence that speaks, as CJI Surya Kant put it, in the same rhythm rather than in discordant notes. 🎼
