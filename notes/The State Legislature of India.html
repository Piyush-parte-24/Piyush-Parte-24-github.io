🎬 Opening Scene · The Big Picture

🌳 Master Mind Map — Seven Systems, One Chapter

Chapter III of Part VI is the State-level mirror of the Union Parliament chapter (Articles 79–122) — but with deliberate asymmetries: the Council is optional and only delays, there is no joint sitting, and the Governor is part of the Legislature without being a member of any House. 🧩

🎬 Act I · Building the Chambers

🏛️ Composition — Articles 168–171

Who and what is the "State Legislature"? Articles 168–171 answer: the Governor plus one or two Houses; the Council's existence is optional; the Assembly is directly elected; the Council is indirectly elected and partly nominated.

168🏛️ Constitution of Legislatures in States🔴 Core · Governor + House(s)

168(1) Every State has a Legislature consisting of the Governor and — in the States named in the clause (AP, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, UP) — two Houses; in every other State, one House.

168(2) Where there are two Houses: Legislative Council and Legislative Assembly. Where there is one: the Legislative Assembly.

💡 Why the Governor counts: no Bill becomes law without the Governor's assent (or the President's under Art. 201), so the Governor is a constitutional part of the Legislature even though he neither sits nor votes in any House.
⚠️ Because the Constitution names the bicameral States in Art. 168(1), creating or abolishing a Council needs that list changed — which Art. 169(2) lets Parliament do by ordinary law.
↔ Art. 79 (Parliament)↔ Art. 169↔ Art. 153 (Governor)
169➕➖ Abolition or Creation of Legislative Councils🔴 Core · Assembly initiates · Parliament legislates

169(1) Parliament may by law abolish an existing Council or create one — but only if the State Assembly has first passed a resolution by both of these majorities:

  • 🅰️ a majority of the total membership of the Assembly; and
  • 🅱️ not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.

169(2) The law may contain the consequential amendments to the Constitution (e.g., Art. 168(1) list) and supplemental, incidental provisions.

169(3) Such a law is not an amendment under Art. 368 — ordinary Parliamentary majority, no State ratification.

🧮 Worked example (230-member Assembly, 180 present and voting): limb A needs 116 votes (majority of 230 = ⌊230/2⌋+1); limb B needs 120 votes (⅔ of 180). Both must hold, so the resolution needs at least 120 affirmative votes.
🚨 Three traps: (1) the Assembly's resolution does not itself abolish the Council; (2) Parliament is not compelled to act (e.g., pending proposals); (3) the Council's own consent is not required. History: Punjab (1970), West Bengal (1969), Tamil Nadu (1986) abolished; Andhra abolished (1985) and revived (2007).

170🗳️ Composition of Legislative Assemblies🔴 Core · Direct election · Constituencies · Delimitation

170(1) Not more than 500 and not fewer than 60 members, directly elected from territorial constituencies (subject to Art. 333 history and special provisions).

⭐ Exceptions by special constitutional provisions: Sikkim 32 (Art. 371F), Goa 40 (Art. 371-I), Mizoram 40 (Art. 371G). Delhi/Puducherry have Assemblies as Union Territories (Arts. 239A/239AA) — not under this Chapter.

170(2) As far as practicable, the ratio of population to seats is the same throughout the State.

170(3) After each census, seats and territorial divisions are readjusted by the authority and manner Parliament prescribes.

🧊 Freeze, stated precisely: Total seats stay tied to the 1971 census; constituency boundaries were readjusted on the 2001 census (84th/87th Amendments, Delimitation Act 2002). The freeze lifts on publication of the figures of the first census taken after 2026 — not merely when the calendar reaches 2026.

Reservation links: Art. 332 — SC/ST seats (extended to 2030 by the 104th Amendment; Anglo-Indian nomination under Art. 333 ended). Art. 332A (106th Amendment, 2023) — one-third women's reservation, operational only after the next delimitation (Art. 334A).

🎬 Act II · Time, Eligibility & Ceremony

⏳ Duration, Qualification & the Governor — Articles 172–177

A Legislature must be properly constituted, regularly summoned and answerable to the electorate. These six articles set its lifespan, its entry ticket, its meeting rhythm and the Governor's links with the Houses.

172⌛ Duration of State Legislatures🔴 Core · Assembly temporary · Council continuing

172(1) Unless sooner dissolved, the Assembly continues for 5 years from the date appointed for its first meeting — expiry operates as dissolution. (Not from the date of results.)

During a Proclamation of National Emergency, Parliament may extend the term by law for one year at a time; the extension cannot go beyond six months after the Emergency ceases.

172(2) The Council is not subject to dissolution; as nearly as possible one-third retire every second year.

📌 Constitution vs statute: the Constitution fixes biennial retirement; the normal 6-year MLC term comes from the Representation of the People Act, 1951. A casual-vacancy member serves the unexpired term.
⚠️ President's Rule (Art. 356) does not extend the Assembly's term under Art. 172 — only a National Emergency does.
🧠 5 · 6 · 1 · 6Assembly 5 years · max session gap 6 months (Art. 174) · Emergency extension 1 year at a time · outer limit 6 months after Emergency ends. Council: ⅓ retire every 2 years.
173🪪 Qualification for Membership🔴 Core · Citizen · Oath · Age 25/30 · Parliament's law
  • 173(a) Citizen of India, who makes and subscribes — before a person authorised by the Election Commission — the Third Schedule oath or affirmation.
  • 173(b) At least 25 years (Assembly) / 30 years (Council).
  • 173(c) Any other qualification prescribed by or under a law of Parliament (RPA 1951 — e.g., being an elector).
⚠️ Two different oaths: Art. 173 = candidate's qualifying oath before an ECI-authorised person; Art. 188 = member's seat-taking oath before the Governor or his appointee.
↔ Art. 84↔ Art. 188Third Schedule
174🔔 Sessions, Prorogation & Dissolution🔴 Core · Six-month rule

174(1) The Governor summons the House(s) at such time and place as he thinks fit; six months must not intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next → at least two sessions a year.

174(2) The Governor may prorogue either House and dissolve the Legislative Assembly.

⚖️ On whose advice? Ordinarily the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Art. 163; Shamsher Singh, 1974); his discretion is judicially limited (Nabam Rebia, 2016 — advancing a session without ministerial advice was held invalid).
🎯 Text vs convention: Budget / Monsoon / Winter sessions are convention. The Constitution says only "six months" — not three sessions or a fixed number of sitting days.
175🎙️ Governor's Right to Address & Send Messages🟠 Supporting · General power

175(1) The Governor may address the Assembly, or either House or both Houses assembled together, and may require members' attendance for that purpose.

175(2) He may send messages on a pending Bill or any other matter; the House must consider the matter with all convenient despatch.

🔎 175 vs 176: Art. 175 is an optional general power; Art. 176 is a mandatory special address on fixed occasions.
176📣 Special Address by the Governor🔴 Core · First session after election + first session of each year

176(1) The Governor addresses the Assembly (or both Houses together) at the commencement of (i) the first session after each general election to the Assembly and (ii) the first session of each year, informing the Legislature of the causes of its summons.

176(2) Rules must allot time for discussing the matters in the address — the Motion of Thanks. (Defeat of the Motion is conventionally treated as loss of confidence.)

⚠️ Not every session — and if both triggers coincide, one address suffices. The address is drafted by the Council of Ministers; it is the government's policy statement.
↔ Art. 87 (President's address)
177🗣️ Rights of Ministers & the Advocate-General🟠 Supporting · Speak, don't automatically vote

Every Minister and the Advocate-General may speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of, either House, a joint sitting, and any committee of which he is named a member — but no right to vote by virtue of this Article.

A Minister who is a member votes only in the House of which he is a member. The Advocate-General has no vote anywhere.

🔗 Responsible government: Art. 164(2) — Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Assembly (never the Council). Art. 164(4) — a Minister who is not a legislator for six consecutive months ceases to be Minister.
🎬 Act III · Guardians of the Chamber

🪑 Presiding Officers & Secretariat — Articles 178–187

The Assembly and the Council have mirror-image presiding structures — learn one set, and the other follows. A key difference from Parliament: the Council elects its own Chairman; the Governor is not ex officio Chairman (compare the Vice-President in the Rajya Sabha, Art. 89).

178🪑 Speaker & Deputy Speaker of the Assembly🔴 Core · Elected from Assembly members

The Assembly shall, as soon as may be, choose two of its members as Speaker and Deputy Speaker, and fill either office when vacant. ("As soon as may be" is not a fixed number of days.)

🎯 Why the Speaker matters: maintains order, interprets rules, certifies Money Bills (Art. 199(3)), decides Tenth Schedule disqualifications (as a tribunal — Kihoto Hollohan), and has the casting vote.
179🚪 Vacation, Resignation & Removal — Assembly🔴 Core · Effective majority · 14 days' notice
  • (a) Vacates office on ceasing to be an Assembly member.
  • (b) Speaker resigns to the Deputy Speaker; Deputy Speaker resigns to the Speaker — in writing.
  • (c) Removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Assembly; at least 14 days' notice of the intention to move it.

Continuity proviso: on dissolution the Speaker does not vacate office until immediately before the first meeting of the new Assembly (the proviso protects the Speaker, not the Deputy Speaker).

⚠️ "All the then members" = effective majority (vacant seats excluded) — not "present and voting", and not two-thirds.
180🔄 Performing the Speaker's Duties🟠 Supporting · Vacancy vs absence

180(1) If the Speaker's office is vacant, the Deputy Speaker performs the duties; if both are vacant, the Governor appoints an Assembly member to perform them (the basis of the pro-tem Speaker arrangement).

180(2) During the Speaker's absence from a sitting, the Deputy Speaker presides; if both are absent, a person fixed by the rules — failing which, by the Assembly — acts.

181⛔ No Presiding During One's Own Removal🟠 Supporting · Special voting rule

181(1) The Speaker or Deputy Speaker shall not preside while a resolution for his own removal is under consideration, even if present.

181(2) The Speaker may speak and take part, and may vote in the first instance (as a member) — but not in the case of an equality of votes (no casting vote).

182🏛️ Chairman & Deputy Chairman of the Council🔴 Core · Elected by the Council

The Council chooses two of its members as Chairman and Deputy Chairman as soon as may be, and fills vacancies.

⚠️ Rajya Sabha contrast: the Vice-President is ex officio RS Chairman. A State's Governor is not ex officio Chairman of the Council.
183🚪 Vacation, Resignation & Removal — Council🟠 Supporting · Parallel to Art. 179
  • (a) Vacate on ceasing to be a Council member.
  • (b) Chairman resigns to the Deputy Chairman and vice versa.
  • (c) Removal by a majority of all the then members of the Council, with 14 days' notice.

There is no "dissolution continuity" proviso — the Council never dissolves.

184🔄 Performing the Chairman's Duties🟠 Supporting · Council stand-ins

184(1) Chairman's office vacant → Deputy Chairman acts; both vacant → the Governor appoints a Council member.

184(2) Absence from a sitting → Deputy Chairman presides; both absent → person fixed by rules, failing which by the Council.

185⛔ Council Officers & Their Own Removal🟠 Supporting · Parallel to Art. 181

185(1) Neither officer may preside while a resolution for his own removal is considered.

185(2) The Chairman may speak and vote in the first instance but has no casting vote.

186💼 Salaries & Allowances of Presiding Officers🟢 Factual · State law + Second Schedule

Fixed by State legislative law; until then, per the Second Schedule. Under Art. 202(3)(b) they are charged on the State Consolidated Fund — discussed but not voted.

187🏢 Secretariat of the State Legislature🟢 Factual · Administrative autonomy

187(1) Each House has a separate secretarial staff (posts common to both Houses are allowed).

187(2) The State Legislature may regulate recruitment and service conditions by law.

187(3) Until such law, the Governor may make rules after consulting the Speaker / Chairman.

💡 A separate secretariat keeps the Legislature administratively independent of executive departments.

188🤚 Oath or Affirmation by Members🔴 Core · Before taking the seat

Every Assembly/Council member must make and subscribe the Third Schedule oath or affirmation before taking his seat, before the Governor or a person appointed by him. The oath includes allegiance to the Constitution and upholding India's sovereignty and integrity.

🧠 Pair: 173 = candidate's qualifying oath (ECI-authorised person) · 188 = member's seat-taking oath (Governor or appointee) · 193 = penalty for sitting/voting without it.
189🗳️ Voting, Vacancies & Quorum🔴 Core · Majority present and voting · Casting vote · Quorum

189(1) Questions are decided by a majority of members present and voting, excluding the presiding officer, who votes only on a tie (casting vote).

189(2) A House may act despite vacancies; proceedings stay valid even if it is later discovered that a non-entitled person sat or voted.

189(3) Quorum: ten members or one-tenth of the total membership, whichever is greater (unless the Legislature provides otherwise by law).

189(4) If quorum is lacking, the presiding officer must adjourn or suspend the meeting until it is met.

🧮 Quorum maths: default quorum = max(10, N/10). 230-member Assembly → max(10, 23) = 23. 60-member Assembly → max(10, 6) = 10. 40-member Council → 10.
🚨 Clause trap: quorum is 189(3) — clause (2) is about vacancies and validity of proceedings.
190🚪 Vacation of Seats🔴 Core · Dual membership · Resignation · 60 days' absence
  • 190(1) No person may be a member of both Houses of the same State Legislature; State law decides which seat is vacated.
  • 190(2) No person may be a member of the Legislatures of two or more States; unless all but one seat are resigned within the period set by rules made by the President, all become vacant.
  • 190(3) Seat falls vacant on disqualification under Art. 191(1)/(2), or on resignation in writing to the Speaker/Chairman that is accepted — which they must refuse if satisfied it is not voluntary or genuine.
  • 190(4) Absent from all meetings for 60 days without permission → the House may declare the seat vacant (days of prorogation or adjournment over four consecutive days are excluded).
⚠️ Not automatic: sixty days' absence empowers the House to declare vacancy; it does not vacate the seat by itself.
191🚫 Disqualifications for Membership🔴 Core · Five general grounds + Tenth Schedule

191(1) A person is disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member if he:

  1. holds an office of profit under the Union or any State Government (other than an office the State Legislature has by law exempted);
  2. is of unsound mind, declared so by a competent court;
  3. is an undischarged insolvent;
  4. is not a citizen, has voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship, or owes allegiance to a foreign State;
  5. is disqualified by or under a law made by Parliament.

Explanation: a Union or State Minister does not hold an office of profit by reason of being a Minister.

191(2) Also disqualified if disqualified under the Tenth Schedule (anti-defection).

⚖️ Statutory route

RPA 1951 — e.g., conviction with imprisonment of two years or more disqualifies (immediately after Lily Thomas, 2013), corrupt practices, certain government contracts.

🔀 Anti-defection route

Voluntarily giving up party membership; defying a party direction on a vote without permission/condonation; special rules for independents and nominated members.

🧠 O-U-I-C-L"Our Unique Insolvent Citizens Lose" → Office of profit · Unsound mind · Insolvent · Citizenship · Law of Parliament.
🔍 Office-of-profit insight: the label doesn't decide — appointment, removal, remuneration, control and functions do. The question is whether the office can yield profit, not whether it actually did.
192⚖️ Decision on Questions of Disqualification🔴 Core · Governor acts on ECI opinion

192(1) A question whether a member has become subject to a disqualification under Art. 191(1) goes to the Governor, whose decision is "final".

192(2) Before deciding, he must obtain the opinion of the Election Commission and act according to that opinion.

193💸 Penalty for Sitting or Voting When Not Entitled🔴 Core · ₹500 per day · Debt due to the State

A person who sits or votes as a member before complying with Article 188, or when he knows that he is not qualified, is disqualified, or is prohibited from doing so by a law made by Parliament or the State Legislature, is liable to a penalty of ₹500 for each day on which he so sits or votes.

The amount is recoverable as a debt due to the State.

⚠️ Personal liability vs valid proceedings: Art. 189(2) keeps the House's proceedings valid even if a non-entitled person sat or voted, but the member remains personally liable under Art. 193.
🧠 The Seat-Taking Chain173 qualifying oath → 188 seat-taking oath → 189 vote & quorum → 193 penalty for wrongful participation.

194🛡️ Powers, Privileges & Immunities🔴 Core · Speech protected · Not a blanket immunity

194(1) Subject to the Constitution and the rules and standing orders regulating procedure, there is freedom of speech in the Legislature of every State.

194(2) No member is liable to any court proceedings for anything said or any vote given in the Legislature or any committee. No person is liable for the publication, by or under the authority of a House, of any report, paper, votes or proceedings.

194(3) In other respects, the powers, privileges and immunities are those defined by the State Legislature by law. Until so defined, they are those of that House and its members and committees immediately before section 26 of the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 came into force (which removed the older reference to the House of Commons).

194(4) These provisions also apply to persons entitled to speak and take part in proceedings under the Constitution, such as Ministers and the Advocate-General (Art. 177), even if they are not members.

⚠️ Privilege is not a licence. In Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), a seven-judge Bench unanimously held that bribery connected with a legislative speech or vote is not protected by Art. 105(2) or 194(2), overruling P. V. Narasimha Rao (1998). In Raja Ram Pal (2007), the Court held that privilege claims are open to judicial review for constitutional violations.
📰 Reporting link: Art. 361A separately protects substantially true reports of legislative proceedings, subject to its conditions. It does not cover a secret sitting.
↔ Art. 105↔ Art. 19(1)(a)↔ Art. 212↔ Art. 361AKeshav Singh (Spl. Ref. 1/1964)
195💵 Salaries & Allowances of Members🟢 Factual · Determined by State law

Members of the Assembly and the Council receive such salaries and allowances as the State Legislature determines by law. Until it does so, the rates and conditions that applied immediately before the commencement of the Constitution to members of the corresponding Province's Assembly continue.

⚠️ Keep the articles apart: Art. 186 covers the presiding officers, which are charged expenditure under Art. 202(3). Art. 195 covers ordinary members, whose pay is not charged by Art. 202(3).
🎬 Act V · From Proposal to Law

📜 Bills, Money Bills & Assent — Articles 196–201

The Assembly is the decisive elected chamber. The Council can revise and delay ordinary legislation, but it cannot permanently block an Assembly Bill. Money Bills follow an even shorter route. At State level there is no joint sitting to resolve a deadlock.

📜 Ordinary Bill

May originate in either House. An Assembly-origin Bill can ultimately pass over Council opposition through Art. 197's repeat-passage mechanism.

💰 Money Bill

May originate only in the Assembly. The Council has 14 days to recommend changes, and the Assembly may accept or reject any of them.

🧠 Two ClocksOrdinary Assembly Bill: 3 months + 1 month (Art. 197).
Money Bill: 14 days (Art. 198).
These are Council-stage limits. They are not deadlines for the Governor's assent.
196📜 Introduction, Passage & Lapse of Bills🔴 Core · Origin · Prorogation · Dissolution

196(1) Subject to Arts. 198 and 207, a Bill may originate in either House of a bicameral Legislature.

196(2) Subject to Arts. 197 and 198, a Bill is deemed passed only if both Houses agree to it, either without amendment or with amendments agreed by both.

196(3) A pending Bill does not lapse on prorogation.

196(4) A Bill pending in the Council that has not been passed by the Assembly does not lapse when the Assembly is dissolved.

196(5) A Bill pending in the Assembly, or passed by it and pending in the Council, lapses on dissolution.

⚠️ Prorogation is not dissolution. The first ends a session, while the second ends the Assembly's life. Their effects on Bills differ.
197⏳ Council's Limited Power over Ordinary Bills🔴 Core · 3 months, then 1 month · No joint sitting

197(1) A Bill passed by the Assembly goes to the Council. The Assembly may pass it again, in the same or a later session, with or without amendments, if the Council rejects it, lets more than three months pass without passing it, or passes it with amendments the Assembly does not accept.

197(2) If, after the second transmission, the Council rejects it, lets more than one month pass, or proposes unacceptable amendments, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Assembly the second time, with any Council amendments the Assembly has accepted.

197(3) This Article does not apply to Money Bills.

🚨 Precision matters: "four months" is shorthand for the two Council delay periods (3 + 1). It is not a guaranteed enactment time. Rejection can trigger the next stage earlier, and the Assembly's own scheduling adds time. A Council-origin Bill rejected by the Assembly has no override route at all.
↔ Art. 108 (contrast: joint sitting)↔ Art. 198
198💵 Special Procedure for Money Bills🔴 Core · Assembly only · Recommendations · 14 days

198(1) A Money Bill cannot be introduced in the Council.

198(2) After the Assembly passes it, it goes to the Council for recommendations, which must be returned within 14 days of receipt.

198(3)–(4) The Assembly may accept or reject any or all recommendations. If it accepts, the Bill is deemed passed with those amendments. If it rejects, the Bill is deemed passed without them.

198(5) If the Council does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is deemed passed by both Houses in the form the Assembly passed it.

💡 The Council recommends. It cannot amend with binding effect, and it cannot reject. Its agreement is not a condition for passage.
↔ Art. 109↔ Art. 199↔ Art. 207(1)
199🏷️ Definition of a Money Bill🔴 Core · "Only" listed matters · Speaker's certificate

199(1) A Bill is a Money Bill if it contains only provisions dealing with all or any of these matters:

  1. Imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax;
  2. Regulation of borrowing or guarantees by the State, or amendment of law on its financial obligations;
  3. Custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund, and payments into or withdrawals from them;
  4. Appropriation of money out of the Consolidated Fund;
  5. Declaring expenditure to be charged on the Consolidated Fund, or increasing it;
  6. Receipt, custody or issue of money of the Consolidated Fund or Public Account, or audit of State accounts;
  7. Any matter incidental to (a)–(f).

199(2) A Bill is not a Money Bill merely because it provides for fines or other pecuniary penalties, licence fees or fees for services, or taxes imposed by a local authority for local purposes.

199(3) If a question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill, the decision of the Assembly Speaker is final.

199(4) The Speaker's certificate must be endorsed on the Bill when it is sent to the Council and when it is presented to the Governor.

⚠️ "Final" in the text vs judicial review: The certification of Money Bills has been litigated (the Aadhaar judgment of 2018 and the Roger Mathew reference of 2019 are the usual pointers). For exams, state the express wording first, then note that review is contested.
🧠 Recognition TestOnly listed financial matters + incidental provisions = Money Bill.
Listed matters mixed with other substantive provisions give a Financial Bill under Art. 207 instead.
200👑 Governor's Assent to Bills🔴 Core · Assent · Withhold · Reserve · Return (non-Money)

When a Bill passed by the House or Houses is presented to him, the Governor declares that he assents, withholds assent, or reserves the Bill for the President.

First proviso: as soon as possible after presentation, the Governor may return a Bill other than a Money Bill with a message asking the House(s) to reconsider it. If the Bill is passed again, with or without amendment, and presented again, the Governor shall not withhold assent.

Second proviso: the Governor must reserve (and not assent to) a Bill that, in his opinion, would so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position it is designed to fill.

Governor's courseKey point
AssentThe Bill becomes law (subject to its commencement clause)
Withhold assentThe Bill fails unless dealt with otherwise. The scope of this course is a live judicial question.
Return for reconsiderationOnly for a non-Money Bill. After repassage, assent cannot be withheld.
Reserve for the PresidentArt. 201 then governs. Reservation is compulsory under the High Court proviso.
🚨 Three different clocks: Council delay (Art. 197), Council recommendations (Art. 198) and assent (Art. 200) are separate stages. Art. 200 itself fixes no number of days, only "as soon as possible" in the first proviso.
⚖️ Current-law caution: The Governor's discretion, the effect of withholding assent, and any judicially prescribed timelines (see State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor, 2023, and later proceedings) have been actively litigated. This note summarises the constitutional text. Check the latest Supreme Court position before relying on a categorical statement.
↔ Art. 111↔ Art. 163↔ Art. 201↔ Art. 254(2)
201🇮🇳 Bills Reserved for the President🔴 Core · Presidential choice · Six-month reconsideration

The President declares either assent or withholding of assent to a reserved Bill.

For a non-Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it with a message. The House(s) must reconsider it within six months from receiving the message. If it is passed again, it is presented again to the President.

🚨 Whose six months? This is the Legislature's reconsideration period. It is not a deadline for the President to decide. Unlike Art. 200's first proviso, Art. 201 does not expressly oblige assent after repassage.
🔗 Federal link: Under Art. 254(2), a State law on a Concurrent List matter that is repugnant to an earlier Union law can prevail in that State if reserved and assented to by the President. Parliament may still override it later.
🎬 Act VI · Follow the Public Money

💰 Budget & Financial Procedure — Articles 202–207

The executive proposes financial policy, the Assembly authorises grants, and the Legislature supplies the legal authority to withdraw money. Discussion, voting and appropriation are separate constitutional steps.

➡️

💡 Two approvals, two jobs: Voting a demand approves a grant. An Appropriation Act authorises the withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund. A voted demand alone is not a withdrawal warrant.
202📊 Annual Financial Statement🔴 Core · Estimated receipts & expenditure · Charged vs voted

202(1) The Governor causes a statement of the State's estimated receipts and expenditure for each financial year to be laid before the House or Houses. This is the Annual Financial Statement, commonly called the State Budget.

202(2) The estimates must distinguish expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund from other expenditure, and must show expenditure on revenue account separately from other expenditure.

202(3) Expenditure charged on the State Consolidated Fund includes:

  • (a) the emoluments and allowances of the Governor and other expenditure relating to his office;
  • (b) the salaries and allowances of the Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman and Deputy Chairman;
  • (c) debt charges for which the State is liable (interest, sinking-fund and redemption charges);
  • (d) expenditure on the salaries and allowances of High Court Judges;
  • (e) sums required to satisfy any judgment, decree or award of a court or arbitral tribunal;
  • (f) any other expenditure declared charged by the Constitution or by State law.
🚨 High Court pensions: Clause (d) covers salaries and allowances only. Pensions of High Court Judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (Art. 112(3)(d)(iii)).
🗣️ Charged does not mean hidden: Charged expenditure can be discussed but is not submitted to the Assembly's vote. It is still included in the Appropriation Bill.
↔ Art. 112 (Union Budget)↔ Art. 203↔ Art. 266
203🗳️ Procedure for Estimates & Demands for Grants🔴 Core · Assembly alone votes · Governor's recommendation

203(1) Charged expenditure is not submitted to vote, though it may be discussed.

203(2) Other expenditure is submitted as demands for grants to the Assembly, which may assent, refuse assent, or assent subject to a reduction.

203(3) No demand for a grant may be made except on the Governor's recommendation.

✂️ Policy Cut

By practice, a motion to reduce the demand to ₹1 expresses disapproval of the underlying policy.

💸 Economy Cut

A motion to reduce the demand by a specified amount points to a suggested saving.

🪙 Token Cut

A motion to reduce the demand by ₹100 ventilates a specific grievance.

⚠️ Text vs practice: Art. 203 creates the power to assent, refuse or reduce. The three cut-motion categories come from legislative rules and practice, not from the Article's wording.
Assembly's monopoly: the Council has no power to vote on demands for grants, even in a bicameral State.
204📜 Appropriation Bills🔴 Core · No withdrawal without appropriation by law

204(1) As soon as grants have been made, an Appropriation Bill provides for withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund to meet the voted grants and the charged expenditure.

204(2) No amendment may be proposed in either House that would vary the amount or alter the destination of a grant, or vary charged expenditure. The presiding person's decision that an amendment is inadmissible is final.

204(3) Subject to Arts. 205 and 206, no money may be withdrawn from the Fund except under an appropriation made by law.

🧠 Grant ≠ Withdrawal203 approves the demand. 204 authorises the draw.
↔ Art. 114↔ Art. 199(1)(d)↔ Art. 266(3)
205🔄 Supplementary, Additional & Excess Grants🟠 Supporting · Shortfall · New service · Overspending

205(1)(a) If the amount authorised proves insufficient, or a need arises for additional expenditure on a new service not contemplated in the Budget, the Governor causes a further statement to be laid before the Legislature.

205(1)(b) If money has been spent on a service in a year in excess of the amount granted, a demand for the excess is presented to the Assembly.

205(2) The provisions of Arts. 202, 203 and 204 apply to these further statements and demands.

🧠 S-A-ESupplementary = existing provision falls short.
Additional = a new service needs provision.
Excess = spending has already exceeded the grant.
206🌉 Vote on Account, Vote of Credit & Exceptional Grant🔴 Core · Temporary bridge · Uncertain demand · Special grant
  • 206(1)(a) Vote on account: an advance grant for part of a financial year, pending completion of the voting and appropriation procedure.
  • 206(1)(b) Vote of credit: a grant for an unexpected demand whose magnitude or indefinite character prevents ordinary detailed estimates.
  • 206(1)(c) Exceptional grant: a grant for a purpose that forms no part of the current service of any financial year.

206(2) Arts. 203 and 204 apply to these grants and the related appropriation.

⚠️ No constitutional "two-month rule": Two months is a common vote-on-account practice, not a period fixed by Art. 206. An interim budget is not the same legal concept as a vote on account.
🧠 A-C-EAccount = Advance · Credit = Cannot estimate normally · Exceptional = Extra to current service.
207🧾 Special Provisions for Financial Bills🔴 Core · Governor's recommendation · Origin

207(1) A Bill or amendment making provision for any matter in Art. 199(1)(a)–(f) may be introduced or moved only on the Governor's recommendation, and such a Bill cannot be introduced in the Council. Proviso: no recommendation is needed for an amendment that reduces or abolishes a tax.

207(2) A Bill does not fall under clause (1) merely because it deals with fines or penalties, licence or service fees, or local-authority taxes for local purposes.

207(3) A Bill that would involve expenditure from the State Consolidated Fund cannot be passed by either House unless the Governor has recommended its consideration to that House.

🎬 Act VII · Autonomy within the Constitution

⚖️ Rules, Language & Constitutional Limits — Articles 208–212

A House governs its own proceedings, but its rules remain subject to the Constitution. Procedural autonomy protects deliberation. It does not place a Legislature beyond constitutional scrutiny.

209 💰 Financial procedure lawTimely completion

208📘 Rules of Procedure🟠 Supporting · House-made rules · Subject to the Constitution

208(1) Each House may make rules to regulate its procedure and conduct of business, subject to the Constitution.

208(2) Until it does so, the rules and standing orders in force before the Constitution continue, with adaptations by the Speaker or Chairman.

208(3) In a bicameral State, the Governor, after consulting the Speaker and the Chairman, may make rules on communication between the Houses.

⚠️ No joint-sitting loophole: Rules on inter-House communication cannot create an Art. 108-style deadlock mechanism at State level.
↔ Art. 118
209⏱️ Regulation of Financial Procedure by Law🟢 Factual · Timely completion · Law prevails over rules

The State Legislature may, to ensure timely completion of financial business, regulate by law the procedure and conduct of business in the House(s) for any financial matter or Appropriation Bill.

If such a law conflicts with a rule made or continued under Art. 208, the law prevails to the extent of the inconsistency.

🧠 208 vs 209208 = House rules.
209 = financial-procedure law that overrides inconsistent House rules.
↔ Art. 119
210🗣️ Language Used in the Legislature🟠 Supporting · State language · Hindi · English · Mother tongue

210(1) Subject to Art. 348, business is transacted in the State's official language(s), in Hindi, or in English. The Speaker or Chairman may allow a member who cannot adequately express himself in these languages to speak in his mother tongue.

210(2) Unless the State Legislature provides otherwise by law, the words "or in English" cease to have effect after 15 years from the commencement of the Constitution. The period is 25 years for Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya and Tripura, and 40 years for Arunachal Pradesh, Goa and Mizoram.

📜 Speech vs legal text: Art. 210 governs legislative business. Art. 348 separately governs the authoritative texts of Bills, Acts and other instruments.
↔ Art. 120↔ Arts. 343–348
211⚖️ Restriction on Discussion of Judges' Conduct🔴 Core · Supreme Court & High Court judges

No discussion may take place in a State Legislature concerning the conduct of any Supreme Court or High Court Judge in the discharge of his duties.

🚨 Union–State difference: Art. 121 allows Parliament to discuss judicial conduct on a motion for presenting an address for a Judge's removal. Art. 211 has no such exception, because State Legislatures have no role in removing Judges.
💡 The bar protects judicial independence. It is not a ban on debating legislation or public policy merely because courts have considered related questions.
↔ Art. 121↔ Art. 124(4)↔ Art. 217
212🏛️ Courts Not to Inquire into Legislative Proceedings🔴 Core · Procedural irregularity ≠ substantive unconstitutionality

212(1) The validity of any proceedings in a State Legislature cannot be called in question on the ground of an alleged irregularity of procedure.

212(2) No officer or member in whom powers are vested for regulating procedure, conducting business or maintaining order is subject to the jurisdiction of any court for exercising those powers.

⚖️ Not an absolute shield: Courts distinguish protected procedural irregularity from substantive illegality or unconstitutionality (see Raja Ram Pal, 2007). Internal procedure does not remove judicial review of constitutional limits.
🧠 The BoundaryProcedural irregularity: protected.
Substantive constitutional violation: review may remain available.

🔗 Beyond the Chapter

🧩 Constitutional Connections

Articles 168–212 work together with provisions on the executive, elections, public funds and legislative competence. The strongest answers explain these links instead of reciting isolated article numbers.

👑 Governor & Responsible Government

Arts. 153–163 establish the Governor and the aid-and-advice principle. Art. 164(2) makes the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Assembly, so the Council cannot topple the government.

🗳️ Elections & Representation

Art. 324 vests superintendence of elections in the Election Commission. Arts. 325–329 cover rolls, adult suffrage, election law and election disputes. Arts. 330–334A contain reservation provisions.

💰 The Public Funds

Art. 266 distinguishes the Consolidated Fund from the Public Account. Art. 267 allows a Contingency Fund. An advance from it bridges an urgent need but does not remove the need for later legislative authorisation.

📚 Competence & Repugnancy

Arts. 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule allocate legislative fields. Art. 254 addresses inconsistency in Concurrent List matters. Assent does not cure lack of competence or a breach of fundamental rights.

🔀 Anti-Defection

Art. 191(2) links membership to the Tenth Schedule. The Speaker or Chairman decides as a tribunal, subject to judicial review. This is separate from the Governor–ECI route under Art. 192.

🧾 Audit & Accountability

Under Art. 151(2), the CAG's State reports go to the Governor, who lays them before the Legislature. Committees such as the Public Accounts Committee examine spending after the money has been spent.

🗂️ The Judicial Lens

⚖️ Landmark Cases — Principles, Not Just Names

Use a case for the specific proposition it establishes. Some questions have developed through later judgments and references, so an older holding should not automatically be treated as the last word.

1965 · Special Reference No. 1 of 1964 (Keshav Singh)

Reconciled legislative privilege with fundamental rights and judicial review. It is the foundational reference on privilege and the courts.

1974 · Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab

The Governor ordinarily acts on ministerial aid and advice. Read Arts. 174 and 200 within that parliamentary framework.

1992 · Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu

Upheld the Tenth Schedule's adjudication by the Speaker or Chairman, who acts as a tribunal. The decision is subject to judicial review.

2006 · Jaya Bachchan v. Union of India

On office of profit, the question is whether the office is capable of yielding a profit, not whether profit was actually received.

2007 · Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha

Privilege does not place every legislative act beyond review. It separates procedural irregularity from reviewable constitutional infirmity.

2013 · Lily Thomas v. Union of India

Struck down the provision that deferred conviction-based disqualification for sitting legislators.

2016 · Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker

Held that the Governor could not advance the Assembly session contrary to ministerial advice. A related issue, on the Speaker's power while facing a removal notice, was later referred to a larger Bench, so verify subsequent developments.

2020 · Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker

Anti-defection petitions must be decided within a reasonable period, ordinarily three months. This period is not written into the Tenth Schedule.

2022 · Ashish Shelar v. Maharashtra Legislative Assembly

Set aside a one-year legislative suspension, showing that "internal legislative matter" does not by itself bar scrutiny.

2023 · State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor of Punjab

Addressed the Governor's handling of Bills and the limits of inaction. Read it alongside later authoritative developments on Arts. 200–201.

2024 · Sita Soren v. Union of India

A seven-judge Bench held that bribery is not protected legislative speech or voting under Arts. 105(2) and 194(2).

📌 Updating discipline: This is a selected case map, not a full survey. Check the latest Supreme Court position, especially on Arts. 200–201 and the Nabam Rebia reference.

🧪 Tap-to-Reveal Exam Checks

1. Can an Assembly abolish its Council by passing the Art. 169 resolution?

No. The resolution (a majority of total membership plus two-thirds of members present and voting) only enables Parliament to legislate. Abolition needs a Parliamentary law.

2. Do Council members vote in the Presidential election?

No. The electoral college includes elected members of State Assemblies, not members of Councils or nominated members.

3. Does Art. 172 fix the Council member's six-year term?

No. Art. 172 fixes the Council's continuing character and the retirement of one-third every second year. The normal six-year term comes from the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

4. Must the Governor address the House at every session?

No. The Art. 176 address is required at the first session after a general election and at the first session of each year.

5. A 40-member Council meets with eight members. Is there a quorum?

No. The quorum is the greater of 10 and one-tenth of 40, which is 10. The presiding officer must adjourn or suspend the meeting under Art. 189(4).

6. Does the Governor decide anti-defection cases on the ECI's opinion?

No. That route covers Art. 191(1) disqualifications through Art. 192. Tenth Schedule cases go to the Speaker or Chairman, subject to judicial review.

7. Can the Council force a joint sitting after rejecting an ordinary Bill?

No. The State has no equivalent of Art. 108. Art. 197 gives the Assembly its repeat-passage route.

8. Does a pending Bill lapse when the House is prorogued?

No. Art. 196(3) preserves it. Dissolution has different effects, set out in Art. 196(4)–(5).

9. Can the Governor return a Money Bill for reconsideration?

No. The return proviso in Art. 200 excludes Money Bills.

10. Does "charged expenditure" mean it cannot be discussed?

No. It is not voted, but it may be discussed, and it is still included in the Appropriation Bill.

11. Can an expenditure Bill under Art. 207(3) originate in the Council?

Yes, if it does not also fall within Art. 207(1). The Governor's recommendation for consideration is needed before passage in each House.

12. Does Art. 212 exclude every court challenge to legislative proceedings?

No. It bars challenges based on procedural irregularity. Substantive constitutional violations raise a different question.

📝 A Strong Descriptive-Answer Structure

For "Assess the role of Legislative Councils": start with Arts. 168–171, then explain their revising and deliberative value. Contrast ordinary-Bill delay (Art. 197) with the Money Bill recommendation role (Art. 198), and note that Ministers are not responsible to the Council. Then weigh expertise and representation against cost and the risk of patronage, and end with a balanced conclusion.
🏆 Final Scene · The Whole Architecture

🏛️ The Legislature in One Connected Story

The people directly elect the Assembly. A Council, where it exists, adds a second deliberative layer. Presiding officers keep order, members sit under constitutional qualifications and privileges, and Bills pass through the Houses to the Governor's assent. Public money follows its own chain of estimates, grants and appropriation. Throughout, the Assembly is politically predominant, but no institution is constitutionally unlimited.

Representation gives the Legislature its democratic claim; deliberation gives its decisions depth; financial control gives accountability practical force; and the Constitution gives every power its boundary.
